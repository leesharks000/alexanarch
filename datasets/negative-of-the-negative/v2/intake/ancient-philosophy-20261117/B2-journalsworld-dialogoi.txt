 Version History About Ancient Philosophy Today: Dialogoi
 Ancient Philosophy Today: DIALOGOI — Journal Profile
 Ancient Philosophy Today: DIALOGOI is a peer-reviewed journal of ancient philosophy published by Edinburgh University Press, whose first issue appeared in April 2019. It was founded to do one specific thing that its founders judged the existing journal landscape was doing badly: to provide a forum for mutual engagement between ancient and contemporary philosophy. In the journal’s own mission wording, it fruitfully connects interpretive work in ancient philosophy to current discussions in metaphysics, epistemology and ethics, and it assesses the continuing relevance of ancient theories to current philosophical interests and debates. It publishes original articles as well as book reviews, it appears twice a year, and its name carries its programme: dialogoi — dialogues — because the journal wants ancient and modern philosophers in conversation, not in separate rooms.
 This profile is anchored to the identifier pair carried by this JournalsWorld record and confirmed by the publisher’s own journal pages: print ISSN 2516-1156 and electronic ISSN 2516-1164 . Its articles carry DOIs under the publisher’s prefix in the form 10.3366/anph, the journal code anph being Edinburgh University Press’s short code for the title. Any reference carrying those ISSNs, or that DOI pattern, belongs to this journal; the many other ancient-philosophy periodicals — some with confusingly similar names — carry different identifiers and are treated in their own profiles in this directory.

 Why the Journal Was Founded: Work That Fell Between Two Rooms
 The founding story is unusually well documented, because one of the founders has told it in print. Anna Marmodoro — a metaphysician working across ancient, late-antique and medieval philosophy and contemporary analytic metaphysics — explained in an interview that work at the intersection of two areas and two methodologies finds publication difficult in general: contemporary philosophy journals look for certain things, history-of-philosophy journals look for others, and excellent work that speaks to both can fall between the cracks, with only occasional exceptions in established contemporary venues. That experience motivated the new journal. Marmodoro joined forces with Erasmus Mayr, whose specialism is ethics; as she puts it, she covers metaphysics and he covers ethics, two core areas of philosophy between them, and the editorial board was built to cover many further areas and to be geographically diverse, so that different institutional traditions of doing philosophy are represented. The journal was prepared through 2018 — the founders date their editorial work from that year — joined Edinburgh University Press’s list as one of its new journals for 2019, and published its first issue in April 2019. The Press’s own annual reporting presented it in exactly the mission terms quoted above, and the University of Oxford’s Humanities Division announced the launch to the ancient-philosophy community in the same words.
 The founders’ own positions locate the journal precisely. Marmodoro has held the Chair of Metaphysics at Durham University and a fellowship at Corpus Christi College, Oxford, and is now at Saint Louis University, with honorary positions at Durham; her curriculum vitae lists her, from 2019, as co-founder and co-editor of “Dialogoi. Ancient Philosophy Today” at Edinburgh University Press. That a working metaphysician and a working ethicist, rather than two historians of philosophy, founded an ancient-philosophy journal is the editorial point: the journal’s centre of gravity is philosophical argument conducted with ancient texts, not antiquarian commentary about them.
 Scope: Ancient Texts, Current Questions
 The mission divides into three commitments, each of which an author should be able to see in a manuscript before submitting it. First, interpretive work in ancient philosophy: careful, scholarly reading of ancient texts and thinkers — Plato and Aristotle at the centre, but the journal’s run ranges across the classical, Hellenistic, Roman and late-antique worlds, and a recent issue’s theme, Hebrew commentaries on Aristotle’s logical and theoretical works, shows medieval reception comfortably inside the tent where the argument warrants it. Second, connection to current discussion: the ancient material must be brought into relation with live debates in metaphysics, epistemology or ethics — the three fields the mission names — so that a paper should be able to say which contemporary conversation it joins and what the ancient theory contributes to it. Third, assessment of continuing relevance: the journal is interested in whether ancient theories are still candidates for truth, still usable, still capable of correcting a modern assumption — not only in what they meant in context, though the interpretation must be sound enough to bear the philosophical weight placed on it.
 The journal’s published contents show the model working. A single recent issue (Volume 8, Issue 1, April 2026) is built as an exchange: a target treatment of Gersonides on the acquisition of first principles in Aristotle’s Posterior Analytics , followed by two commissioned responses arguing with it; a study of Abraham Bibago on dialectic and doubt in Aristotle’s Metaphysics B, followed by a response on dialectic as propaedeutic. Earlier volumes include work on philosophical rhetoric in ancient Greece and Rome that moves deliberately between Plato’s and Aristotle’s official suspicion of rhetoric and the reformed, philosophical rhetoric both of them also proposed. The pattern — ancient texts read closely, contemporary stakes made explicit, disagreement staged in the open through response pieces — is the dialogue the title promises, practised at the level of the individual issue. Book reviews form the journal’s second content stream, reviewing current scholarly books for the same dual audience.
 Reading an Issue: What the Dialogue Form Looks Like
 A reader opening a recent issue meets the format at once. The April 2026 number opens with an editorial framing its theme — Hebrew commentaries on Aristotle’s logical and theoretical works — and then stages the conversation the theme deserves: Charles H. Manekin’s study of Gersonides on Posterior Analytics 2.19 and the acquisition of first principles is answered, in the same issue, by David Bronstein on innatism, scepticism and empiricism in the Gersonides reading, and by Miira Tuominen on how Gersonides transforms Aristotle’s view of coming to know principles; Yehuda Halper’s study of Abraham Bibago on dialectic and doubt in Metaphysics B is answered by Gabriela Rossi on dialectic as propaedeutic. Note what this structure achieves. The medieval material is treated with full historical seriousness — these are commentaries on Aristotle, read as commentaries — and at the same time every piece is in argument with living positions on innatism, scepticism, empiricism, demonstration and the point of dialectic. That is the mission, cashed out in a table of contents, and it is a demanding format for contributors: a DIALOGOI author should expect expert readers from both sides of the ancient-contemporary line, and should write so that both can follow, and profit from, the argument.
 The journal’s themed and exchange-based issues also give it a second use, as a teaching and seminar text. A target article with two printed responses is a ready-made graduate-seminar unit in a way a lone article is not, and librarians building ancient-philosophy collections for departments that teach ancient and contemporary philosophy side by side — a common arrangement — will find the title unusually easy to justify. Edinburgh University Press markets the journal to libraries directly from its journal pages, and the two-issues-a-year rhythm keeps the subscription within the reach of the departmental budgets that are its natural home.
 Peer Review: Triply Anonymised, and Proud of It
 The publisher’s submission pages describe a review process that goes one step beyond the double-anonymised norm, and authors should understand it before they submit. All general submissions are assessed using triply anonymised peer review: the editors and associate editors do not know the identity of the author until after a decision has been made; the reviewers never know the identity of the author; and the author never knows the identity of the reviewers. Submissions to special issues are assessed using doubly anonymised review instead, because some special-issue contributions are invited by guest editors — the editors and guest editors may therefore know authorship, while reviewers and authors remain anonymous to each other. In practice this means a general submission must be prepared for genuinely blind handling from the first page: no identifying title page conventions that break anonymity, no self-identifying acknowledgements, and a manuscript file that can circulate without its author’s name attached. Submissions are formatted in accordance with the journal’s manuscript-structure notes and its full style guide, and are sent as PDF files to the journal’s submission address given on the publisher’s submit page. The same page directs book-review business separately: review copies go to the journal’s reviews editor at the address the Press lists (in New Haven, Connecticut, in the listing consulted), and enquiries about book reviews go to the reviews contact the Press names. A journal that anonymises its editors as well as its reviewers is making a claim about where quality is judged — in the argument, not the affiliation — and authors from institutions of every size are the intended beneficiaries.
 Metrics and Standing of a Young Journal
 A journal founded in 2019 must be measured on a young journal’s scale, and this profile reports its figures attributed, with their dates. The stored record for this post carried an h-index of 3 and a Scopus coverage window of 2021–2025 — that window is the indexing start in the listings consulted, not the journal’s birth date, which is 2019. SCImago’s journal-value tables, working from Scopus data, listed the title with an SJR of 0.189 and a quartile of Q2 in Philosophy, with 11 documents in the 2025 listing year — figures consistent with a small, twice-yearly journal publishing a dozen or so substantial papers a year. OpenAlex, resolved in the foreground through its ISSN endpoint to source S4210174052, gives the inclusive picture: 148 works, 154 citations, an h-index of 7, an i10-index of 3 and a two-year mean citedness of about 0.51, with Edinburgh University Press as host organisation and the United Kingdom as country, and with topic assignments — classical philosophy and thought; epistemology, ethics and metaphysics; medieval philosophy and theology — that match the mission exactly. That two-year mean citedness, notably higher than the journal’s size might suggest, fits a journal whose papers are being taken up by contemporary philosophers as well as by historians: citations arrive faster when a paper joins a live debate. A foreground search of the Directory of Open Access Journals at checking time returned no listing for either ISSN, which is consistent with the journal’s subscription-based publication with Edinburgh University Press; no article processing charge is stated in the sources consulted, and any question about charges, licences or self-archiving terms should be put to the Press, whose publication-ethics and AI policies for authors are posted on the journal’s own pages.
 Standing, for this title, is better read from its company than from its counts. Edinburgh University Press is one of the United Kingdom’s principal humanities publishers, and the journal sits on its philosophy list beside long-established titles; its founding editors hold chairs at major universities on two continents; and its issues are built from invited exchanges with named senior figures — the April 2026 issue’s contributors and respondents are established scholars of Aristotle and of medieval philosophy. A young journal that senior scholars agree to argue in, in public, has cleared the credibility bar that metrics take a decade to register.
 How to Submit: A Practical Route
 First, test the manuscript against the mission, honestly: does it interpret ancient philosophy and connect that interpretation to a current discussion in metaphysics, epistemology or ethics and take a position on the ancient theory’s continuing relevance? A paper doing only the first of those is a paper for a history-of-philosophy journal; a paper doing only the second is a paper for a contemporary journal; DIALOGOI exists for the conjunction. Second, prepare the file for triply anonymised review — anonymous throughout, formatted to the journal’s manuscript-structure notes and style guide, and sent as a PDF to the submission address on the publisher’s page for the journal. Third, if the piece belongs to a special issue, expect doubly anonymised handling instead, and follow any guest editor’s instructions that accompany the call. Fourth, for book reviews, work through the reviews route the Press publishes rather than the article route: review copies and review enquiries have their own named destinations. Timelines and decisions rest with the editors; the anonymised process is designed so that the answer an author receives is an answer about the paper.
 Similar Journals
 The ancient-philosophy journal landscape is crowded, and DIALOGOI’s neighbours clarify its niche. The long-established specialist titles — journals devoted to ancient philosophy as a historical discipline, to Plato studies, to Aristotle studies, and the classical-quarterly tradition more broadly — serve interpretation first. The big contemporary journals of metaphysics, epistemology and ethics occasionally publish ancient material when it speaks directly to their debates, as the founders themselves acknowledged. DIALOGOI was built for the space between those two shelves: ancient in its texts, contemporary in its questions, dialogic in its form. Readers of this directory can compare it with the other ancient-philosophy and classics titles profiled here, using the method this profile has used throughout — title plus ISSN pair plus publisher — since at least three unrelated journals in this field trade under names containing “ancient philosophy” or “dialogue” in some form.
