On the Sublime (Ancient Greek: Περὶ Ὕψους, Latin: De sublimitate) is a foundational 1st-century CE treatise on literary criticism and aesthetics written by an unknown Greek author known conventionally as Longinus or Pseudo-Longinus. Written as an instructional letter to a Roman friend named Postumius Terentianus, the text examines what makes literature truly great and how language can elevate the human spirit.

Core Concept: What is the Sublime?
Transport Over Persuasion. Unlike standard rhetoric that slowly convinces a reader's logic, Longinus defines the sublime as a "loftiness and excellence of language" that strikes like a lightning bolt. It instantly overwhelms, uplifts, and transports the audience out of themselves.
A Spark of the Soul. Longinus argues that true sublimity is an echo of a writer's own greatness of soul. It acts as an emotional and spiritual spark that leaps directly from the mind of the author into the mind of the reader.

Grandeur of Thought. The power to form great, noble, and inspiring ideas, which Longinus considers the most important innate gift.
Strong and Inspired Emotion. Vehement, genuine passion that naturally elevates the tone of the work.
Effective Figures of Speech. The skillful use of rhetorical and poetic devices, such as metaphors, hyperbole, and rhetorical questions, without making them feel artificial.

Literary Faults & Cultural Context
Pitfalls of Overreaching. When writers try too hard to sound grand without genuine emotion, they fall into specific faults. These include turgidity (empty bombast), puerility (childish, overly academic cleverness), and frigidity (misplaced or exaggerated emotion).

Notable Examples. To illustrate his points, Longinus analyzes over 50 ancient authors, including Homer, Plato, Sappho, and Demosthenes. He notably quotes the opening of the Book of Genesis ("Let there be light") as a prime example of sublime simplicity.

Historical Impact. Though largely overlooked in antiquity, the treatise was rediscovered in the Renaissance and translated into French by Nicolas Boileau in 1674. It went on to heavily influence Romanticism and later philosophers like Edmund Burke and Immanuel Kant.
