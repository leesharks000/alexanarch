About us

Data Workers’ Inquiry is a community-centered project in which data workers join us as community researchers to lead their own inquiry in their respective workplaces. Our methodology aims to deepen the tradition of community-based participatory research by empowering data workers to guide the direction of the research, such that it is oriented towards their needs and goals of building workplace power, but supported by formally trained qualitative researchers.

    A couple of days ago, I posted my report with Data Workers' Inquiry about the data privacy violations that data workers face in their daily work powering AI, and I love the engagements so far! But i keep coming back to the moment that sparked this whole inquiry. My cousin came to visit me, and during his stay, He kept on making all sorts of faces at his laptop camera. When I asked what he was doing, he showed me a Google Photos folder filled with pictures of himself smiling, frowning, looking up, looking down, turning his head sideways… He was doing AI-related data work. He was making about $30 a week from it. To him, it was straightforward: take the pictures, submit the tasks, get paid. But I kept thinking about those photos. Where were they going? What were they being used for? How long would they exist? And did he really know what he was giving up? That question stuck with me. Eventually, it led me to talk to my comrades doing data work in Kenya and to look into consent and biometric data in the AI supply chain and what I found made me realize the issue was much bigger than just my cousin. Sometimes, the people building AI aren’t just providing their labor. They’re also giving up parts of themselves. Faces. Voices. Images. Behaviour. Sometimes even parts of their private lives. And the person doing the work might not really know what happens to their data after they hit “send.” I’ve finally put the inquiry together: https://lnkd.in/dP45s94E I’ll be sharing more from it over the coming days. But for now, I’m curious: When you use an AI system, how often do you think about the people at the back end(building it) and the personal data behind it?

    “Just Send us Pictures of Your feet”: Consent and Biometric Extraction in the AI Supply Chain, by James Oyange https://data-workers.org

    I haven’t posted much on LinkedIn this year (my bad hehe) because I’ve been tied up with projects, building new things, and thinking about where AI labour stands today. One project I’ve been focused on is my research with the Data Workers' Inquiry, which is now out. The research began with a tough question: why are data workers asked to share their faces, voices, and even photos of their children as part of their jobs? After speaking with nine workers in Kenya, a troubling pattern emerged. Workers were not only giving their labour to build AI systems, but they were also giving away deeply personal data, often with no clear purpose given, no informed consent obtained, and no idea where any of that data ends up. One worker created a personal library of his own face on Google Photos to finish tasks more quickly, earning just $0.25 per task. Another had to search for what a 'baby cot' was, since so many tasks asked for photos of babies sleeping in them. In all my years working behind the scenes in moderation/annotation, I never realized that a worker’s own body could become the raw material for the AI systems we use today. With this inquiry, I want to share some of these stories and raise tough questions about data protection, informed consent, and the exploitation of AI’s hidden workforce. A big shoutout to everyone who trusted me with their stories, and to the whole team at Data Workers' Inquiry and The Distributed AI Research Institute (DAIR). Thank you for giving this work a real platform. I had carried this story for a long time, hoping it would be heard. You made that happen! The inquiry is now out. I invite you to read it, think about it, and most importantly, listen to the workers behind the technology, and share/repost! This research is also part of a much bigger journey I’ve been on, one that has led me to build something of my own.     What happens to consent in a workplace where refusing a request means losing the work? That is the question that guides the new report by James Mojez Oyange, a data annotator and former content moderator in Nairobi, and one of the workers whose face, voice and documents were collected by the companies that employed him. The report begins with his own experience, but it goes far beyond an individual case. Drawing on in-depth interviews with nine data workers in Kenya who have worked for Sama, Appen, Remotask, TELUS and CloudFactory, Mojez documents what firms in the AI supply chain collect from the people who annotate their data: facial footage, identity documents, keystrokes, screen recordings and images made inside their own homes. The workers appear under pseudonyms to protect them and their future employment. Far from proposing a debate between technology and privacy, the research examines how consent is requested in conditions where refusal carries a cost, how the requirements of Kenya's Data Protection Act and the GDPR apply to work that is subcontracted across borders, and what remains available to workers once their data has left their hands. The report opens a conversation about ownership and about a question that runs through the whole of it: what happens when the people who train AI systems become part of the material those systems are trained on? Read the full report at: https://lnkd.in/eRX-J5BG

    🚨 NEW INQUIRY OUT TODAY Did you know Apple relies on data work for its AI technologies and voice assistants? Luciana, an anonymous data worker from Spain, denounces how Apple and its business process outsourcing providers employ market and competition dynamics to demand more from workers and create acceptance for deteriorating working conditions. Data workers in Spain face systematic wage discrimination and violations of the principle of equal treatment depending on the language region. Same work, in the same city, for the same employer but different wages: global inequality manifested in pay grades. She also documents and discusses the paths workers find to resist exploitation and demand better, through lawsuits, within workers’ councils and trade unions, or through whistleblowing. Highlighting the importance of addressing legal loopholes, strictly and consistently implementing the EU Supply Chain Act, broadening protections for whistleblowers, and expanding protections and rights of works councils and independent trade unions. Luciana points out that the structural problems of data work not only have significant negative psychosocial consequences for workers, but also for healthcare systems, families, social security and society as a whole. Raising the need for genuine, internationally-oriented solidarity, and reminding us that big tech companies do not hold all the cards. Available at https://lnkd.in/drxajnpm
