Abstract

As AI adoption accelerates, understanding how risks emerge and evolve is
critical. Media reports of AI incidents and hazards provide a real-time
window into the types, frequency, and impact of AI-related risks and harms
– and play a powerful role in shaping public discourse and policy agendas.
This paper analyses live data from the OECD.AI Incidents and Hazards
Monitor (AIM), classifying media reports into 14 themes, including synthetic
media, child safety, cyberattacks, privacy, and health. It identifies three
distinct patterns of media coverage – increasing, intermittent, and declining
– and traces how media attention shifts across themes over time.

Résumé

À mesure que l’adoption et l’utilisation des systèmes d'IA s’accélère, il est
essentiel de comprendre comment les risques associés apparaissent et
évoluent. Les reportages médiatiques sur les incidents et dangers liés à l'IA
offrent un aperçu en temps réels sur la nature, la fréquence et l’impact des
risques liés à l’IA, et jouent un rôle important dans la formation du débat
public et des programmes politiques. Ce rapport analyse les données en
temps réels du OECD.AI Incidents and Hazards Monitor (AIM), en classant
les reportages médiatiques en 14 groupes thématiques, notamment les
médias synthétiques, la sécurité des enfants, les cyberattaques, la
protection de la vie privée et la santé. Il met en évidence trois types de
tendance distinctes de couverture médiatiques – croissante, intermittente et
décroissante – et retrace l’évolution de l’attention médiatique portée au fil
du temps selon les thèmes.

Executive summary
Recent AI developments and adoption have been accompanied by increased attention to associated risks,
highlighting the need to identify and respond to these challenges. These risks may correspond to potential
harms (“hazards”) or result in actual harm (“incidents”). Effective mechanisms for monitoring, reporting,
and response are essential to understand, prevent, and mitigate these risks, as well as remedy incidents.
In early 2025, the members of the Global Partnership on AI (GPAI) approved a common framework for
reporting AI incidents, designed as an international data-sharing template for public and private
organisations across jurisdictions and sectors (OECD, 2025[1]). This is especially crucial as some
jurisdictions – including the EU through its AI Act – introduce reporting for AI incidents.
Data on AI incidents and hazards is critical for AI developers, deployers, and users to support their risk
management efforts. They also help policymakers, researchers, and the public understand which risks are
materialising, which are being addressed, how frequently they occur, and how severe their impacts are.
At present, there are few public and comparable sources of information on AI incidents and hazards. While
major AI companies often track and sometimes share such information, much of it remains confidential
(OECD, 2025[2]). Proxy indicators – such as court rulings on AI-related cases or media reports – can offer
partial insights into where and what types of incidents are occurring.
This report analyses trends based on publicly available media articles. The findings reflect media and
public interest rather than comprehensive or systematic tracking of AI incidents and hazards. Importantly,
media reports capture only what journalists are aware of and deemed newsworthy. Other types of AI
incidents, such as those resolved directly by developers or providers before reaching public attention, are
therefore not captured in this analysis.
Media reports serve as a key channel through which AI incidents become visible to policymakers and the
public, shaping awareness, public discourse, and policy agendas. Conversely, reliance on media sources
may introduce errors of inclusion, as some reported incidents may be mischaracterised, exaggerated, or
inaccurately attributed to AI due to limited background or technical understanding.
While the total number of AI incidents and hazards reported in public media surged between 2022 and
2025, their share relative to overall AI-related media articles slightly declined. The OECD.AI Incidents and
Hazards Monitor (AIM) tracks these media-reported AI incidents and hazards in real-time and leverages
the OECD common reporting framework to categorise them consistently.
Using machine learning techniques and keyword analysis, this report classifies media-reported AI incidents
and hazards into the following 14 themes: risks linked to synthetic media; AI-enabled warfare; cyberattacks
and fraud; risks related to online platforms, such as inappropriate recommendations; privacy violations;
risks linked specifically to biometric data; risks linked to autonomous vehicles; issues specific to large
language models (LLMs) such as hallucinations; election interference and other geopolitical issues; labour
market disruptions; child safety; health issues; risks of intellectual property infringement; and other longterm risks. While some themes may be closely related, each highlights a distinct and important category
of AI risks and harms. Each media-reported incident or hazard was assigned to the single most relevant
theme based on the available information, enabling analytical clarity and preventing double counting.
Analysis across the 14 themes reveals three distinct trends in the share of media coverage:

Themes attracting an increasing share of media coverage include AI incidents and hazards linked

1 Introduction
Artificial intelligence (AI) technologies have seen rapid adoption over recent years. Alongside this adoption,
AI risks have started to materialise.
In response, various stakeholders, jurisdictions have emphasised the importance of reporting, monitoring
and responding to AI incidents. In this context, the informal OECD.AI expert group on AI incidents, set up
in January 2023, has informed the development of a common understanding of AI incidents and hazards.
The expert group worked on two workstreams. The first workstream focused on defining AI incidents and
related terminology and building a common framework to report them. The second workstream sought
both to test the framework with actual examples and begin to build an evidence base to help understand
trends in actual and potential AI risks.
The OECD definitions of an AI incident, AI hazard, and related terminology provide a shared understanding
of these AI-related risks (OECD, 2024[3]). A common set of definitions supports consistent identification
and measurement of AI incidents and hazards based on their severity, across borders and sectors
(Box 1.1).

Box 1.1. Definitions of AI incidents and hazards
An AI incident is an event, circumstance or series of events where the development, use or malfunction
of one or more AI systems directly or indirectly leads to any of the following harms:
(a) injury or harm to the health of a person or groups of people;
(b) disruption of the management and operation of critical infrastructure;
(c) violations of human rights or a breach of obligations under the applicable law intended to protect
fundamental, labour and intellectual property rights;
(d) harm to property, communities or the environment.
An AI hazard is an event, circumstance or series of events where the development, use or malfunction
of one or more AI systems could plausibly lead to an AI incident, i.e., any of the following harms:
(a) injury or harm to the health of a person or groups of people;
(b) disruption of the management and operation of critical infrastructure;
(c) violations to human rights or a breach of obligations under applicable law intended to protect
fundamental labour and intellectual property rights;
(d) harm to property, communities or the environment.
Source: OECD (2024[3]).

The OECD common reporting framework for AI incidents, published in February 2025, supports a
consistent approach to tracking AI incidents (OECD, 2025[1]). A shared framework helps stakeholders –
including policymakers, developers, deployers and users – report incidents in a standardised manner to
help understand the evolution and impacts of AI risks and harms. The framework enables the collection of
information related to the socio-economic context of the AI incident, the associated AI model, and additional
details such as the model’s inputs, outputs, and relevant OECD AI Principles (OECD.AI, 2025[4]).

Building on this, the OECD.AI Incidents and Hazards Monitor (AIM) tracks real-time AI incidents and
hazards as reported in the media (OECD.AI, 2025[5]). Currently the data rely on reports from the top 50%
of media outlets, based on the Alexa traffic rankings (OECD.AI, 2025[6]). These reports are aggregated by
Event Registry, a news intelligence platform which monitors over 150 000 news articles daily. Over time,
AIM data will help identify patterns in media coverage and can provide insights into the nature and impact
of risks and harms associated with AI technologies.
While AIM currently relies exclusively on media reporting, these sources provide a valuable and informative
starting point. Media reports serve as a key channel through which AI incidents become visible to
policymakers and the public, shaping awareness, public discourse, and policy agendas. As such, they offer
important insights into the types of AI-related issues that attract attention, prompt concern, and drive
regulatory and institutional responses. Future developments, such as incorporating case law data,
academic literature, and open submissions, could expand the AIM’s coverage and improve understanding
of AI risks across the globe. Reports emerging from the G7 Hiroshima AI Process could further complement
this work by providing concrete examples of how AI incidents are identified, assessed, and managed under
different risk management frameworks (OECD.AI, 2025[7]).
Between 2022 and 2025, the number of AI incidents and hazards reported by the media rose steadily, from
92 to 324 per month on average (Figure 1.1, Panel A). However, their share relative to total AI news
declined, from 3.2% in 2022 to around 2.5% in 2025 (Figure 1.1, Panel B).

2 Identifying key themes in media-

reported AI incidents and hazards

A thematic analysis of media-reported AI incidents and hazards between January 2022 to September 2025
resulted in the clustering of the data into 14 categories (Table 2.1). These categories are developed from
machine learning analysis and keyword analysis. While certain thematic clusters inevitably intersect – for
instance, some incidents related to child safety may involve the use of deepfakes, which could also be
captured under synthetic media – the 14 themes capture distinct and policy-relevant areas of AI-related
risks and harms and warrant separate attention despite potential overlaps.
To maintain analytical clarity and avoid double counting, each media-reported incident or hazard is
assigned to the single most relevant theme based on the available information. This approach ensures
conceptual coherence and enables comparability across themes. For example, incidents involving
synthetic media used to generate child sexual abuse material are relevant to both synthetic media and
child safety, but are assigned only to the most relevant cluster. Further methodological details are available
in Annexes A and B. As new types of media-reported AI incidents and hazards emerge, these clusters
may evolve or expand, reflecting ongoing developments in AI.

•

Trends in the share of media coverage of AI-related risks indicate how media attention has shifted over
time. These trends do not measure how severe incidents are, the types of harm involved, or the number
of affected stakeholders, and should not be read as indicators of these factors. Instead, media coverage
should be interpreted in relative terms, by comparing how attention is distributed across the 14 thematic
areas. As a reference point, if media attention were evenly distributed, each theme would represent roughly
7% of the total.

3 Increasing coverage
The total number of media-reported AI incidents has increased across most themes over time. However,
only four of the fourteen key themes identified in Section 2 – synthetic media, child safety, cyber-attacks
and fraud, and labour market – have seen a rising share of media attention relative to the total (Figure 3.1).
While the share of coverage for these four themes has grown, the rate of growth and the overall volume of
reports vary across them.

Synthetic media
The share of media-reported AI incidents and hazards related to synthetic media grew 2.5 times between
2022 and 2025, representing over 14% the total in the third quarter of 2025. These reports include video,
image, and voice generation that may result in false or misleading information. They are sometimes
referred to as “deepfakes”. Notable peaks happened in May and November 2023 and June 2024
(Figure 3.2).
Notably, the share of this type of reported AI incidents and hazards peaked in November 2023, when it
made up 22% of all AIM reports. One notable incident driving this peak is the AI-generated deepfake videos
and images of Indian celebrities. This incident was reported by 853 news outlets. Synthetic media risks
peaked again in summer 2024, fuelled by deepfake generation in the context of the U.S. presidential
election1.

Child safety
The share of media-reported AI incidents and hazards related to child safety doubled between 2022 and
2025 following a peak in late 2023. In April 2024, 9% of all media-reported AI incidents and hazards were
related to the topic of child safety (Figure 3.3). Between January 2022 and August 2023, these types of
reports represented 2.6% of all reports in AIM. This share increased to 7.7% in the last quarter of 2023
and reached 5.5%, on average, in 2024 until September 2025.
These reports underscore the increase of AI-generated child sexual abuse material (CSAM), at times
created by teenagers themselves. These types of issues were reported by the media in countries including
Australia, Brazil, Greece, Peru, Singapore and Spain2. Child safety also includes recommendation
algorithms and targeted ads showing inappropriate content to children and the use of images of real
children without consent3.

Cyberattacks and fraud
Media-reported AI incidents and hazards linked to cyberattacks and fraud increased by a factor of 2.7 as
a share of total reports captured by AIM over the past 3.5 years (Figure 3.4). These reports are linked to
the use of AI systems for financial fraud and scams, and phishing attacks4. There were notable peaks in
April 2022, December 2023 and March 2025. On average, the share of media-reported AI incidents and

6 Conclusion and next steps
The OECD.AI Incidents and Hazards Monitor (AIM) offers a unique, real-time view window into how media
attention to different AI-related risks is evolving. By tracking media reports of AI incidents and hazards
across multiple domains, AIM reveals where attention to AI risks and harms is rising, uneven, or declining.
Analysis of this data can help surface trends and insights into nature and impact of issues associated with
AI technologies. The classification of media reports into 14 thematic clusters offers a structured framework
for this analysis.
While media attention varies by theme and over time, AIM underscores the need for continuous monitoring
to detect and understand emerging incidents and hazards as they occur. Peaks in reporting often coincide
with high-profile events or major technological developments, while declining attention may conceal
persistent or underreported risks.
As AI adoption expands globally, AIM serves as a key resource for tracking trends in media coverage of
AI risks and harms and their potential impacts on society. Although AIM currently relies on media-reported
data, it plays a crucial role in building an evidence base to better understand, prevent, and respond to AIrelated risks and harms.
Next steps could include allowing open submissions to complement media-based monitoring and
reorganising incidents and hazards visually by theme to support deeper analysis. Integrating additional
datasets – such as case law, global recall portals, or academic research – could provide a more complete
view of AI incidents and hazards, helping policymakers identify systemic risks, cascading effects, and
potential prevention and response mechanisms.

Annex A. Methodological note

The OECD.AI Incidents and Hazards Monitor (AIM) detects AI-related risks and harms from reputable
news outlets in real-time and many languages. A machine learning exercise was conducted to identify key
trends in AIM data from January 2022 to September 2025.

Identifying key thematic clusters
LLM-generated summaries of AI incidents and hazards were collected and transformed into a semantic
representation capturing the underlying concepts. Using a k-means clustering algorithm, these reports
were classified into 25 thematic clusters each capturing semantically similar risk characteristics. For each
cluster, the most relevant keywords were identified to better understand underlying themes and reduce
apparent overlaps. This resulted in 14 distinct themes.
Other topic-modelling algorithms – including the Latent Dirichlet allocation (LDA), latent semantic analysis
(LSA) with singular value decomposition (SVD) – were tested (Cao et al., 2009[16]; Foltz, 1996[17]). These
algorithms resulted in thematic clusters driven by geographical themes (locations and notable
stakeholders) or to specific companies and platforms. Since the objective of the analysis is to identify global
trends in AI incidents and hazards across thematic risk categories, these algorithms were not retained.
To ensure analytical clarity and prevent double counting, each event was assigned to a single thematic
cluster based on its dominant topic. See Annex B for more information.

Sentence embedding
To identify the themes of AI incidents and hazards, event summaries were analysed using natural language
processing. The authors applied Hugging Face’s sentence-transformer model, all-MiniLM-L6-v2, which
converts each summary into a numerical vector capturing contextual and semantic relationships in the text
(Hugging Face, 2025[18]). These vectors were then used for clustering.

Clustering AI incidents and hazards
After embedding, the k-means clustering algorithm – an unsupervised machine learning algorithm – was
used to group similar reports without prior labels.
The authors tested different numbers of clusters (10, 15, 20, 25 and 30) using the elbow method and
silhouette scores (Januzaj, Beqiri and Luma, 2023[19]; Shi et al., 2021[20]). Since these methods gave
inconsistent results, the authors chose 25 clusters based on a qualitative keywords review. For each
cluster, the term frequency-inverse document frequency (TF-IDF) method identified the most relevant
keywords (Qaiser and Ali, 2018[21]). English stop-words (e.g., “the”, “a”, “is”) were removed to improve the
performance of the model. These keywords helped interpret and label themes.

Manual validation for cluster coherence
An internal review of the 25 clusters – using keyword analysis, event counts, and random manual checks
of selected media reports – led to their consolidation into 14 distinct themes.

Correlation testing shows a strong positive relationship (0.90) between the monthly counts of mediareported AI incidents and hazards. This suggests that underlying events and media attention follow similar
patterns over time for both, as illustrated in Figure A.2. This finding does not support the view that isolated
incidents drive short-term spikes in media reporting of hazards.

Annex B. Similarity between themes

To ensure analytical clarity and avoid double counting, each media-reported AI incident or hazard is
assigned to a single theme based on the most relevant available information. While this approach supports
coherence and comparability across themes, some reports may be relevant to more than one theme.
To capture these overlaps, an additional analysis examined the relatedness between themes. For each
theme, a centroid embedding was calculated from the embeddings of its associated media reports and
compared using cosine similarity (Morissette and Chartier, 2013[23]). The resulting similarity matrix shows
how closely related the themes are to one another, with higher values indicating greater semantic similarity
(Figure B.1).
