Theophrastus (c. 371 – c. 287 BCE) was an ancient Greek philosopher, naturalist, and the successor to Aristotle as the head of the Peripatetic school (the Lyceum) in Athens. Originally named Tyrtamus, he was given the nickname "Theophrastus"—meaning "he of godlike speech"—by Aristotle due to his remarkable oratorical skills.
While he wrote over 200 treatises covering physics, logic, metaphysics, and ethics, he is most famous today as the "Father of Botany" for establishing the foundational framework of plant science.

Major Surviving Works

Though less than 10% of his massive library of work survives, his extant texts remain deeply influential across multiple fields:
• Enquiry into Plants (Historia Plantarum): A nine-volume biological masterpiece where he classified and described over 500 plant varieties, detailing their geographical distribution, structures, and medicinal properties.
• On the Causes of Plants (De Causis Plantarum): A six-volume counterpart focusing on plant physiology. It explores plant reproduction, cultivation methods, and the direct impacts of climate on crop yields.
• The Characters (Charaktēres): A distinct detour into social and moral psychology. It features 30 brief, humorous, and biting sketches of undesirable human personality traits (such as "The Flatterer," "The Arrogant," and "The Loquacious Man").
• Minor Scientific Treatises: Surviving shorter works include On Fire, On Stones, On Odours, and Weather Signs, the latter earning him a reputation among historians as one of the world's earliest documented weather forecasters.

Core Philosophical & Scientific Contributions

• Empirical Botany: Rather than relying purely on philosophical deduction, Theophrastus observed the natural world directly. He categorized plants systematically by their growth forms (trees, shrubs, undershrubs, and herbs).
• Expanding Logic: Alongside his colleague Eudemus of Rhodes, he advanced the Aristotelian logic system by developing complex hypothetical and modal syllogisms.
• Refining Metaphysics: He challenged aspects of Aristotle’s rigid teleology (the idea that everything in nature exists for a distinct final purpose). Instead, he argued that some natural phenomena occur simply by necessity or coincidence.
