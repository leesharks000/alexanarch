The reason for this collection
AI hallucination cases: what are courts saying?
My interest in AI hallucination cases is in the language and reasoning of the judgments: how courts describe unreliable material, distinguish suspicion from an established finding, respond to explanations and discuss the proper use of AI.
This public page is deliberately focused on selected passages. Reading them together can reveal shared concerns, different approaches and questions worth exploring as the international discussion develops. The collection is selective and does not measure how often these issues occur or establish a worldwide consensus.
Case names identify the source and allow the passage to be placed in context. My purpose is to study the courts’ comments and contribute to an understanding of AI and legal practice, rather than to name or shame individuals.

What inclusion means
A decision may appear because it contains a relevant observation, warning, allegation, explanation or finding. AI use may be confirmed, suspected, disputed or not established at all. Some passages concern fabricated or unreliable material without establishing that AI produced it.
Inclusion does not itself establish that anyone used AI, fabricated material, misled a court or acted improperly.

Questions running through the judgments
Reliability and verification
What courts say about checking citations, quotations, evidence and legal propositions.

Context and response
How judgments record explanations, uncertainty, responsibility and the court’s response.

The developing role of AI
Comments about legitimate assistance, professional judgment and the limits of automation.

Keeping the words in context
Fabricated/false citations: reading the comments in context
Does an entry prove an AI hallucination?The distinction matters. A judgment may report an accusation, reproduce a submission, express a concern or make a finding. Those are different things. An extract must not be treated as establishing more than the decision actually says.
What does “fabricated/false citations” cover here?It is a research label for the subject discussed in the source, which can include fake legal citations, invented authorities, inaccurate quotations, misleading references or concerns about a legal proposition. It does not establish how the material was produced. The wording of each judgment, including any uncertainty about AI, remains central.
Why include comments where AI use is not established?The purpose is to understand the international discussion of AI hallucination cases and related citation problems. General warnings, disputed explanations and comments about appropriate AI assistance can contribute to that discussion. A relevant judicial comment can therefore justify inclusion without a finding that anyone used AI.
Why retain case references?They identify the decision so readers can understand its context. A case name is a source reference, not a label attached to a person’s conduct. The purpose of the collection is comparative reading and research.
Why are some passages shortened or redacted?The public collection concentrates on the relevant comment. Ellipses indicate omissions and square brackets may indicate editorial substitutions or redactions. These should not be read as additional words spoken by the court. The full judgment remains the source for its complete wording, reasoning and outcome.
How does this differ from the UK tracker?The UK AI Hallucination Cases Tracker offers more detailed UK records, outcomes, sources and research charts. This international page concentrates on selected judicial comments across jurisdictions, including fabricated/false citations and wider observations about AI. A UK decision may also appear here where its passage contributes to that wider discussion.
Does the collection predict what courts will do?No. It brings together comments for study. Decisions arise in different legal systems and factual settings. Similar language does not necessarily mean the same rule or outcome applies elsewhere.

Key Takeaway

This public-facing international tracker records public references to suspected or confirmed AI hallucination cases and other suspected or confirmed incidents of fabricated/false citations in court judgments, tribunal decisions and legal proceedings worldwide. Inclusion in this tracker does not mean that any named person, lawyer, party or court necessarily used AI, was suspected of using AI, acted improperly, or was found to have done so. It means only that the public source records, raises or discusses an issue concerning AI hallucinations, fabricated/false citations, or something similar.

The fuller private tracker, available only by request, contains deeper research including the false citations themselves, whether judges repeated them in judgments, the types of fabricated or false material, whether they concern case law, legislation or something else, who discovered them, whether the issue involved litigants in person/pro se or lawyers, the reasons given, and any aggravating or mitigating features. This tracker is always evolving and cases may be added or removed at any point.
