ChatGPT said:
In Aristotle's Sophistical Refutations (34), when he says that "before us there was nothing to say of syllogizing", he is making a claim about the systematic study of logic, not claiming that nobody before him ever reasoned by syllogism.

What Aristotle means
Aristotle is essentially saying:

People before him used arguments and syllogistic reasoning, but they had not yet developed a systematic account of what makes an argument a syllogism, how syllogisms are structured, and which forms are valid.

So Aristotle sees himself as the first to turn syllogistic reasoning into a formal subject of investigation.

This distinction matters. A syllogism is not merely any piece of reasoning. In Aristotle's technical sense, it is an argument in which, given certain premises, a conclusion follows necessarily because of their structure.

For example:

All humans are mortal.
Socrates is a human.
Therefore, Socrates is mortal.
Aristotle didn't invent the practice of reasoning like this. What he claims to have done is discover and articulate the general structure underlying such arguments.

So where does that put Socrates?
It puts Socrates before Aristotle as a practitioner of dialectical reasoning, but not as a systematic logician.

Socrates certainly engaged in rigorous argument. His questioning often proceeds by taking someone's claim, eliciting further commitments, and showing that those commitments lead to a contradiction or an unacceptable conclusion. Plato's dialogues preserve many examples.

But Socrates did not, as far as our evidence goes, formulate a theory of syllogistic logic comparable to Aristotle's.

So you could distinguish them this way:

Socrates / Aristotle
Uses reasoning/argument: Yes / Yes
Practices dialectic: Yes / Yes
Explicitly studies valid argument forms: Not systematically / Yes
Develops a formal theory of syllogism: No / Yes
Treats logic as a systematic discipline: No / Yes

There's an especially interesting wrinkle here: Socrates may have been one of the historical predecessors Aristotle has in mind when describing the development of dialectic, but Aristotle's claim is not that earlier philosophers were incapable of reasoning. Rather, they lacked the theoretical account of reasoning that Aristotle was now providing.

And this helps explain why Aristotle can simultaneously regard Socrates as enormously important to dialectic while claiming that "before us there was nothing to say of syllogizing."

If you're reading this specifically in relation to Socrates' elenchus in Plato, the really interesting question is whether the Socratic elenchus itself counts as an Aristotelian syllogism. The answer is sometimes, but not straightforwardly—and that distinction is important for understanding both Socrates and Aristotle.
