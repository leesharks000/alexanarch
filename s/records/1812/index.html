Current version v2.1 (27 November 2026), which superseded v1.1 and v1.0 in place the same day; what each version added is stated at the end of this description. The received account is stated first and in full: Matthew from Mark, a sayings source and material of its own; nothing passing from Revelation to Matthew; where the two agree, a common stock. The paper does not begin by disputing that. It takes Revelation as a source and works with it. Each section takes places of Revelation, states an operation, performs it, and sets the result beside Matthew's Greek: give the speaker a past; give the life a beginning, a land, a teaching, an economy and adversaries; turn the name into a story; make the end of the world a parable; open the tomb. 0. The question

0.1. The received account, in full. Matthew is a life of Jesus written in Greek late in the first century by an author who had Mark before him and reworked it. What Matthew has beyond Mark came from a sayings collection shared with Luke, or from Luke's use of Matthew, and from material of Matthew's own, written or oral, shaped by scripture. Revelation is another kind of book, a prophecy sent to seven assemblies in Asia, and where the two agree in wording they draw on a common stock: the Septuagint, the sayings of Jesus in circulation, the diction of early Christian prophecy. On this account nothing passes from Revelation to Matthew. Where anything passes between them it passes the other way, from the tradition of the sayings into the prophet's book.

0.2. This paper asks a different question and does not begin by disputing that account. Suppose a writer has Revelation, whole, and wants a Gospel. What must that writer do? Which operations, applied to which places, give which parts of Matthew? The question is answered by doing it. Each section takes a place or several places of Revelation, states an operation, performs it, and sets the result beside Matthew's Greek.

0.3. Three things are meant by "write" and they are kept apart. (a) A relation in Revelation and a relation in Matthew are the same relation with a stated change: this is shown by quotation and by counts over the whole New Testament. (b) The change is one of a small set of moves that recur: this is shown by the ledger. (c) A move, given to a reader who is shown only the place in Revelation and the rule, produces Matthew's passage: this has been done once, under seal, and is reported in section VII. Most of what follows is (a) and (b). Only section VII is (c). A fourth kind of support appears from section II on: a line written down before it was counted. Where a pairing was so written, the text says so, and section XIV gives the tally with the failures.

0.4. What the demonstration shows, and what it does not. It shows a procedure that another reader can run, alter, reverse or break, and it measures how much of Matthew the procedure reaches. It does not, taken alone, say who wrote first: every pairing below can be read from right to left, as the work of a seer who had Matthew and compressed it. The paper keeps to that limit in every section that demonstrates. It does not keep silent about the larger argument. A count of how much of one book can be written from another has no weight until it is said why the count matters, and section P says it: what the programme holds, on what grounds held elsewhere in the archive, and what it expects to show. The two registers are marked wherever they meet. Shown here means shown in this paper with its data. Held means the programme's position, argued in the records named.

0.5. The source is held open (Figure 2, at XIII.5). A writer who knows a book does not move through it in order. Any place may be drawn on at any point of the new work, several at once, and the same place twice. Tests that looked for Revelation's sequence in Matthew's sequence did not find it, by any of the tests run (section XIV; the notebook, 2BB, 2BD, 2BE). The unit is the relation: who does what to whom, in what mode.

0.6. Matthew treats Revelation like scripture. This is the paper's account of what the procedure is, and it is stated here because everything after reads differently under it. Matthew's handling of the Septuagint is known and uncontested: a phrase kept, a saying of a prophet staged as an event, a scene restaged with another bearer (Pharaoh and the children, Herod and the children), two scriptures run together in one line. The operations of sections I to XI are these operations, with Revelation in the place of the prophet. Counted on the same instrument, in the notebook (2BG), the likeness holds at three points and fails at two.

  • Order. Matthew's thirteen uses of Isaiah stand in no order of Isaiah (rank correlation −0.14), its fourteen of the Psalms in no order of the Psalter (+0.01). Revelation's figure is −0.09. No one supposes Matthew walked Isaiah from the first chapter to the last, and the test for a walked source was the wrong test.
  • Company. Of the verses where Matthew uses scripture without naming it, 55 per cent are also reached from Revelation, against 28.6 per cent of the Gospel at large. Of the verses where Matthew cites scripture with a formula, 22 per cent. Revelation is worked in where scripture is worked in tacitly, in the same verses.
  • The one walk. Revelation 12:1–6 is taken in order through Matthew 2, beside the infancy of Moses in the same chapter: narrative scripture walked for narrative. The order then named a verse before the verse was read. ὁ δράκων ἕστηκεν ἐνώπιον τῆς γυναικὸς τῆς μελλούσης τεκεῖν; ἐστάθη ἐπάνω οὗ ἦν τὸ παιδίον, of the star. A star and a standing in one verse are found in those two verses of the New Testament and no other.
  • Against: fewer of Revelation's words are kept than scripture's (a median of one shared stem to a row, against two for the allusions commonly granted). What happens in the source is often reversed; what the source judges of it is reversed twice in 158 rows, and one of the two stands inside Matthew's open reversal of the law (S.6).
  • And it is never named. The book presents itself as the speech of Jesus (1:1; the seven letters). In a telling of his life that speech is spoken by him. A formula of citation has no place to stand.

P. The programme this belongs to

P.1. Revelation First is the thesis that the Apocalypse is the generative source of the rest of the New Testament: that the epistles and then the Gospels were unfolded from it. It is distinct from the argument that Revelation was written early. The early date is old (Robinson 1976; Gentry 1989) and says nothing about what came from what. That the whole corpus unfolds from the Apocalypse has, to the author's knowledge, no antecedent as a sustained argument; Newton in 1733 held that Hebrews and the Petrine letters allude to it, and the Muratorian fragment has Paul following John (#1735 §I). The thesis can be held at three strengths: that Revelation can generate the later books by stated procedures; that the New Testament reads as one corpus with Revelation at its head; that Revelation was written first. The author holds the third, on the evidence, and grants that a reader may accept the first alone. This paper demonstrates only at the first. The rest of this section is the argument inside which the demonstration has its weight.

P.2. The literary-genetic argument (held; argued in "Revelation First: Literary Genealogy and the Missing Ancestors of the Epistles", #1735). A literature proposed as earliest should be tested as an ancestor and not only dated as an object. Its forms should be derivable from materials that exist and were available before it; and what follows it may be expected to show transformations of it. For any feature of a text the ancestry on offer is one of four kinds: an extant antecedent; a tradition attested independently; an antecedent reconstructed because the later text seems to need one; an innovation left unexplained.

Put to Revelation, the test returns extant antecedents throughout. The throne and the living creatures are Ezekiel's, the beasts and the one like a son of man Daniel's, the lampstand Zechariah's, the plagues those of Exodus, the city Isaiah's and Ezekiel's; the seven and the Logos in their midst are in Philo, word for word enough that the founders of the modern commentary printed the passage beside the lampstands (#1758; Bousset 1906; Charles 1920). The genealogy of the book can be traced, inspected and mapped down to the letter, and no Christian document has to be supposed before it to explain any part of it.

Put to the epistles and the Gospels in the received order, the test returns reconstructions. Paul's letters arrive with a Christian grammar already made: formulae marked as received (1 Cor 15:3; 11:23), a hymn whose earlier life has a literature of its own (Phil 2:6–11), and in the first letter on the received chronology a "word of the Lord" (1 Thess 4:15) that no one can produce. The field's own category, pre-Pauline, names the gap. For the Gospels the list is longer: a sayings source, a special source for Matthew and one for Luke, a passion narrative before Mark, a small apocalypse beneath Mark 13, a signs source beneath John, collections of testimonies, and behind them a tradition carried by mouth. None is extant. Each is inferred from the texts it is brought in to explain.

So the asymmetry. The one book of the New Testament whose literary ancestry is complete in surviving texts is placed last and read as derivative. The books whose ancestry has to be supplied by documents no one has seen are placed first and read as sources. Put Revelation first and the direction of explanation runs through extant texts at each step: the Septuagint and Philo into the Apocalypse (shown, in the records named), the Apocalypse into the epistles (shown in part, P.5), the Apocalypse with scripture into the Gospels (shown in part, here). In the reverse order the same steps cannot be taken without intermediaries made up for the purpose. The programme holds a further member of the first step, the Slavonic Josephus, argued in "The Word That Became Text" and not relied on here.

P.2a. The order of forms (the author's argument, with one count). The received sequence, in full: sayings and stories of a teacher passed on by mouth; Paul's letters, with little of the life in them; then Mark, Matthew and Luke with a sayings source, and John; then Revelation, a late and separate work. The same account holds that the movement was apocalyptic from its first day. The author: "an intra nt narrative of beginning with nauve, crufty biography and evolving towards the abstract and visionary simply contradicts everything we know about second temple literature." And: "the received account puts two separate unexplained genre innovations first, separately. tbe epistolary form; and then as a formally unrelated later innovation, biographical tradition with no surviving text. two. not one. both of which require their own imaginary texts to explain."

Put as a burden. The letter of an apostle to an assembly, read as scripture, is a new form. The told life of a teacher of one's own time is a new form. The vision is the period's old and working form (Daniel, the books of Enoch, and after them Ezra and Baruch; stated from general knowledge of the period's literature and not counted here). An account of these books owes an explanation of the two new forms. The received order puts the two inventions first, apart, each resting on its own shelf of texts that do not survive (P.2), and the inherited form last. The author's order derives both from a book that exists: the letter from the book that opens and closes as one and holds seven, and the life from that book and the letters together (section E).

The count. χάρις ὑμῖν καὶ εἰρήνη ἀπὸ: grace, peace and "from" in one verse stand in thirteen verses of the New Testament, the greeting of twelve letters under Paul's name and Revelation 1:4. A book whose last verse is "grace … with" you or all: thirteen books, eleven under Paul's name, Hebrews, and Revelation (22:21). So the new form's frame stands whole, opening and close, on the one book in the old form, addressed to seven assemblies. The count cannot say which way the greeting travelled. The argument ranks two orders by what each has to assume, and shows nothing about any one verse.

It can also be read as a succession of capacities, each station able to do something the one before could not.

This is a genealogy of capacities and no chronology. It is offered so that direction can be asked a second way, apart from which words are kept: which innovations presuppose which others? One worked case shows the question and its difficulty. Matthew 16:18 needs two things to exist before it can be written: apostles as the foundation of a building (Rev 21:14; Eph 2:20) and a follower named Cephas who is called a pillar (Gal 2:9). The relation in Matthew exists only in their fusion (E.4). But the received account reads the same three texts the other way, with Revelation's twelve names presupposing a told life in which twelve were chosen. Presupposition can be argued in both directions until there is a rule for it, fixed before the cases are read. That rule is owed. The reviewer adds a station for Thomas, "the figure's speech becomes an interpretive encounter"; the author has not ruled where Thomas stands, and section T places it nowhere in an order.

P.3. How Revelation was kept from consideration as a source (held; documented in "I Know You Are, But What Am I?", #1746, and in "Secondary Source", #1741). This is described as an arrangement and its effects. No motive is claimed for it and none is needed.

In a source argument a text has standing when it can be presupposed without first being proved, when material can be assigned to it, when a text of it can be established, and when it is admitted as a candidate wherever a source is sought. In eighteen works of source criticism from Lachmann (1835) to Streeter (1924), read in the original languages at the locus, the reconstructed documents hold all four powers and the Apocalypse holds none. Holtzmann sets aside in advance any inference that presupposes a relation between the Apocalypse and the Gospels, and proceeds from sources his own inquiry constructs. Streeter assumes the sayings source and dates the extant book last. At one verse the record runs the other way: in 1843 Hitzig argued from Matthew 24:30 and Revelation 1:7 that Matthew used the Apocalypse, and called it a compelling proof; over sixty years the dependence was granted, as possible or as fact, by Volkmar, Keim, Bernhard Weiss, Bousset and Merx. Each acknowledgment stands where it leaves the source theories untouched: a footnote to a date, an evangelist's insertion, a note in a commentary on the other book. A census run before Hitzig was read returned his pairing: the tribes, the mourning and the clouds stand together in those two verses and in no other window of some 2.5 million words of Greek (#1746 §IV). The pairing is a row of this paper's ledger, produced by the engine.

The reception did the rest by parts (#1741). The book was received as derivative in its author (a second John), its date (the end of Domitian), its matter (sources, myth, a letter of Paul), its figure (the Gospels' Jesus, already known) and its language (charged to the writer as bad Greek). The relations that run out of it, the Muratorian fragment's report and the crown of life it shares with James, were answered or left unused. The generative position was given to the Gospels, to Paul, and to documents reconstructed behind them.

The effect on this paper's question is exact. The reason no one has counted how much of Matthew can be written from Revelation is that the question had no place to be asked: the book was dated out of candidacy before any source was sought, and the place a source would fill was already held by a hypothesis. An extant text does not need to prove that it exists. A hypothetical one does (#1746). The burden has been carried the other way round.

P.4. Why the count matters. On the received account nothing passes from Revelation to Matthew, and what the two share is common stock. That account predicts that a procedure such as this one reaches little, and that what it reaches is scattered, weak, and explained as well by the Septuagint.

Shown here. The ledger has 158 relations reaching 305 of Matthew's 1,067 verses, 28.6 per cent; 265 verses without the rows entered for relation alone (XIV.1). By the kind of material, on the paper's proxy for the strata (XIV.1a): 100 of the 458 verses Matthew has from Mark; 70 of the 184 of the double tradition; 135 of the 425 that are Matthew's own. The two strata for which lost documents are posited, the double tradition and Matthew's own material, are the two the procedure reaches furthest into: 38 and 32 per cent. This was reached in two days (the first version of this paper, a day earlier, had 86 relations and 178 verses), by one author working with a machine, from a tagged text, with every row checked in both books and the lines that were written down before counting tallied with their failures (XIV). It was not dug for. Whole regions were opened by a single reading: Matthew 18 from the seven letters, the night of the arrest as the letters' promises staged and failed, Matthew 11 as the opening of the Apocalypse. The last was the author's reading of chapters held out from the search, registered before any count, and run once: five of ten lines held, among them σὺ εἶ ὁ ἐρχόμενος, John's question, against the greeting John sends from ὁ ὢν καὶ ὁ ἦν καὶ ὁ ἐρχόμενος, and ᾧ ἐὰν βούληται ὁ υἱὸς ἀποκαλύψαι against the title of the book, Ἀποκάλυψις Ἰησοῦ Χριστοῦ, ἣν ἔδωκεν αὐτῷ ὁ θεός.

P.5. The epistles are the easier case, for three reasons that are on the surface. The book contains seven letters, so the form is there to be expanded. Ancient testimony says so in as many words: the Muratorian fragment has Paul writing to seven assemblies by name sequens prodecessoris sui Johannis ordinem, following the order of his predecessor John (seated: Tregelles 1867). And there is the matter of ancestry. Letters are everywhere in the Jewish and Greek worlds, and the claim is not about letters as such. It is about one form: a letter from one who speaks for the risen figure to an assembly, with address, commendation, correction and promise. For that form the one surviving ancestor between the Septuagint and Paul is this (#1735). The figure in the midst does not send messages to the assemblies. He gives them to the messengers and commissions them to go and give them: the book commissions future writing, and the epistles are what it commissions.

P.7. The Gospels are the harder case, and this paper takes the harder case. A letter unfolded from a letter keeps its form. A Gospel unfolded from an apocalypse changes everything a reader sees first: vision becomes narrative, the first person becomes the third, heaven becomes a land with towns in it, and what is about to come becomes what happened. No ancient witness says a Gospel was written so. And a Gospel has a second source that cannot be removed from any account of it. If a stated procedure nevertheless reaches from Revelation into Matthew's own material, in Greek, with counts, that is evidence of a different weight from the epistolary case.

P.9. What follows if it holds. First, the materials for which the study of the Gospels posits lost documents are here set against a book that exists and can be inspected, doing some of the same work, and the burden lies on the document that has to be supposed. Second, if the figure of the Gospels can be produced from the figure of the Apocalypse, then that figure is a work of literature before it is a biography, and the search for the person behind the Gospels has a text in front of it that it has not been reading as a source. Third, if Matthew handles the Apocalypse as scripture, the Apocalypse was scripture to the writer of the first Gospel: the latest book of the canon, on the received dating, was among the writings a Gospel was made from. Fourth, the book that the canon puts last and the commentaries treat as derivative would stand at the head.

P.10. Why this will be resisted, stated as costs and not as motives. Items (a) to (c) describe how the field is arranged and can be checked against its handbooks. I.6. The other accounts of the same thing. Three positions have been taken on the sayings the exalted speaker utters in Revelation. This paper is a fourth, and it is nearer the oldest of the three than the newest.

(a) Bultmann. Form criticism held that prophets, speaking in the first person as the risen Lord, supplied sayings which the tradition then carried as sayings of Jesus, and it pointed to Revelation as the place where the process could be watched, 16:15 among its examples (History of the Synoptic Tradition, ET 1963, 127, as Bauckham reports it in his opening notes). Bauckham's article opens from this: the words of the exalted Christ in the Apocalypse are the one undisputed first-century case of utterance in Christ's name in the first person, and so the standing exhibit of everyone who holds that prophets made sayings. The direction worked in this section, from the speaker's saying to the teacher's, was a mainstream position of the last century for sayings of exactly this kind. What that position never proposed is that the exhibit is the source. It reasoned from Revelation to lost oracles like Revelation's. Here the extant book does the work the lost oracles were asked to do.

(b) Vos (1965), the one systematic study, and not read: no open copy was found (see Works cited). As reported by those who have it: twenty-five sayings of Jesus found in Revelation, in a form that holds steady under variation; direct use distinguished from indirect; the conclusion that the seer knew a fixed tradition of the sayings, carried orally (Tripaldi, 6–7, with notes 23–31). Bauckham, in the same notes, calls that conclusion something of an overreaction to (a) and observes that Vos scarcely takes up the question of the prophets. One item bears on the programme directly. At Revelation 2:10b, the crown of life, Vos is reported to find a probable unwritten saying of Jesus (Vos 192–193, as Tripaldi cites it, n. 31). That is the place from which the programme derives James (P.3). On the report, the received account has no source for the promise there either, and supplies one that is not extant.

(c) Bauckham (1977; again 1983; reprinted 1993, 92–117). Read at its first page, in the passages of the reprint that are publicly excerpted, and through a review; not read in full. The thief of 3:3 and 16:15 and the knocking of 3:20 are taken to depend on the Synoptic parables of the Thief and the Watching Servants. Its first three numbered conclusions: such parables were in wide use from the beginning, and for these two the evidence outside the Gospels is good (1 Thessalonians 5:2; 2 Peter 3:10; Didache 16:1; and the versions in Thomas 21 and 103); they were collected and paired early; and use in exhortation wore the story away and left the saying: the word is deparabolization, carried furthest at 3:20, where the figure of the story says "I".

The observation in (c) is the same as I.2. The operation is the inverse. So there is agreement on the textual relation and disagreement on the operation that produced it, and the disagreement is older than this paper: (a) and (c) already hold the two directions between them. One datum both owe an account of. Outside the Gospels and Thomas the thief is everywhere a saying and nowhere a story: 1 Thessalonians 5:2, 2 Peter 3:10, Revelation 3:3 and 16:15. On (c) that is one story worn away four times. On the account here it is one saying kept four times and given a householder once. The count does not decide between them.

J. Give the life its circumstances: Josephus

J.1. The received account, in full. Josephus is the outside witness. XIII. The procedure

XIII.0. The labels. The research record and the ledger name the rules by letter; this paper has mostly used the verbs. P = the three parameters (P1 the speaker given a past; P2 the life told in a named land; P3 the source's own layers open beside it, set out at XIII.1a). M = the moves on who bears a relation: M1 KEEP; M2 EXCHANGE the patient, the adversary's treatment falling on the teacher or on hearers; M3 the costly act of the speaker or the Lamb done by the adversary, or refused; M4 the rider's charge taken by the teacher; M5 OPPOSE commerce, image, wealth; M6 the faithful's trial to the teacher and the seer's part to followers; M7 the speaker's reproofs and exposures said of the adversaries in the land. XIII.2. Three of the seven moves are one consequence of P1. The adversary's sufferings, the Lamb's costly acts and the faithful's trial all need somewhere to land once there is a person with a past, and they land on that person.

XIII.3. Why the frame is where it is. The birth and the tomb are the two densest regions of the map, and there is a reason beyond density. A speaker who is and is to come has no beginning and no end. A person with a past has both. P1 cannot be carried out without a birth and a death, and those are the two things a vision of a living speaker has least reason to tell of that speaker. The source tells them of others: a child born in chapter 12, witnesses killed and raised in 11, an adversary shut in and loosed in 20. The frame is what P1 requires, taken from where the source has it.

XIV.1a. Reach by kind of material. All of Matthew is the wrong denominator for a procedure that leaves Mark in place. Matthew's verses were sorted by machine, and the sorting is a proxy and not a synopsis: a verse counts as Mark's if it shares three content words (or half its content words, at least two) with some verse of Mark; failing that, as shared with Luke on the same test; failing that, as Matthew's own. That gives 458, 184 and 425 verses. The ledger reaches 100 of the first (21.8 per cent), 70 of the second (38.0 per cent) and 135 of the third (31.8 per cent): 305 verses. At the first version these stood at 51, 40 and 87. Before any count the order was predicted with Matthew's own material highest; the material shared with Luke has stood first since the rows found through Philo, and stands further ahead now. Two denominators a reviewer asked for are not yet built: Matthew's changes inside passages shared with Mark, and the scenes that carry the book's structure. Both need a synopsis in the seat.

XIV.2. Unreached. Several chapters have two or three rows. Of the eighty verses of chapters 11 and 12, twenty are reached and the rest are held out of the search, so that a frozen grammar can be tried on them. The genealogy, the body of the Sermon, the healings, the sabbath controversies and most of the narrative shared with Mark are outside. The procedure says "for each stretch of the life" and does not say what the stretches are or their order, beyond the frame. The narrative Matthew shares with Mark is outside it. Whatever account is given of Matthew, Mark is in it.

XIV.3. Unexplained within reach. Where a relation fits two conditions, the procedure does not say which governs. The seat is kept at 19:28 and refused at 20:23. A reviewer asks whether the circumstances of the asking decide it, and the source suggests they do. The promise has a condition in it: Ὁ νικῶν δώσω αὐτῷ καθίσαι μετ’ ἐμοῦ, to the one who conquers, ὡς κἀγὼ ἐνίκησα καὶ ἐκάθισα μετὰ τοῦ Πατρός μου. At 19:28 the seats are told to those who have left everything, as what they shall have. At 20:22 the seat is asked for as rank, and the answer is the condition put as a question, δύνασθε πιεῖν τὸ ποτήριον ὃ ἐγὼ μέλλω πίνειν, and then the Father of the promise's second half: it is for those for whom it is prepared ὑπὸ τοῦ Πατρός μου. So nothing is refused that the source gave. The source's condition is staged. As a rule: a promise with a condition is kept where the condition is taken as met, and its condition is made a trial where the promise is claimed. That is one case, and a rule from one case is a proposal.

XIV.6. Other accounts. The two-source hypothesis, the Farrer–Goulder hypothesis and the two-Gospel hypothesis each say how Matthew stands to Mark and Luke. None places Revelation among Matthew's sources, and this paper does not displace any of them on the ground they cover. It adds a relation they do not consider and shows it is workable as a procedure. Sim (1996) shows that Matthew holds the outlook of apocalyptic eschatology throughout, and compares it with Revelation among others; that is a shared outlook and makes no claim of source. Goulder is the nearest in kind: a writer with one source, expanding it by stated habits of composition. His source was Mark and his habits were midrash and the lectionary. The procedure here has a different source and reaches different parts of the book. Whether the two can be run together on one text, and what each then has left to do, has not been tried and is a question of substance.

XIV.7. Direction. The rows are relations between two texts. Section VII shows that a stated rule produces Matthew's parable from Revelation's two verses: the two verses suffice under the rule supplied. Two cuts were tried, the title alone and the wage alone (Appendix A.2, A.3), and neither produced it. That is sufficiency under one rule and the failure of two cuts. It is no demonstration that both verses are necessary under every rule. No row shows that the rule was run in the first century, or in which direction. One argument from the rows themselves has been put and counted. Read from Revelation to Matthew the operations are those Matthew performs on scripture (S). Read the other way they are different operations: a denial written as an assembly's praise, a guiding star as stars thrown down. If Revelation never treats a source so, the reverse reading asks this writer to break its practice for one book. The count did not bear that out. Revelation's own scripture, on the same instrument, shares a median of two stems a row, as Matthew's does, and the rows between the two books stand as far from the one practice as from the other; and the letters do make scripture's stories into promise and charge (the manna, Balaam, Jezebel). The argument stands as a reading of particular rows. What decides direction, on the programme's account, is not in this paper: the number and the mechanisms of the borrowings taken together across the epistles and the Gospels, and the genealogy of P.2, which runs through extant texts in one order only.

XIV.8. Since the first reissue: what failed, what is owed, and three corrections of method.

Instruments that failed. Five automatic finders were built to locate the letters, Philo or Josephus in Matthew without a reader, each sealed before its run, and all five failed: two rules of rare shared lemmas over the ledger, a third built to price chance, a pair count against Philo, and a finder at the level of the word family whose own null was faulty. What works in this paper is a reader naming pairs from memory, a sealed list, and a check of the words. That is a limit of the method and is stated as one.

Corrections of method, each at the author's word.

1. No received date is a premise. "we must never rely in any established dating in this investigation." The received account is stated first and in full at each place, as a claim to be accounted for. No sentence of this paper weighs direction by a date. Where the first reissue said the Apocalypse was "the latest book of the canon, on the received dating", that is the received account speaking.

2. The order of the Gospels is a premise in neither direction. XIV.1a spoke of "a procedure that leaves Mark in place" and XIV.2 said "whatever account is given of Matthew, Mark is in it."
