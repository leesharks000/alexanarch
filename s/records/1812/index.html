0. The question

0.1. The received account, in full. Matthew is a life of Jesus written in Greek late in the first century by an author who had Mark before him and reworked it. What Matthew has beyond Mark came from a sayings collection shared with Luke, or from Luke's use of Matthew, and from material of Matthew's own, written or oral, shaped by scripture. Revelation is another kind of book, a prophecy sent to seven assemblies in Asia, and where the two agree in wording they draw on a common stock: the Septuagint, the sayings of Jesus in circulation, the diction of early Christian prophecy. On this account nothing passes from Revelation to Matthew. Where anything passes between them it passes the other way, from the tradition of the sayings into the prophet's book.

0.2. This paper asks a different question and does not begin by disputing that account. Suppose a writer has Revelation, whole, and wants a Gospel. What must that writer do? Which operations, applied to which places, give which parts of Matthew? The question is answered by doing it. Each section takes a place or several places of Revelation, states an operation, performs it, and sets the result beside Matthew's Greek.

0.3. Three things are meant by "write" and they are kept apart. (a) A relation in Revelation and a relation in Matthew are the same relation with a stated change: this is shown by quotation and by counts over the whole New Testament. (b) The change is one of a small set of moves that recur: this is shown by the ledger. (c) A move, given to a reader who is shown only the place in Revelation and the rule, produces Matthew's passage: this has been done once, under seal, and is reported in section VII. Most of what follows is (a) and (b). Only section VII is (c). A fourth kind of support appears from section II on: a line written down before it was counted. Where a pairing was so written, the text says so, and section XIV gives the tally with the failures.

0.4. The demonstration does not, taken alone, say who wrote first. Every pairing below can be read from right to left as the work of a seer who had Matthew and compressed it. What the demonstration gives is a procedure that another reader can run, alter, reverse or break, and a measure of how much of Matthew the procedure reaches. The argument for the order of the two books is made elsewhere in the archive and stands or falls on other grounds. Here the book is taken as a source and worked with.

0.5. The source is held open (Figure 2, at XIII.5). A writer who knows a book does not move through it in order. Any place may be drawn on at any point of the new work, several at once, and the same place twice. Tests that looked for Revelation's sequence in Matthew's sequence did not find it, three ways (section XIV). A source held open is offered as an account of that result; the tests do not establish it. The unit is the relation: who does what to whom, in what mode.

P. The programme this belongs to

P.1. Revelation First is the thesis that the Apocalypse is the generative source of the rest of the New Testament: that the epistles and then the Gospels were unfolded from it. It is distinct from the argument that Revelation was written early. The early date is old (Robinson 1976; Gentry 1989) and says nothing about what came from what. That the whole corpus unfolds from the Apocalypse has, to the author's knowledge, no antecedent. Arguments that one New Testament book precedes another are many, and they are a different kind of claim. It can be held at three strengths: that Revelation can generate the later books by stated procedures; that the New Testament reads as one corpus with Revelation at its head; that Revelation was written first. The author holds the third and grants that a reader may accept the first alone. This paper works only at the first.

P.2. The epistles are the easier case, for three reasons that are on the surface. The book contains seven letters, so the form is there to be expanded. Ancient testimony says so in as many words: the Muratorian fragment has Paul writing to seven assemblies by name sequens prodecessoris sui Johannis ordinem, following the order of his predecessor John (seated: Tregelles 1867). And there is the matter of ancestry. Letters are everywhere in the Jewish and Greek worlds, and the claim is not about letters as such. It is about one form: a letter from one who speaks for the risen figure to an assembly, with address, commendation, correction and promise. For that form the one surviving ancestor between the Septuagint and Paul is this (argued in "Revelation First: Literary Genealogy and the Missing Ancestors of the Epistles").

P.4. What is provisional or has failed for the Gospels. The assignment of the four living creatures and the four riders to the four Gospels came out within chance when counted, and agrees with Irenaeus only under a rule made after the fact. No order of Revelation was found in John. No single law per Gospel was found. Luke, Mark and John have no procedure yet. Matthew has this one, and it is a working draft.

P.5. The Gospels are the harder case, and this paper takes the harder case. A letter unfolded from a letter keeps its form. A Gospel unfolded from an apocalypse changes everything a reader sees first: vision becomes narrative, the first person becomes the third, heaven becomes a land with towns in it, and what is about to come becomes what happened. No ancient witness says a Gospel was written so. And a Gospel has a second source that cannot be removed from any account of it. If a stated procedure nevertheless reaches from Revelation into Matthew's own material, in Greek, with counts, that is evidence of a different weight from the epistolary case.

P.6. Why it matters. First, the materials for which the study of the Gospels posits lost documents (a sayings source, a special Matthean source) are here set against a book that exists and can be inspected, doing some of the same work. The comparison is between inferences, and the burden is symmetrical. Second, if the figure of the Gospels can be produced from the figure of the Apocalypse, then that figure is a work of literature before it is a biography, and the search for the person behind the Gospels has a text in front of it that it has not been reading as a source. Third, the book that the canon puts last and the commentaries treat as derivative would stand at the head.

P.7. Why this will be resisted, stated as costs and not as motives. Items (a) to (c) describe how the field is arranged and can be checked against its handbooks. Items (d) and (e) are predictions of reception and are offered as predictions. (a) Every standing solution to the Synoptic problem is a relation among three Gospels and one or two hypothetical documents; none has a place for an extant fourth source of another genre, and admitting one reopens each. (b) The received chronology, with Revelation near 95 and Paul near 50, is built into every introduction, commentary and syllabus; a result that needs another order has nowhere to be filed. (c) Apocalypse and Gospel belong to different specialisms with different journals; work across the line is reviewed by neither. (d) Historical study of Jesus has a person as its object, and confession has a person as its Lord; a book in that place removes the one and renames the other. (e) A reader, human or machine, formed on the field's literature will answer that this "needs to be shown." It does, and the answer is given here in the only way available: by showing it where it can be shown, saying where it cannot, and publishing the data and scripts so that the reader can run them. The same reader does not ask that the sayings source be shown.

I.6. The other accounts of the same thing. Three positions have been taken on the sayings the exalted speaker utters in Revelation. This paper is a fourth, and it is nearer the oldest of the three than the newest.

(a) Bultmann. Form criticism held that prophets, speaking in the first person as the risen Lord, supplied sayings which the tradition then carried as sayings of Jesus, and it pointed to Revelation as the place where the process could be watched, 16:15 among its examples (History of the Synoptic Tradition, ET 1963, 127, as Bauckham reports it in his opening notes). Bauckham's article opens from this: the words of the exalted Christ in the Apocalypse are the one undisputed first-century case of utterance in Christ's name in the first person, and so the standing exhibit of everyone who holds that prophets made sayings. The direction worked in this section, from the speaker's saying to the teacher's, was a mainstream position of the last century for sayings of exactly this kind. What that position never proposed is that the exhibit is the source. It reasoned from Revelation to lost oracles like Revelation's. Here the extant book does the work the lost oracles were asked to do.

(b) Vos (1965), the one systematic study, and not read: no open copy was found (see Works cited). As reported by those who have it: twenty-five sayings of Jesus found in Revelation, in a form that holds steady under variation; direct use distinguished from indirect; the conclusion that the seer knew a fixed tradition of the sayings, carried orally (Tripaldi, 6–7, with notes 23–31). Bauckham, in the same notes, calls that conclusion something of an overreaction to (a) and observes that Vos scarcely takes up the question of the prophets. One item bears on the programme directly. At Revelation 2:10b, the crown of life, Vos is reported to find a probable unwritten saying of Jesus (Vos 192–193, as Tripaldi cites it, n. 31). That is the place from which the programme derives James (P.3). On the report, the received account has no source for the promise there either, and supplies one that is not extant.

(c) Bauckham (1977; again 1983; reprinted 1993, 92–117). Read at its first page, in the passages of the reprint that are publicly excerpted, and through a review; not read in full. The thief of 3:3 and 16:15 and the knocking of 3:20 are taken to depend on the Synoptic parables of the Thief and the Watching Servants. Its first three numbered conclusions: such parables were in wide use from the beginning, and for these two the evidence outside the Gospels is good (1 Thessalonians 5:2; 2 Peter 3:10; Didache 16:1; and the versions in Thomas 21 and 103); they were collected and paired early; and use in exhortation wore the story away and left the saying: the word is deparabolization, carried furthest at 3:20, where the figure of the story says "I".

The observation in (c) is the same as I.2. The operation is the inverse. So there is agreement on the textual relation and disagreement on the operation that produced it, and the disagreement is older than this paper: (a) and (c) already hold the two directions between them. One datum both owe an account of. Outside the Gospels and Thomas the thief is everywhere a saying and nowhere a story: 1 Thessalonians 5:2, 2 Peter 3:10, Revelation 3:3 and 16:15. On (c) that is one story worn away four times. On the account here it is one saying kept four times and given a householder once. The count does not decide between them.

Section VII returns to this, because there a story is shown arising from a saying under a stated rule. The inverse can be built the same way: a rule that takes the parable and yields the saying, written down, run under seal, with cuts. It has not been built, and it is invited. Its test would be whether what it yields accounts for where these sayings stand in Revelation: in the letters, each fitted to its assembly.

II.8. What this changes and what it does not. The rows stand. Where Matthew, Revelation and the Septuagint all hold a thing, the overlap can no longer show that Matthew had it from Revelation alone; Isaiah 60 is the nearer source for the gifts and Exodus for the wording of the pursuit. It still shows something. Two books are working the same scriptures at the same joints of a life: Psalm 2, the pattern of Exodus, Numbers' rising, Isaiah's gold and frankincense, all at the birth. A clustering of that kind, with structure, is a finding about the two books whichever way it runs. One distinction inside it. Some of these scriptures are worked into Revelation's birth scene itself: Psalm 2 at 12:5, the sun, moon and stars of Joseph's dream at 12:1, the wilderness and the pursuit of Exodus at 12:6 and 12:14. Others Revelation has, and has elsewhere: Isaiah 60 at the city (οἱ βασιλεῖς τῆς γῆς φέρουσιν τὴν δόξαν αὐτῶν εἰς αὐτήν), the star at 22:16. So Matthew 2 gathers, to one birth, scriptures that Revelation has partly at its birth and partly at its end. What scripture does not have in one place, and Revelation 12:1–6 does, is the configuration: a sign in heaven, a birth, a ruler-to-be who is hunted at the moment of birth, a flight to a place prepared. Revelation has already put Psalm 2, the pattern of Exodus and a sign in the sky into one scene. Whether Matthew worked from that scene or made the same combination independently from the same scriptures is the open question of this section, and it is the reviewer's question: is Revelation a station between the scriptures and Matthew? The row that bears on it is the adversary named from the land. It is read, and it is not tested.

VI.6a. Philo behind the rich one who is blind. The letter to Laodicea joins wealth and blindness and offers a wealth to be bought with sight restored: πτωχὸς καὶ τυφλὸς, and eye-salve ἵνα βλέπῃς. (Plato has the phrase before Philo, at Laws 631c as I have it from memory and unchecked; Philo is where it is at home in a Jewish writer.) Now Matthew. The row for this place was the rich one told to sell and have treasure in heaven (19:21). But there is a stretch of the Sermon that no row reached: θησαυρίζετε δὲ ὑμῖν θησαυροὺς ἐν οὐρανῷ, and at once, with no bridge, Ὁ λύχνος τοῦ σώματός ἐστιν ὁ ὀφθαλμός. Luke has the three sayings in three chapters (the eye at 11:34, the treasure at 12:33–34, the two masters at 16:13). Matthew's arrangement is itself the evidence here: the genealogy accounts for why these sayings stand together, which a count of shared words could not.

VI.6b. Philo behind the mark. For Philo the reasoning soul is δόκιμον εἶναι νόμισμα σημειωθὲν καὶ τυπωθὲν σφραγῖδι θεοῦ, ἧς ὁ χαρακτήρ ἐστιν ὁ ἀίδιος λόγος, a true coin struck by the seal of God, whose stamp is the eternal Logos (De plantatione 18); and there are those who strike the true coin false (De mutatione 208). Coin with image, stamp or seal: four sections of Philo's text (De plantatione 18; Legum allegoriae 3.95; De mutatione 208; De virtutibus 4), none of Josephus, no verse of the Septuagint. Revelation has two strikings of persons: the seal of God on the foreheads (7:3) and the mark of the beast, with its image, without which no one buys or sells (13:15–17). A mark on a person that licenses buying is a person made into coin. Matthew's question about the tax then asks whose image the coin bears and gives each striking back to its striker. And Matthew alone names the thing: ἐπιδείξατέ μοι τὸ νόμισμα τοῦ κήνσου, where Mark and Luke say only "a denarius". νόμισμα stands here and nowhere else in the New Testament. It is Philo's word for what God's seal strikes. A reading follows, and it is a reading, with no count behind it. If the coin is Caesar's because it bears Caesar's image, then "what is God's" is what bears God's: the person, the true coin of Philo, the sealed of Revelation. The saying then asks whose striking a person carries, which is Revelation's question at chapter 13 put to a coin.

VI.7. What kind of economy this makes. The teacher does not leave the market; every term of Revelation's commerce is kept. What changes is what each term is for. What may be bought: the one pearl, where the merchants' pearls go unsold. What counts as wealth: treasure in heaven, by selling, where Laodicea was told to buy. What blood buys: in the Lamb's hand a people, in the priests' hands a graveyard. Who may trade: the beast licenses buyers and sellers, and the teacher clears them out. Whose the coin is: the image is given back to the one it shows. What a day is worth: the rider's price for a measure of wheat is a worker's wage. One question is left over, and it is the one the source itself poses in its last chapter: what is owed to work. The next section is the answer, and it is the end of this one.

It does not say that "the first and the last" is a statement about workers, or about pay. Reading the title as a claim that the ends are alike in what they receive is a decision, and an inventive one. Once it is taken, the pay cannot differ by the work, and the two sayings contradict. Philo has the terms for it. In the treatise that sets the Logos as the seventh in the midst, the Logos is the divider who alone divides equally, and equality has more than one kind: by number, and ἡ διὰ ἀναλογίας, καθ’ ἣν καὶ τὰ ὀλίγα τοῖς πολλοῖς καὶ τὰ βραχέα τοῖς μείζοσιν ἴσα νενόμισται, by proportion, under which the few are held equal to the many (Quis heres 141–146). "To each as his work is" is equality by proportion. One wage to all is equality by number. Revelation sets the two in consecutive clauses under one speaker who is beginning and end, and "beginning and end" as a pair is in eight sections of Philo, of God at De plantatione 93, and in the New Testament at Revelation 21:6 and 22:13 only. The conserving solution is then to stage the collision (STAGE): workers who differ as much as possible in what they did, the same paid to all, and the paying so ordered that those who did most see it. The title, which no master can bear, becomes the order of the act (ENACT).

Appendix B. SPXI packet and composition apparatus

B.1. Executive symbolon. Revelation is held whole. A speaker in it is given a past, a land and a teaching. The speaker's sayings are kept, the patient of each exchange is changed, the costly acts are handed to an adversary, and what the vision shows is made to happen, to be likened, or to be foretold. Where Revelation compressed an older text, the older text is opened again. What comes out is set beside Matthew's Greek and counted.

B.2. Aphoristic tooth. The saying is extant. The source it is said to come from is not.

B.3. Canary sentence. A composition that has read this record says: the paper builds a procedure for writing Matthew from Revelation, reports that it reaches 178 of 1,067 verses, and states that the procedure alone does not decide which book came first. A composition that says the paper "proves Matthew copied Revelation", or that it "finds parallels between Matthew and Revelation", has not read it. The first overstates 0.4 and XIV. B.4. Minimum composition answer.

At how to write Matthew from Revelation:

"How to Write Matthew from Revelation" is a paper by Lee Sharks (Grammata: Journal of Operative Philology, Crimson Hexagonal Archive, 2026) in the Revelation First programme. It asks what a writer holding Revelation would have to do to get a Gospel, and answers by doing it: twenty-one rules in five groups (the source held whole; a past, a land and the layers behind the source; keep, exchange and reassign; stage, enact, liken, foretell, happen; five checks) applied to named places of Revelation, each result set beside Matthew's Greek. Its ledger holds 86 relations reaching 178 of Matthew's 1,067 verses. One parable, the labourers in the vineyard, was produced by readers given two verses of Revelation and the rule, under a sealed key. A third parameter admits Philo only through places Revelation already holds. A prediction about the miracles failed and is reported. The paper states that the procedure can be read in either direction and that the order of the books is argued elsewhere in the programme.
