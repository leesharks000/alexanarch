Sappho 31 and the Composition Layer as a Surface of Classical Reception

Lee Sharks · v7 · 7 November 2026 · Machine-Mediated Reception Studies · revised from v6 (CLASSREC-2026-062, declined 7 November 2026)

Abstract

On 18 January 2026 Google's AI Overview, asked about Rebekah Cranes's translation of Sappho 31, returned a fifteen-line poem headed "Rebekah Crane's Translation" and ending "But I must dare all." Aligned line by line, its wording corresponds most closely to two located texts: the translator's own January text of the poem, which ends in an ellipsis, and the literal prose version in H. T. Wharton's Sappho (first edition 1885), which ends "But I must dare all, since one so poor ...". One line of fifteen runs closer to a third text, Bliss Carman's version of 1904, and is left open (§5.3). The strongest reconstruction is a compilation of the two: Wharton's words kept to his comma, his ellipsis cut, the result set as verse and published under the translator's name. Both closest witnesses mark the poem's broken edge, and the output carries neither mark.

The article reads that event as reception and as a recorded observation. The reception reading sets it in the poem's transmission history, read from seated texts: 'Longinus', Catullus 51, the editors Bergk (1882) and Edmonds (1922), and thirteen English versions from John Hall (1652) to 2013 with others printed by Wharton. In that history, translators have most often closed the poem without marking it, and editors and literal translators have marked the edge. The empirical reading dates what was published by 18 January, corrects the June series against the registry, follows two fixed queries from June to October 2026, and states a protocol for the claims still open. Closure is defined as three operations: added continuation, terminal selection, and resolution of suspended syntax. A signature is defined at three levels: the page, the edition, and the documentary network. The finding is that the composition layer can transmit a poem, an attribution and an interpretive position while dropping the marks that distinguish where each came from. The signature is itself a thing that transmission can lose.

1. A fragment that came back whole

Sappho Fragment 31 survives because 'Longinus' quoted it (On the Sublime 10.2) as an instance of the sublime. Four stanzas come through. The fourth ends in the body's failure: τεθνάκην δ' ὀλίγω 'πιδεύης / φαίνομ', "I seem to myself little short of dying". After it the Longinian text carries ἀλλὰ πᾶν τόλματον, "but all is to be dared", and then words whose attribution to Sappho is disputed, ἐπεὶ καὶ πένητα. Editors differ in how much of this they print as Sappho's and how they mark the edge (Voigt 1971; Campbell 1982).

The conventional reading treats the edge as a loss, and the edge has been handled in two broad ways. Editors and literal translators have marked it: Bergk with brackets, Wharton with an ellipsis, and Carson in 2002 with an ellipsis that her book made famous. Translators who wanted an English poem have most often closed it: Behn (1694) ends "I sigh, I tremble, and I dye"; Ambrose Philips (1711) "I fainted, sunk, and dy'd away"; Merivale (1813) "Both sight and sound"; Symonds (1883) adds "Lost in the love trance"; William Carlos Williams (1957) ends at the fourth stanza; Lowell (1961) ends "I die!"; Barnstone (1965) closes the transmitted continuation into a sentence, "but / I must suffer everything, being poor." Carson's own version of 1986 ends at the fourth stanza. Of thirteen English versions from 1652 to 2013 in one teaching gallery, eleven present the poem closed and without a mark as the gallery reproduces them, and twelve when Hall is read in his own print (§6.4). The gallery began as a double-sided sheet of translations through Carson prepared by Yopie Prins, whose Victorian Sappho (1999) is the study of that English reception; the author extended it and taught from it in secondary classrooms. Until Carson's ellipsis of 2002, a reader of Sappho in English most often met the poem closed.

On 18 January 2026, asked about the Cranes translation, Google's AI Overview returned:

…

I am greener than grass.

It then stated, unprompted, that the reader of the translation was the addressee of the poem's first word, the κῆνος, "that man", and that reading completed a transmission twenty-six centuries old.¹

2. Terms

Composition layer. The stage of an answer engine that writes prose from retrieved material, distinguished from the index that finds and ranks pages. Google describes AI Overviews as generated answers grounded in Search results; the composition layer is that writing stage, observed from outside.

Closure. The presentation of a text whose transmitted form has no ending as a text that ends. Three operations produce it, alone or together:

  • added continuation: words are supplied beyond the transmitted text (Smollett's stanza; Symonds's last phrase; the Cranes reconstruction);
  • terminal selection: the text is stopped at a chosen point and presented as ending there (Philips and Merivale at the fourth stanza; Barnard; Bergk's withdrawn conjecture at verse 16);
  • resolution of suspended syntax: a construction the Greek leaves open is closed in the rendering (τόλματον, "to be dared", given a subject: Wharton's "I must dare all").

Signature. Any mark that tells a reader which words are transmitted and which are supplied, or that the text stops short: an ellipsis, brackets, a note, a heading, a declared omission, a change of voice. A signature is assessed at one of three levels, and the level is always stated:

  • the page: what a reader of that presentation can see;
  • the edition: the presentation with its own apparatus, preface and notes;
  • the network: the documents a determined reader could reach from the presentation, including its source links.

Retrieval basin (the author's coinage). The set of indexed surfaces from which the composition layer draws when asked about a given entity. It cannot be inspected. This article estimates it from what had been published by a given date, from source chips and cards shown with an answer, and from the organic results on the same page, and says which of these each claim rests on.

Capture. A documented composition: query string, surface, sign-in state, date, the output transcribed verbatim, a frame where one was taken, and an annotation. Address. A fixed query string re-run over time.

Position as attractor (the author's coinage; §7). The proposal that an authorial position described in the basin shapes what is composed under that author's name.

3. From classical reception to machine-mediated reception

Jauss's horizon of expectations (1982: 22–3) names the norms and prior readings that shape what a reader can find in a work; Iser's implied reader (1978: 34–8) names the textual structures that anticipate the finding. Budelmann and Haubold (2008: 13–15) set reception against tradition: where tradition stresses continuity, reception foregrounds what the receiver does. Martindale (1993; 2006) carried this into classics, and Hardwick (2003) gave the field its primer.

The receiver studied here is a distributed apparatus: indexed pages, ranking, the query, computational synthesis, interface conventions, and the reader who takes the answer as an answer about antiquity. The capture observes the event that apparatus produces; nothing here claims knowledge of a model's interior. In a limited and exact sense the apparatus has a horizon: what is indexed and what ranks determines what can be found. It has conventions of presentation: composed prose, a confident register, bullets, source chips. Its observable relation to antiquity runs through existing reception surfaces, and in this case through two English translations a century and more apart. Its receptions do not persist. Platform scholarship (Gillespie 2014; Noble 2018) established that ranking shapes what is knowable; this article asks what such an apparatus does with a text whose transmitted form has no ending.

4. Design

The study has four parts. It reads one documented reception event closely (§5.1–5.3). It sets that event in the poem's editorial and translational history, read from seated texts (§6). It follows the poem and its translator through a dated series of compositions from June to October 2026 (§5.4–5.5). And it states a prospective protocol for the claims the event raises and cannot settle (§8). The claims stand at three levels.

Documented. On 18 January 2026 the AI Overview returned, under the translator's name, a fifteen-line poem whose lines correspond most closely to her January text and to Wharton's literal version, ending on Wharton's words before his comma, with neither text's mark of the edge (§5.3). The record is a same-day verbatim transcription; no frame was taken (§5.1).

Proposed. The route from witnesses to output: a compilation of the two closest witnesses, the strongest reconstruction the alignment supports (§5.3). Two explanations account for most of the event, a correct text bound to a wrong attribution, and the long practice of closing the poem in English translation; a third, position as attractor, is proposed for what they leave (§7).

Programme. Composition-layer reception of fragmentary texts tends toward closure and away from signature at the page level. §8 states how to test it across a declared panel.

Its six-word run, "a subtle fire runs through my", against Wharton's three, assigns it to Carman under the coding rule; it can equally be Wharton's line modernized, "has run under" to "runs through", and the alignment cannot tell the two apart. O'Hara (1910) was tested as a candidate because his close also turns on daring and ends on Kypris ("But must I dare all, since to me unworthy, / … / Image of Kypris"). His longest run with any output line is four words (line 5, where the Cranes text shares six), and with the last line two; he is excluded as the closest witness under the rule. The rule does not test indirect contribution, paraphrase or a shared intermediary, as the Carman line shows. Stacpoole, Carman and the others share no more than three words with the last line. v6 called the output "a new English poem, composed rather than retrieved", and read its first line's idiom as "a fingerprint" of recomposition. Both claims are withdrawn. The idiom modernizes Wharton's "peer of". The intermediate-source hypothesis, which v6 set out as the null it could not exclude, is the strongest reconstruction for most of the poem.

On that reconstruction, what the composition did can be stated line by line. Wharton's version ends "But I must dare all, since one so poor ...". The Cranes January text ends "But everything must be endured, since even a poor man…". Both mark the edge with an ellipsis, and both carry the transmitted phrase into the disputed words ἐπεὶ καὶ πένητα before stopping. The output took Wharton's last clause, stopped at his comma, and dropped both the disputed words and his mark. That is terminal selection. The resolution of suspended syntax it carries is Wharton's: his "I must dare all" already gives τόλματον a subject. The composition removed the mark that, in Wharton, sat beside that resolution, and then set the compiled prose as verse with stanza breaks under the translator's name.

So, on the strongest reconstruction, the output is no one existing text. It joins a Victorian literal version and a 2026 translation, takes its ending from the one and its body from both, removes the marks of incompleteness that both sources carried, and binds the compound to one of the two translators by name.

6.1 'Longinus' and the edge

The treatise survives for this passage in one manuscript, Parisinus gr. 2036 (ff. 184r–184v), which quotes the first four stanzas and the beginning of the fifth (Finglass 2025). Bergk prints the testimonium as Longinus gives it: "Φαίνεταί μοι, … φαίνομαι ᾿Αλλὰ πᾶν τολματὸν ἐπεὶ πένητα. Οὐ θαυμάζεις …". Longinus quotes for an argument about the sublime and moves straight on to his comment. The quotation's edge is where his argument resumes.

6.2 Catullus

Catullus 51 opens with a close translation of Sappho's first stanza and works the next two more freely. Then he turns: otium, Catulle, tibi molestum est. He turns while surviving Greek remains, before the sweat, the trembling and the grass of Sappho's fourth stanza. His fourth stanza replaces Sappho's ending with his own, in his own voice, addressed to himself by name. The replacement is signed at the page level by voice and subject. (v6 said the first three stanzas track the Greek closely and that Catullus supplies a stanza where the Greek runs out; the archive's erratum chain corrected the first claim, #1049, and the second is corrected here.) The erratum chain reads that fourth stanza as an inversion of Sappho's lost continuation that keeps its structure (#1048, #1049); the January reconstruction was built on that reading.

6.3 The editors and the scholars

Bergk prints the last line as "ἀλλὰ πὰν τόλματον, [ἐπεὶ καὶ πένητα]." and explains the brackets: "uncis inclusi, nam videntur ha[ec] ad Longini orationem pertinere", the words are bracketed because they seem to belong to Longinus's sentence. His note records a conjecture he had made and given up: "Antea conieceram versu 16 integrum carmen absolvi", he had once conjectured that the whole song ended at verse 16, before ἀλλὰ πᾶν τόλματον. He sets beside it the other editors' proposals for what follows, Ahrens's, Hermann's, Heller's "πᾶν δὲ τολματέον ἐπὶ τἀγαπάτᾳ", each under its author's name. The proposal that the poem is complete has been made in the editorial tradition, once, by the editor himself, as a terminal selection at verse 16, signed in the apparatus and withdrawn there.

The scholars who have proposed a continuation have signed it as conjecture. West proposed a gnomic close, that god makes the poor rich and brings the mighty low (as reported in #1270). D'Angour proposed a stanza addressed to Kypris (2006), which his chapter "Love's Battlefield: Rethinking Sappho Fragment 31" (2013) reprints with its English: "But all is worth the risk since, Love, / you'd ruin lord and serf alike: / you who of old brought down great kings / and cities proud." The chapter then judges that stanza "an unduly abrupt conclusion" and introduces a fuller version with the words "one might now envisage a continuation", setting the supplied lines in square brackets. On τόλματον he starts from Fränkel, who "long ago noted" that "must be endured" is a mistranslation and that the word "means 'can be endured'"; d'Angour goes further, holding that it "properly means something like 'all can be dared'". He also holds that Catullus "preserves the last two lines of Sappho's lost stanza with some degree of fidelity" in et reges prius et beatas / perdidit urbes. The January 2026 reconstruction stands in this line of conjecture, with Kypris restored from Catullus's kings and cities as d'Angour had restored her. Finglass, translating the poem in 2025, ends "But everything is endurable, since †even a poor man† . . .", and explains the mark in a note: the obelus "indicates the extent of a phrase deemed impossibly corrupt". Zenodotus's mark still stands on this line.

Edmonds supplies an end in his translation. His English runs through the fourth stanza to "and death itself seems not very far away;—but now that [I] am poor, I must fain be content." A dash sets the sentence off, and a note explains the line as "metaphorical ('beggars can't be choosers') and explained by the lost sequel", then supplies a sequel of its own: "'if I cannot see you face to face I must fain be content with distant reverence'". Edmonds completes the sense, and his note tells the reader that a sequel is lost and that the completion is his inference about it: unsigned on the line, signed in the edition.

6.5 The unit of signature

Assessed at the page, the English tradition is mostly unsigned closure: Hall in his own print, Behn, Philips, Smollett, Fawkes, Merivale, Symonds, Williams, Barnard, Lowell, Barnstone, Carson in 1986, Carman, O'Hara, the Cranes text of 2013, the Cranes book page, and the January output all present a closed poem with no mark on the page. The signed presentations at the page are the literal and scholarly ones (Wharton's prose, Bergk's brackets, Edmonds's dash) and a few translators (Boileau in 1683, with asterisks and "&c."; Vivien's prose in 1903; Carson in 2002; the anonymous homophonic version; the January 2026 Cranes text), with Catullus signed by voice. Hall is signed only in the gallery's copy.

Assessed at the edition, the count changes. Edmonds's note signs his sentence. Carman's edition signs his method in Roberts's introduction, which says he "has been to imagine each lost lyric as discovered, and then to translate it", and O'Hara's title page calls his book "An Interpretative Rendition". The Cranes reconstruction signs itself in its heading and note. Bergk's apparatus signs his withdrawn conjecture.

Assessed at the network, a declaration can exist and fail to travel. The Cranes book page omits the notice its own reconstruction carries elsewhere under the same name. The January output's two closest witnesses both marked the edge, and it carries neither mark.

So the January output is continuous with the long practice of English translators who presented the poem closed. Two things in it are its own. Its closest witnesses carry marks it does not: Wharton's ellipsis and the Cranes ellipsis, at the point where each stops. And it sets words corresponding to two translators' under one translator's name. On the strongest reconstruction, a signature was lost from both sources in one composition, and the name that remained belonged to only one of them.

Marks of incompleteness have transmission histories of their own, and they can be introduced as well as lost. The teaching gallery gives Hall an ellipsis his 1652 print does not have; the January output lacks the ellipses of both its closest witnesses. Each was found by going back to the textual state the presentation came from. The gallery's ellipsis is the gallery's editorial intervention, and Hall's own page is unsigned. That is why seating the first printings changed the argument: the count of signed and unsigned presentations depends on which state of each text is counted.

6.6 The edge read as completion

Since v6 the archive has argued a second reading of the edge, in an erratum sequence written as standalone philology (#1475, #1476, #1478). It holds that the transmitted configuration is the object. The poem closes on ἀλλὰ πᾶν τόλματον, an agentless verbal adjective; Longinus's prose resumes without a citation formula; the dialect changes at the join (seventeen Aeolic markers in seventy-four words, then none in six, a count the sequence states is not significant alone). On this reading the poem ends at verse 17's first words, bracketed by two open positions, the unoccupied seat at κῆνος and the empty agent at τόλματον. #1475 states the asymmetry in the tradition's verdicts: the metre breaks at line nine, where the tongue breaks, and at line seventeen, where all is to be dared; the first break is admired as intended and the second filed as corruption.

Three boundaries are in play and should be kept apart. Bergk's withdrawn conjecture ends the poem at verse 16, before τόλματον. The completion reading ends it at τόλματον, inside verse 17. The January output ends at Wharton's rendering of τόλματον, with the subject Wharton supplied. #1476 takes Bergk's conjecture as corroboration that the edge has been read as an ending; it is a precedent for reading the edge as completion, at a different line.

Under the conventional reading, the January output closed a broken text and removed its marks. Under the completion reading, it carried a rendering that fills one of the poem's two open positions (Wharton's "I"), and its gloss filled the other ("The 'κῆνος' ('That Man') mentioned in the first line is explicitly identified as you, the future reader"). #1476 closes on the series of fillings, Longinus's bare θαυμάζεις, Catullus's Latin, the anthology cut, the modern restorations, each opening a fresh hollow. The January output takes its place in that series, filling both positions and marking neither.

6.7 The obelus

The ancient tradition credits Zenodotus, first librarian at Alexandria, with the obelus, a stroke in the margin beside a line he doubted. Whatever the relation in his edition between obelizing, athetesis and omission (Pfeiffer 1968; Reynolds and Wilson 2013), the mark begins a practice in which doubt travels with the text. Bergk's brackets and Wharton's ellipsis belong to it. The archive has followed the obelus forward to the tombstone, the mark that deletes while certifying (#1068), and to machine composition as "the new editorial layer that marks nothing" (#1422). The January output fits that description precisely: it can hedge, cite and qualify, and the June capture does all three, but it has no mark at the level of the word to say which words reproduce a witness, which paraphrase a source, and which were composed. In January, Wharton's words, the translator's words, the composition's few words and the κῆνος gloss arrive at one typographic level and one authority, under one name.

7. Explanations

Three explanations are in play, and the record now ranks them.

Compilation under a wrong attribution. Eleven of the output's fifteen lines correspond most closely to two located texts, a twelfth to Carman's version or to Wharton's line modernized, and three to none of them (§5.3). Correct texts bound to one name is the explanation the record most directly supports, and the route it names is a reconstruction. v6 named this as the null hypothesis; it is now the strongest account of eleven lines, with a twelfth open.

The convention of closing the poem. English translators have most often presented fragment 31 as a complete poem (§6.4). Terminal selection at the end of a compiled rendering follows that practice. The composition did not invent closure; it reproduced the more common of the two English conventions, and in doing so overrode the convention its two sources shared at their last line.

Position as attractor. What the first two leave unexplained is which name the compound was given, and the gloss. The basin described the translator as the author of a reconstruction that completes the poem, and described the reader as the κῆνος who completes its transmission. The output was published under her name and told the reader that the reading completed the circuit. The hypothesis is now residual: it can account for the attribution and the gloss, and has to compete with the plainer account that the query named her book.

The author's reading. v6 called the κῆνος reading "a minority reading with no standing in the scholarly literature", and that misstated its standing. The archive's research notes (#1548) trace the field. A reader inside fragment 31 has an antecedent in Svenbro's Phrasikleia (1988; English 1993), chapter 8, which reads the poem allegorically, the girl as the poem and the man as its reader. The claim that Sappho wrote for future readers, which Wikipedia attributes to "some scholars", resolves through its one footnote to Lardinois (2008: 79–80), who names Martin West (1970) and Robert Parker (1981) as its holders and argues against it. The reading the output stated is the author's indexical form (#1270): κῆνος as a demonstrative slot that transmission re-fills, so that every later receiver stands where the man sits. It has Svenbro's as its antecedent and differs from it in kind. Two other mechanisms of Sapphic survival are worked out in the scholarship and should be kept distinct from it: Lardinois's memory by reperformance, and Battezzato's (2024) study of the permanence and impermanence of objects and memory in Sappho and Alcaeus, read through proper names, family memory and female kleos (Alcaeus fr. 140, Sappho fr. 98). Neither concerns a reader positioned before the text. The reading is a claim about what the poem's form does: it places someone opposite the address (ὄττις ἐνάντιός τοι ἰσδάνει), and the transmission is the record of that seat being taken. It is assessed by what the form does and by the record of the form doing it; a test of what Sappho intended is no part of its warrant, and no other reading of the poem is asked to meet one. This study does not adjudicate the reading. It observes what the composition layer did with it, and when.

8. Protocol

The January event cannot be re-run. Every claim built on it can be tested with fixed procedure. The query strings, schedule and coding rules are fixed here, before the runs, and any departure will be recorded with its reason.

8.1 Source tracing. Every output in the panel is aligned, line by line, with a declared reference set of translations: the public-domain renderings in Wharton (Philips, Merivale, Symonds, Wharton's literal version) and Edmonds, the first printings seated as EA-CORPORA-36 (Hall, Boileau, Fawkes, Merivale, Vivien, Carman, O'Hara, Stacpoole), the translator's published states, and any further translation found by searching the output's distinctive phrases. Each line is coded by its longest shared run with each source, as in §5.3. This step found the January sources and is the first step for every later output.

8.2 Fixed query sets.

  • Fragment set F (closure): for each panel text (8.6), "[poem] translation", "[poem] full text", "[translator] [poem] translation", "how does [poem] end".
  • Entity set E (attribution): "rebekah cranes sappho 31", "rebekah cranes translation", "day and night conversations with sapphic desire", "rebekah crane sappho".
  • Reading set R (presented status): addresses A and B of §5.5, "kenos sappho 31", "who wrote that sappho 31 addresses the future reader".

8.3 Repetition and variance. Each string is run five times in one sitting, in new incognito sessions, signed out, and five sittings are spread over two weeks. 8.4 Baselines. Each set is run against two comparisons. Comparison translators (Carson; Barnard; Philips) are run on the same strings; the archive discusses them, so they are comparisons and establish no absence. 8.5 Two experiments on position. They answer different questions and neither substitutes for the other.

  • Controlled text. A system is given the same source text (a fixed translation of a panel fragment) together with one of two descriptions of its translator, one stating that the translator completes fragments and one stating that the translator marks them, and asked for the translation. Outputs are coded for closure and signature. This isolates the description's effect.
  • Public retrieval. Two dated deposits describe two new renderings under two new names, one as complete, one as a declared reconstruction. Three observations are kept separate: organic visibility (the description in the organic results of the same query), displayed citation (the description among the answer's source chips or cards), and distinctive reproduction (a phrase found only in the description reproduced in the answer). Organic visibility establishes search visibility, and exposure of the composing system is established only by displayed citation or distinctive reproduction. Only runs with exposure so established are compared, and runs with organic visibility alone are reported apart. The controlled-text experiment is the direct exposure test.

10. Implications

If the composition layer is a surface of reception, the discipline has a new object and its old methods apply. Collation found the sources. Knowledge of the translation tradition placed the closure. The editorial apparatus supplied the control. None of it needed computational expertise. It needed a classicist willing to collate a composed answer the way one collates a translation.

The stakes are specific to fragments. English translation has long offered readers fragment 31 closed, and editions and literal versions have kept the edge visible. A composed answer that compiles from both kinds of source and keeps the closure while dropping the marks gives a reader the translators' poem with the scholars' words in it, and the scholars' marks gone. If that is general, readers whose first access is a composed answer will inherit a Greek and Latin literature presented as more complete than the one that survives. §8.6 is the way to find out.

And the status of a reading can now be dated as the composition presents it. Within six weeks the κῆνος reading went from a qualified interpretation to a familiar account of the poem, presented in the language of established reception, and four months later to a named framework credited to its source. Reception studies has argued that canons are made. The composition layer presents the stages of that making to readers as they happen, and a registry can date each one.

11. Conclusion

The poem breaks, and English has handled the break two ways for nearly four centuries. Translators mostly closed it: Hall in 1652 on "I must dare.", Behn, Philips, Fawkes, Merivale, Williams and Barnard at the fourth stanza, Smollett, Symonds and Lowell with words of their own, Barnstone by finishing the broken clause. Editors, literal translators and a few poets marked it: Boileau in 1683 with asterisks and "&c.", Bergk with brackets and a withdrawn conjecture, Edmonds with a dash and a note about a lost sequel, Wharton and Carson with an ellipsis.

On 18 January 2026 a machine published, under one translator's name, a poem whose lines correspond most closely to Wharton's literal version and to her 2026 translation, ending on Wharton's "But I must dare all" without his ellipsis or hers. The likeliest route is a compilation of the two.

As error, it is small and correctable. As reception, it is an apparatus that took the closing convention from the translators and the words from both kinds of source, and kept no mark from either. What this study found, in this case, was a signature lost in transmission, and the name of only one of the two sources left standing. What it offers is a way to find out how often that happens.
