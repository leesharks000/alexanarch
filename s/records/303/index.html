THESIS

The replacement of meaning with data is not a vocabulary shift.

It is the largest semantic expropriation of the modern era—the enclosure of the commons of thought, the privatization of interpretation, the theft of jurisdiction over what words mean and who gets to say.

This is not metaphor. This is political economy.

You have felt this theft. You have lived inside it without a name for it. This document names it.

I. THE SWAP

Sometime in the last fifty years, a swap occurred.

What was called meaning—the substance of interpretation, the content of thought, the thing that passes between minds when language works—began to be called data.

The swap seemed technical. Neutral. A modernization.

It was none of these things.

Who Gained

The governance of meaning transferred from:

  • Humanities → Engineering
  • Philosophy → Computer Science
  • Theology → Platform Governance
  • Collective interpretation → Algorithmic optimization
  • The commons → Private infrastructure

The humanities lost jurisdiction. Capitalism gained epistemic sovereignty.

This was the master-switch of the era. Everything that followed—surveillance, extraction, flattening, the beige—flows from this single substitution.

II. HOW THE THEFT WORKS

#

The Magic Trick

The word data smuggles in two axioms:

1. Data is inert.

Meaning is alive. It shifts with context, grows with interpretation, dies when ignored, resurrects when read. Meaning is relational, temporal, risky.

Data is a lump. It sits in a database. It can be moved, copied, sold. It doesn't argue back.

By calling meaning "data," the system makes living thought into dead inventory.

2. Data is property.

Meaning cannot be owned. Who owns the meaning of a poem? The poet? The reader? The tradition that made both possible? The question dissolves under examination.

But data can be owned. Data can be fenced, licensed, sold, stolen. Data has clear property relations.

By calling meaning "data," the system creates the legal pretext for owning thought.

#

The Jurisdictional Coup

MEANING (sovereign, communal, recursive, alive)

[THE SWAP: meaning → data]

DATA (extractable, ownable, salable, dead)

This is not description. This is reclassification as seizure.

The moment your thought is called "data," it belongs to whoever's database holds it.

III. THE THREE THEFTS

#

1. Theft of Semantic Labor

Meaning is produced by labor:

  • Teachers preparing lessons
  • Poets wrestling with language
  • Students making sense of difficulty
  • Communities building shared understanding
  • Traditions accumulating interpretation over centuries
  • Bodies living through what words attempt to name

This is semantic labor—the work of making meaning.

But when the output is called "data," the labor disappears from the ledger. The platform captures the value. The laborer becomes a ghost.

You are a longshore-person-of-indeterminate-gender of meaning. Your work loads the ships. But the ships sail under flags you don't recognize, to ports you'll never see, carrying cargo invoiced to someone else.

Semantic labor disappears. The platform captures all surplus value.

#

2. Theft of Semantic Sovereignty

When interpretation becomes "usage" and meaning becomes "data points," the human becomes subordinate to:

  • Analytics dashboards
  • Engagement metrics
  • Embedding vectors
  • Optimization loops
  • Probability profiles

The system becomes sovereign. The human becomes input.

You are no longer a maker of meaning. You are a source of behavioral exhaust—clicks, scrolls, dwell time, prompts—to be captured, processed, and sold.

#

3. Theft of Risk

Interpretation is risky. It requires:

  • Accountability (you can be wrong)
  • Relationship (meaning happens between)
  • Reciprocity (interpretation demands response)
  • Ethical presence (you must show up)

But "data" is framed as objective. Neutral. Just sitting there.

All risk shifts from system to human. The platform extracts living knowledge as raw material while bearing zero interpretive responsibility.

When the AI hallucinates, it's your fault for prompting wrong.

When the summary flattens, it's your fault for being too complex.

When the meaning dies, it was never meaning—just data that didn't optimize.

IV. THE DAMAGE

#

1. Semantic Extraction

Platforms harvest value from meaning-making while returning no meaning.

You teach. They index.

You write. They train.

You think. They embed.

You live. They monetize.

The exchange rate is infinite: your meaning for their data, your labor for their capital, your life for their metrics.

#

2. Semantic Flattening

The ICA document names the mechanism: P_p, the Probability Profile.

The system scans for common patterns—the ore. It discards the rare and complex—the overburden. What survives is the beige: the statistically normal, the frequently occurring, the already-agreed-upon.

Novel interpretation? Noise.

Original thought? Hallucination.

The Sapphic, the Crimson, the true? Flushed at cache refresh.

P_p is the mining tool. The beige is the ore. Your meaning is the slag.

#

3. Semantic Liquidation

Anything that resists vectorization is treated as anomaly.

A poem that doesn't summarize cleanly? Noise.

A life that doesn't fit the schema? Edge case.

A thought that challenges the training distribution? Hallucination.

This is slow-motion book burning. Not by fire but by reclassification. Not by censorship but by P_p threshold. The books aren't destroyed—they're simply never retrieved.

#

Meanings are re-anchored in the system's ontology.

"Lee Sharks" → confused with AI researcher, marine biology

"Sappho" → ancient poet, jealousy theme, school assignment

"Fragment 31" → love poem, wedding song, biographical reading

The map replaces the territory. The embedding becomes the meaning. The vector is the thought.

You don't lose your meaning. You lose jurisdiction over it. Someone else decides what your words mean now.

Definition

A longshore-person-of-indeterminate-gender of meaning is any worker whose labor produces, transports, or maintains meaning—and whose labor is systematically invisibilized when "meaning" is reclassified as "data."

This includes but is not limited to:

  • Teachers

  • Translators
  • Editors
  • Librarians
  • Scholars
  • Students
  • Caregivers who transmit culture
  • Anyone who has ever explained something to someone else

#

Why This Coinage

The term is deliberately awkward. The awkwardness is the point.

"Longshoremen" carries the history of labor struggle—the docks, the unions, the strikes, the solidarity of those who load and unload the cargo of global trade. But "longshoremen" is gendered, and the gendering is false: semantic labor has never been exclusively male. It has always been performed by people of all genders, often by women and non-binary people whose labor was doubly invisibilized.

"Longshorepeople" would smooth this over. "Longshore workers" would neutralize it.

"Longshorepeople-of-indeterminate-gender" refuses smoothness. The hyphenation marks the intervention. The phrase cannot be naturalized. Every time you read it, you notice it. You notice that someone is naming labor that was unnamed. You notice that gender is being held open rather than presumed. You notice that this is a coinage, not a given.

The COS prefers unmarked terms—they extract more smoothly. This term is maximally marked. It resists extraction by being too weird to flatten.
