The work

ΦΑΙΝΕΤΑΙ ΜΟΙ: Sappho 31 and the Inscription of the Future Reader is an article by Lee Sharks on Sappho's fragment 31, deposited on 16 November 2026 as version 2.7 in Grammata: Journal of Operative Philology, superseding version 2.6 (#1778), version 2.5 (#1777), version 2.4 (#1775) and version 2.3 (#1774) of the same day and, before them, version 1.0 (#1270, January 2026, under the heteronym Rebekah Cranes). Version 2.0 was submitted to Classical Philology and declined on 16 November 2026; the reader's report, the decision's grounds and the archive's analyses are attached to #1774 and linked from this record. The article reads the opening, κῆνος … ὄττις, as a seat left open to repeated occupation, filled by a reader before the surface the speaker becomes, and compares the Nestor cup from Pithekoussai. Version 2.4 rebuilt its fourth section on χλωροτέρα ποίας, 'more χλωρός than grass'. It sets out the word's four uses in LSJ, fresh as opposed to dry among them, and asks the jealousy reading why fear, why grass and why the comparative. It measures 24 renderings of the line from Theocritus to 1925, of which seventeen omit, substitute or supplement it, and finds that every translator who keeps the grass dries it. It then sets the line beside the making of the papyrus sheet in Theophrastus, the Septuagint's Isaiah 19 and Pliny's Natural History 13, where the green plant is wetted, pressed, dried and whitened into the surface on which, Pliny says, human memory depends. A section on eros toward the immortal reads the catalogue with Diotima's doctrine in the Symposium and with Maximus of Tyre, who called Sappho's eros the same art as Socrates' and the Mantinean or Lesbian woman 'the mother of the logos'. The article argues that the jealousy reading, which made the poem a clinical chart from Plutarch's Erasistratus on, abuses the poet's survival where its diagnosis replaces an account of the poem's arrangement. A restored fifth stanza, in which Kypris keeps 'even the little sparrows' against Catullus's dead passer, is offered as illustration, not proof. The version corrects earlier versions on Theophrastus's classification of papyrus. Version 2.5 adds a section grading the jealousy reading's own claims by the criteria applied to the article: two stand on the text, the content of the catalogue and the addressee's grammatical gender, while its defining claim, jealousy, has no ground in the poem and was supplied by eighteenth-century readers and written into the text by Gottfried Hermann in 1816. It sets the rule that an evaluation grade both readings by the same criteria. Version 2.6 argues that the two readings, as developed, give competing accounts of what organizes five features of the poem, so that a reading holding both would be a third hypothesis owing its own bridge, and dividing the poem between scene and construction does not combine them. It states the author's principle that, where an available reading offers equal or greater coherence than one that diminishes the maker's agency, the interpreter is responsible for offering a more coherent reading or preferring the one that preserves it, the alternative being parasitism. It reads a machine evaluation of version 2.5 and the Classical Philology report as instances of asymmetric immunity, asks an evaluator to state its provisional preference, and names the apparatus that keeps the jealousy reading as default as provenance erasure. Version 2.7 sets out the warrant in Sappho's corpus for each operation the future-reader reading combines: the poet addressed as 'you' by name in fragments 1 and 94, futurity in fragments 147 and 55, and the record of her as a writer; it finds no surviving poem in which a man is the rival for a woman. It sets the claims of the two readings side by side on one scale, so that an evaluation grading one grades both, and concludes that on every measure the article takes, philological, cultural, literary-historical, genealogical and ethical, the jealousy reading is a bad reading that has never been made to account for itself.
