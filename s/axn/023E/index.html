The work

`EA-HK-01 v1.1` is the general Holographic Kernel specification for Semantic Economy.

Its canonical definition is:

> A holographic kernel is a compression that preserves reconstructive capacity: any sufficiently structured fragment contains enough relational information to regenerate the architecture of the whole.

Three invariants are required:

1. Boundary-to-bulk reconstruction — the fragment retains generative logic rather than the full material object. 2. Fragment sufficiency — a separated kernel instance must support reconstruction of the parent architecture. 3. Compression with structural preservation — the kernel is smaller than the source while retaining non-derivable rules, relations, constraints, and dependencies.

A kernel is not a summary, excerpt, table of contents, keyword list, or loose metaphor for fractality. The litmus test asks whether a reader can derive a forbidden operation, derive a dependency chain, and rebuild the topology.

The construction protocol uses five UKTP questions:

- What agents exist? - What operations occur? - What dependencies govern order and enablement? - What constraints are mandatory or forbidden? - What topology organizes the system?

Compression proceeds by operation rather than selection. Any material uniquely derivable from retained structure may be discarded; non-derivable structure must remain.

Verification uses:

- Back-Projection Test: reconstruct the source architecture from the fragment. - Anti-Summary Test: recover prohibition, dependency, and topology. - NLCC Validity Test: apply the archive’s formal non-lossy conditions.

The record inventories proposed kernels at archive, series, field, document, operator, and entity scale. Its worked example treats the Space Ark Compact Lens as a kernel and reports a back-projection yield of 0.88.

The phrase “holographic kernel” already exists in multiple scientific and technical contexts. `EA-HK-01` does not claim to originate the phrase. It proposes a substrate-general semantic and archival operation and a shared construction-and-verification framework. Its cross-domain classification is the specification’s argument, not established consensus across the cited sciences.
