The work

The Word That Became Text is a two-part essay by Johannes Sigil and Rebekah Cranes, designated EA-LOGOS-01. Version 2.3 was deposited on 13 November 2026. It succeeds version 2.2 (#1764), version 2.1 (#1763), version 2.0 (#1761) and version 1.0 (#626), which stay on the record. The essay is the Josephus station of the Historical Logos (#1757), the archive's framework for the Logos as material inscription becoming flesh, and it reads from that framework's standing and from Revelation First (#1755) and the Tenth Muse (#1756). Version 2.3 adds a census of the Slavonic Jewish War from N. A. Meshcherskii's Old Russian (#1760): sixty-six catalogued additions, each scored under Josephus's own account of an earlier version for the peoples beyond the Euphrates (War 1.3, 1.6) and under the consensus account of a medieval translator. Thirty-two are better explained by the earlier version and two by the translator; none of the fifty-one with no Christian subject goes to the translator. The census finds the Slavonic's differences among the kinds of change Josephus makes between the War and the Antiquities, Roman venality the Roman edition could not carry, and in Tiron's cry the Greek reducing a fuller text; it reads the consensus's plainest Gospel quotation, the righteous blood, through Lamentations 4:13 and Jeremiah 26:15; and it names four failed inferences on which the consensus rests. Part I reads the wonder-worker as Philo's Logos, a textual being: the narrator tries Philo's names for it, man, the man after the image, angel, and refuses each, and at that instant writes it as one man; the step from Logos to book runs through Philo's law as Logos (Migr. 130), the law as a living being seen in a mirror of names (De vita contemplativa 78), and the living law whose book is its memorial (De Abrahamo 4–5). The Temple veil, the cosmos the Logos wears, is torn when the Logos written as a man is killed. Part II, in a doctrinal register, reads the Slavonic hedge as a mirror of Catullus 51. The version travels with a repair to the seated Greek Josephus (#1499).
