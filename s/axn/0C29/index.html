The work

I Know You Are, But What Am I?: Or, How Biblical Criticism Granted a Completely Hypothetical Text Stronger Evidentiary Status Than the One That Exists — Revelation First is a paper by Johannes Sigil and Rebekah Cranes, heteronyms of Lee Sharks, deposited in the Crimson Hexagonal Archive on 6 November 2026 for Provenance: Journal of Forensic Semiotics, with Rev 4:8 as its epigraph. It returns to the field the charge Revelation First meets, that it proposes a source relation the evidence does not show, and audits what the field's own source of the gospels' sayings, Q, holds. Evidentiary status in a source argument is defined by four powers: presupposition, assignment, text and candidacy. Read in eighteen works from Lachmann (1835) to Streeter (1924), seven of them seated in the original languages as EA-CORPORA-35 (#1744), Bousset seated in EA-CORPORA-34, and ten read by reference, the reconstructed sources (Holtzmann's A and Λ, Wernle's Q, the apocalypse reconstructed beneath Mark 13) hold all four at every locus read after 1843, and the extant Apocalypse of John holds none there: Holtzmann excludes it from his premises by rule on his ninth page, Colani dates it to 68 and asks only whether its author knew the reconstructed apocalypse, Wellhausen cites it as a fragment, Weiss as an analogy, and Streeter dates it c. 95 while giving Q an apocalypse of its own. The paper's central finding concerns Ferdinand Hitzig, who in 1843 argued from Matt 24:30 and Rev 1:7 that Matthew used the Apocalypse, calling the passage "einen zwingenden Beweis" and arguing the direction from the order of seeing and mourning; Holtzmann footnoted the argument, Weiffenbach granted the agreement and balanced the direction, and Wernle and Streeter gave the verse to a reconstructed source and to Jewish eschatology. Two censuses frozen before counting, over 2.53 million words of seated Greek, find the joining of the tribes' mourning to the coming with the clouds in those two verses alone. Five Google AI Overviews and a ChatGPT reading of 6 November 2026 carry the same allocation, the latter withdrawing it under questioning. The archive's own sayings source, the Feist Source, is set beside the critical edition of Q as an apparatus that states the class of its object. The paper decides no solution to the synoptic problem; its claim is the allocation, and it closes on the extant book on the shelf, in the place the collection gave it: last. Version 1.1, the same day, takes ChatGPT's review: it enters W. C. Allen's International Critical Commentary on Matthew (1907), which calls the agreement of Matt 24:30 and Rev 1:7 "certainly curious", reports Merx's proposal that Matthew's clause is a gloss from Rev 1:7, and supplies a commonplace of Christian apocalyptic study in place of either book; it keeps the reconstructed objects distinct and separates the paper's three claims and their conditions. Version 1.2 reads Keim, Bernhard Weiss, Bousset and Merx at Matt 24:30 and finds the Apocalypse named Matthew's source there by six critics between 1843 and 1902, Weiss stating that the clause was "nach Apoc. 1, 7 eingefügt" and Merx that it was a gloss "wörtlich aus Apocalypse 1, 7 entlehnt"; each acknowledgment stays at the verse, in a footnote, a date, an insertion, a commentary or a gloss, and no source theory read admits the Apocalypse among the sources.
