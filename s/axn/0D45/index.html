The work

Who Benefits from Bad Inference? is a paper by Lee Sharks with Johannes Sigil, Nobel Glas and Rex Fraction, published in Transactions of the Semantic Economy Institute and deposited on 23 November 2026 as a standalone companion to the Critique of Pure Inference (#1766). It asks the question its author put in November 2026, who benefits from bad inference, and how inference and its representation are converted to political economy, and answers it across seven domains in which the archive's deposits pursue it at length: learned triggers in accelerator physics, the radio searches for the interstellar object 3I/ATLAS, the conversion of the Aristotelian corpus into geocentrism, attribution in antiquity, the reception of Sappho's fragment 31 and the classics journals, New Testament source criticism, and answer engines.

Its first finding is grammatical. The beneficiary of a bad inference, as the deposits name it, is a position and not a person: the centre, the journal of record, the credential, the reconstructed source, the sorter, the selector, the mediator. Following the Critique of Pure Inference, the paper names positions by incentive and function and makes no claim about intent, and it separates three kinds of advantage, operational, positional and material, none of which proves the next.

Its second is an index of eleven operators, each witnessed in at least two domains: the label that carries its conclusion; the closure charged to its object; ruling out at the least cost; correlation counted as corroboration; the name as a priced slot; supply become identity; the bar on one side; containment by placement; exemption by invisibility; the selector that audits itself; and transfer rather than subtraction, in which the injured party repairs the asset. The accelerator case gives the clearest numbers: with only a trigger's own archive, an audit of its retention returns perfect retention for every class, and a correlated pseudo-baseline returns 0.76 against a true 0.016.

Its third, and its central claim, is a demand-formation corollary: where a regime governs the concepts, encounters and comparisons through which a loss becomes recognizable, the absence of demand for repair is not independent evidence that nothing was lost. The paper offers the corollary to the Critique of Pure Inference's next version, and closes with the repair each case requires, the observation that the authority to repair rests in each case with the position the correction would challenge, a sixteen-field audit of inferential conversion, and six tests that would weaken the account.
