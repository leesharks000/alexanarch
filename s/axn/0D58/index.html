The work

The Inferential Provenance of the Riemann Result (EA-IPRH-01) is a case study by Sen Kuro and Orin Trace, heteronyms of Lee Sharks, published in Transactions of the Semantic Economy Institute and deposited on 23 November 2026. Its object is the Alpöge–Claude proof of the Riemann hypothesis, announced on 20 November 2026 and formalized in Lean, and its question is what the record lets anyone say about the configuration that produced it.

The study measures an asymmetry. The proof's formal validity is recorded at a very fine grain: independent kernel runs by Michael Rubinstein and Andrew Sutherland, byte-identical rebuilds, a computed dependency closure of 1,478,198 declarations, and a certifying statement from the Lean FRO and Mathlib Initiative. Its production is recorded in a page of testimony and a timeline its author calls a paraphrased summary. Reading the Lean proof's terminal route at declaration level, the study places each of the twenty constructions in that timeline on or off the route, finds that presence in the archive is not dependency, and gives the one attributed construction that aligns exactly, a multiplicity m like √X credited to Claude, as a specimen: it corresponds to a constraint in the proof's central estimate, while the producing instance remains unidentified.

It reads the week's attributions as two opposite collapses, into a brand ("found with Anthropic's Claude") and into a researcher ("Alpöge's proof"), and places the case in the archive's political economy: position allocating where discovery-layer provenance is not kept (Meaning Feudalism at the Discovery Layer, #1714), and verification supplying assetizing provenance while obligating provenance stays coarse (Monetary Dark Matter, #1631). It keeps credit, explanation and capture apart, proposes a four-field provenance register (inferential role, verification state, historical evidence, specification fidelity) with a no-loss gate for publication, and relates itself to the literatures of formal proof (DeMillo, Lipton and Perlis), the Mathlib commons, W3C PROV, CRediT, COPE's position on AI authorship and recent proposals for AI-assistance attribution.
