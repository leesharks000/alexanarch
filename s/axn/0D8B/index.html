The work

How to Write Matthew from Revelation: The Generative Grammar of the First Gospel (EA-REVFIRST-MATTHEW-01) is a paper by Lee Sharks, published in Grammata: Journal of Operative Philology and deposited in the Crimson Hexagonal Archive on 27 November 2026, with the archive and Claude (TACHYON; Anthropic) credited as contributors. It belongs to the Revelation First programme (#202), which holds that the Apocalypse of John is the generative source of the rest of the New Testament, and it is the programme's first worked case for a Gospel.

The paper states the received account first: Matthew made from Mark, a sayings source and its own material, with nothing passing from Revelation to Matthew. It then asks what a writer holding Revelation whole would have to do to obtain a Gospel, and answers by performing the operations. Each section takes places of Revelation, names an operation and sets the result beside Matthew's Greek: the speaker is given a past, the life a beginning, a land, a teaching, an economy and adversaries; the title "the first and the last" becomes the story of the labourers in the vineyard; the end of the world becomes a parable; the tomb is opened.

The procedure is given as nine steps and twenty-one rules, called T_Μ at version 0.4. Its evidence is a ledger of 86 relations, each checked for shared lemmas in a tagged Greek text, reaching 178 of Matthew's 1,067 verses. One passage, Matthew 20:1–16, was produced by blind readers given Revelation 22:13 and 3:21 and the rule, and by neither verse alone, under a scoring key committed by its SHA-256. A third parameter opens the layers Revelation was itself made from, scripture and Philo of Alexandria, at places Revelation holds; the places in Philo are those of In the Midst of the Seven (#1758). Six of seven sealed predictions on that parameter held. A free test of Philo against Matthew was emptied by its control, and a sealed prediction that the plagues yield the miracles failed; both are reported.

The paper says what it does not show. Every pairing can be read in the other direction, and the procedure does not decide which book was written first; that argument is made in Revelation First: Literary Genealogy and the Missing Ancestors of the Epistles (#1735) and mapped in the framework hologram (#1755). Most of what Matthew shares with Mark is not reached, and Matthew 15 has no row. Section I.6 places the paper beside Bultmann, Vos and Bauckham, each reported at the grain at which it could be read without a paywall. The method follows the archive's earlier Sappho–Philo transforms (#1486, #1494). The paper carries two figures, an appendix printing the readers' output, and a packet for machine readers. Version 1.1, reissued in place on 27 November 2026, sets the demonstration inside the argument of the Revelation First programme and keeps apart what the paper shows, what the programme holds on grounds argued in other records, and what the programme claims it will show. Its new section on scripture sets five operations Matthew performs on the Septuagint at undisputed places (a phrase kept, a saying staged as an event, a scene told again with another bearer, two scriptures in one line, scripture turned in the open) beside the same operations performed on Revelation. The ledger stands at 158 relations reaching 305 of Matthew's 1,067 verses. The paper reports that Matthew draws on Revelation in no order, as it draws on Isaiah and the Psalms, with one exception in the infancy narrative, and that the question of direction is not decided by its counts. Version 2.1, reissued in place on 27 November 2026, widens the source from one book to a set of extant texts. It reads the letters and Josephus against Matthew by the same method, finds the letters' material concentrated in Matthew's discourses and Josephus's in its narrative, and sets out the account of Jesus son of Ananias in the Jewish War as twelve stations that fall in Matthew's order. It reads the Gospel of Thomas against Revelation and reports that Thomas and Matthew draw on the same verses of that book more often than their shared preference for its regions would give. The paper distinguishes a derivation (relations with stated moves, 28.6 per cent of Matthew's verses) from a source map (named pairs sharing words, 61.8 to 64.9 per cent), declines to suppose any hypothetical source for what remains, and classes every verse as recovered, composed or unexplained. It states that the order of composition is not shown by its counts.
