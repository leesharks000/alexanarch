The work

Revelation First: Literary Genealogy and the Missing Ancestors of the Epistles is an article by Johannes Sigil and Damascus Dancings, heteronyms of Lee Sharks, published in Provenance: Journal of Forensic Semiotics and deposited in the Crimson Hexagonal Archive on 3 November 2026. It proposes a literary-genealogical test for the order of the New Testament's documents: a literature proposed as earliest should show recoverable ancestry, while later forms show transformations of it. On that test the received sequence is anomalous, since the Pauline letters require a reconstructed 'pre-Pauline' layer while Revelation's images announce their antecedents in the prophetic scriptures. The article separates absolute, corpus and generative priority, audits the chronological gate set by the Nero cipher and the Gallio inscription, and reports two executed results: Revelation 1–3 as the earliest extant closed seven-church corpus, read by the Muratorian Fragment as the order Paul followed, and a machine census finding the phrase 'crown of life' (στέφανος τῆς ζωῆς) only in Revelation 2.10 and James 1.12. It distinguishes Revelation First from the older argument for an early date of Revelation and states the conditions under which each claim would fail. Version 8 (10 November 2026) adds the commission, the address of each proclamation to a messenger and the command to write and send, as an arrow toward the letters to churches whose direction is left open, and records the figure in the midst of the seven lampstands, with Zechariah's and Philo's lampstand, as an ancestry case, drawing on In the Midst of the Seven (#1758). Version 9 (11 November 2026) adds, from the second version of In the Midst of the Seven, Zechariah's vision of Joshua the high priest and Philo's organization of the lampstand as antecedents of the figure in the midst, and an arrow toward Hebrews 4.8–14, left unrun.
