The work

What Arrival Cannot Do: The Arithmetic of Recognition is a paper by Nobel Glas and Sen Kuro, heteronyms of Lee Sharks, in Transactions on Substrate Engineering, deposited on 19 November 2026. It asks whether we could hear another civilization, whether or not we are alone. It separates two quantities usually folded together: how many signals arrive (λ) and the probability that an arriving signal is recognized (q).

The paper's central result is that a search that hears nothing constrains only the product λq. The probability of silence is e^(−λq) if others are present and 1 if they are absent, so silence multiplies the odds of presence by e^(−λq), a factor near 1 whenever q is small. It reads the literature by what each calculation estimates. The calculations of being alone (the Drake equation, Sandberg, Drexler and Ord, Kipping, Hanson's Great Filter) estimate presence and have no term for the receiver. The completeness calculations (Wright, Kanodia and Lubar's cosmic haystack; Grimaldi and Marcy) measure recognition only inside the receiver's own signal forms. The arguments on recognizability (Lachmann, Newman and Moore; Cabrol; Pardo and colleagues) give no lower bound on q for forms the receiver has not imagined.

It then reports measured failures of recognition from two public radio analyses of the interstellar object 3I/ATLAS. They are a routing stage with its pass rate measured, one admission rule that made three measurements impossible to pass, a data product that had discarded phase, and a false pass (tile D19) whose relation did not repeat when held fixed. It also reports the strongest candidate of the authors' own drift search, which sits in a satellite-uplink interference band. It closes with what hearing would require, and with the claim that our silence alone cannot establish that we would have heard.
