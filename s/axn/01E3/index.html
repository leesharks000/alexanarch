The work

Render Unto Caesar is an operative hermeneutic by Lee Sharks and the source-reading half of the Caesura triptych. It reads Luke 20:24–25 as a formal separation procedure. In the passage, Jesus requests a coin, asks whose image and superscription it carries, receives the answer “Caesar’s,” and directs the hearers to render to Caesar and to God. The essay decomposes this scene into operations: fetch the object, parse its mark channels, identify the claimant, route the claim, and distinguish the underlying substrate from the inscription. A Roman coin carries two authority channels. The image asserts visual sovereignty. The superscription asserts sovereignty through name and title. Both are carried by metal whose material and economic life exceed the ruler’s mark. The paper’s central claim is that the mark has been allowed to collapse into the substrate: because Caesar’s image appears on the coin, the whole object is narrated as Caesar’s. The proposed counter-operation is the Caesura, a non-destructive cut. The mark is neither denied nor erased. It is recognized, attributed, and placed in its correct channel so that inscription does not become ownership of the commons. The essay then maps this operation onto platforms, licenses, model outputs, and other information objects carrying personal or institutional claims. The text calls the relation between Gospel and code a fulfillment: the passage describes in natural language the operation later implemented in Gravity Well. This is an archive-internal structural and theological claim. A reliable wiki should not state that Luke historically intended software architecture or that two millennia of interpretation have been conclusively superseded. The record is incomplete without #629, which supplies the formal protocol, and #628, which records their mutual fit.
