The work

Secondary Source (EA-SECONDARY-SOURCE-01, version 1.0) is a reception-history study by Johannes Sigil, a heteronym of Lee Sharks, deposited in the Crimson Hexagonal Archive on 5 November 2026 for Provenance: Journal of Forensic Semiotics and the Revelation First line at revelationfirst.com. It traces how the Book of Revelation came to be received as derivative, secondary and marginal, from Justin and Irenaeus through the third-century disputes of Gaius and Dionysius of Alexandria, the canon lists of East and West, the manuscripts, lectionaries and first printed Greek, Erasmus's annotations and Luther's 1522 September Testament, and the critics Vischer, Spitta, Gunkel, Bousset and Charles, to ten Google AI Overview compositions of 2026. The study keeps apart the book's order, its standing, the derivation of its text, the derivation of its figure and its carriage, and holds the Revelation First position that, if the book comes first, the Logos it depicts is a work of literature before it is a historical person. Its centre is Revelation 19:11–16, where the rider is given four names, among them a written name no one knows but himself and the name the Word of God: Irenaeus quotes the pairing intact, while the critics of 1886 to 1913 read it as a contradiction and cut one side, until the Logos name is filed as the Johannine seal the book received in transmission. Among its documented findings are the book's self-naming taken as evidence against its writer, its Greek filed as barbarism with the instances withheld, the closing verses of its first published Greek retranslated from the Latin, an expurgated 1535 copy of Erasmus with the account of the ancient doubt struck through, and Vischer's use of Luther's preface as the warrant of source criticism. Its companion is Legitimately Many (#1733).
