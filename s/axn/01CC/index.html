The work

The Constraint That Generates is a New Human Canon declaration by Lee Sharks and Johannes Sigil, dated March 30, 2026. It places selected works of Raymond Queneau, Oulipo, Georges Perec, and Italo Calvino into the Crimson Hexagonal Archive’s canon of structures that generate through constraint.

The declaration begins from Queneau’s Cent Mille Milliards de Poèmes, a physical combinatorial book made from ten sonnets whose lines can be recombined into an enormous but rule-governed possibility space. The work reads Queneau’s authorship as residing primarily in the machine of combination: the finite system that makes each possible poem lawful. Oulipian constraint is therefore treated not as deprivation but as an engine.

The mantle sequence extends through Perec and Calvino. Perec represents constraint becoming invisible but load-bearing: a missing letter or a mathematically governed itinerary organizes the whole work whether or not the reader consciously tracks the rule. Calvino represents self-reference and interruption becoming structural rather than decorative. These predecessors are described as solving problems the archive later encountered under automated inference.

The declaration then maps those precedents onto four Hexagonal objects. The Space Ark is a Queneau-type generator and portable reconstruction machine. Sémantique Potentielle transfers potential literature into potential terminology through governed seed terms and operations. The Hexagonal Lexical Engine functions as a Perecian hidden constraint preserving stable denotation. The Assembly Chorus distributes authorship across a process whose method, rather than a single voice, governs the result.

The text’s central proposition is that a finite, explicit rule set can govern an effectively unbounded field of generation while preserving provenance and replayability. Its “canonization” is performative inside the New Human and Crimson Hexagonal systems. The article should therefore distinguish the declaration’s internal authority from external literary-historical consensus.
