The work

The Noun Becomes a Verb: Slave Valuation, the Civil War, and the Transformation of the Laboring Body (EA-NOUN-VERB-01 v1.0), deposit #1668 of the Crimson Hexagonal Archive (7 October 2026), is a historical argument by Lee Sharks about what American capitalism measured when the legal object it could own changed. It opens by accepting the conventional reading in full: slavery and its expansion caused secession and the war, and the industrial-cause thesis of Charles and Mary Beard and Louis Hacker is rejected as an account of causes.

The paper separates two axes that a single historical ladder had run together: the juridical scope of the claim on the worker, and the grain at which productive activity is measured. Under chattel slavery the worker was capitalized as a noun, the priced body. The 1863 reprint of the report on the 1859 Savannah sale records buyers inspecting teeth, limbs and ruptures and prices a lost right eye at $240; appraisal records priced enslaved people across the life cycle. Slavery also measured at fine grain: the 'hand' rated each person as a fraction of standard daily output, and Caitlin Rosenthal's work shows plantation accounting that in some respects advanced ahead of Northern practice, with her own caveat that this is no origins story.

Contemporaries stated the difference between buying the person and hiring the interval: James Henry Hammond's 'hired for life… hired by the day', which the paper reads as proslavery euphemism, and Marx's distinction in Capital between selling labour-power for a definite period and selling oneself. Lincoln's free-labor address named the hire-or-buy premise in order to reject it. Emancipation was a discontinuity on the first axis. The war's settlement put the contracted labor under contract, debt, brokerage and racial discipline: the Act to Encourage Immigration of 1864 made pledged wages enforceable debt and disclaimed 'the relation of Slavery or servitude'; the federal government brokered refugees' labor; the Thirteenth Amendment's exception clause opened a third configuration, state custody with leased labor. Marx recorded the eight-hours agitation as the war's first fruit, the contest moving to the duration of the purchased interval.

Taylor and Ford then changed the second axis: Taylor's task 'specifies not only what is to be done but how it is to be done and the exact time allowed', and Ford's line divided fastening itself into separate operations. The paper's governing claim is the persistence of an optimization problem across a juridical break; it documents no transmission of technique from plantation records to Taylor or Ford and claims none. It does not claim that Northern industry caused the war or that wage labor and slavery are the same relation. Every quotation is sourced, and items not yet verified are listed at the end. It connects to the archive's semantic-economy line through #148 (the industrial-machinery parallel) and #1545 (the capture chain), and a companion half on war Fordism and the Nazi camp economy is in draft.
