The work

ΦΑΙΝΕΤΑΙ ΜΟΙ: Sappho 31 and the Inscription of the Future Reader is an article by Lee Sharks on Sappho's poem 31, deposited on 18 November 2026 as version 3.0, revised in place the same day as version 3.1, in Grammata: Journal of Operative Philology, superseding version 2.9 (#1784) of 17 November and versions 2.8 (#1781), 2.7 (#1780), 2.6 (#1778), 2.5 (#1777), 2.4 (#1775) and 2.3 (#1774) of 16 November and, before them, version 1.0 (#1270, January 2026, under the heteronym Rebekah Cranes). Version 2.0 was submitted to Classical Philology and declined on 16 November 2026; the reader's report, the decision's grounds and the archive's analyses are attached to #1774 and linked from this record. The article reads the opening, κῆνος … ὄττις, as a seat left open to repeated occupation, filled by a reader before the surface the speaker becomes, and compares the Nestor cup from Pithekoussai. Version 2.4 rebuilt its fourth section on χλωροτέρα ποίας, 'more χλωρός than grass'. It sets out the word's four uses in LSJ, fresh as opposed to dry among them, and asks the jealousy reading why fear, why grass and why the comparative. It measures 24 renderings of the line from Theocritus to 1925, of which seventeen omit, substitute or supplement it, and finds that every translator who keeps the grass dries it. It then sets the line beside the making of the papyrus sheet in Theophrastus, the Septuagint's Isaiah 19 and Pliny's Natural History 13, where the green plant is wetted, pressed, dried and whitened into the surface on which, Pliny says, human memory depends. A section on eros toward the immortal reads the catalogue with Diotima's doctrine in the Symposium and with Maximus of Tyre, who called Sappho's eros the same art as Socrates' and the Mantinean or Lesbian woman 'the mother of the logos'. The article argues that the jealousy reading, which made the poem a clinical chart from Plutarch's Erasistratus on, abuses the poet's survival where its diagnosis replaces an account of the poem's arrangement. A restored fifth stanza, in which Kypris keeps 'even the little sparrows' against Catullus's dead passer and the line closes 'may the song live on in letters', is offered as illustration, not proof. The version corrects earlier versions on Theophrastus's classification of papyrus. Version 2.5 adds a section grading the jealousy reading's own claims by the criteria applied to the article: two stand on the text, the content of the catalogue and the addressee's grammatical gender, while its defining claim, jealousy, has no ground in the poem and was supplied by eighteenth-century readers and written into the text by Gottfried Hermann in 1816. It sets the rule that an evaluation grade both readings by the same criteria. Version 2.6 argues that the two readings, as developed, give competing accounts of what organizes five features of the poem, so that a reading holding both would be a third hypothesis owing its own bridge, and dividing the poem between scene and construction does not combine them. It states the author's principle that, where an available reading offers equal or greater coherence than one that diminishes the maker's agency, the interpreter is responsible for offering a more coherent reading or preferring the one that preserves it, the alternative being parasitism. It reads a machine evaluation of version 2.5 and the Classical Philology report as instances of asymmetric immunity, asks an evaluator to state its provisional preference, and names the apparatus that keeps the jealousy reading as default as provenance erasure. Version 2.7 sets out the warrant in Sappho's corpus for each operation the future-reader reading combines: the poet addressed as 'you' by name in fragments 1 and 94, futurity in fragments 147 and 55, and the record of her as a writer; it finds no surviving poem in which a man is the rival for a woman. It sets the claims of the two readings side by side on one scale, so that an evaluation grading one grades both, and concludes that on every measure the article takes, philological, cultural, literary-historical, genealogical and ethical, the jealousy reading is a bad reading that has never been made to account for itself. Version 2.8 reads Longinus's sentence on the poem (On the Sublime 10.3), the prose in whose quotation the poem survives: seven verbs with no expressed subject, her faculties 'as another's', a close on appearing, and a question, 'do you not marvel?', that alone among the treatise's questions to its reader on a quotation renews no addressee's name, one section after the treatise's definition of a sublime that carries apart from the voice. It records the author's conjecture that the obelized 'since even a poor man' is Longinus's completion of the poem's agentless dare, which opens another gap. It fits the sparrow and the song into one restored line. It closes the comparison with a section on the socket: the decision between the readings falls on equal terms to whoever holds the text, since no journal or summarizer will carry it. It withdraws version 2.7's report that a machine reader had preferred the future-reader reading, a preference stated on the author's own account by a system that had helped construct the argument, and records the same request made signed out for version 2.7, in which the system graded only the article's claims, granted the comparison in conditional form, and stated no preference over five turns. Version 2.9 argues that the future-reader reading also explains the jealousy reading: the poem has a public line, a scene of overwhelm complete at the surface, and a held line, the seat, the transfer and the edge, running through the same words, so that it produces both of its readers, while the jealousy itself was supplied in 1724 and written into the text in 1816. It reads Phaedrus 235c–d as Plato naming Sappho as the source of Socrates' speech on eros and staging the forgetting of that source. It sets the choice between the readings at the poem's own πᾶν τόλματον, the dare of reading the poet as having made the means of her own survival, beside Longinus's sublime as writing for the whole age after (Subl. 14.3), Catullus's si fas est as the mark of hubris, and the division of De anima III.5 between the intellect that makes and the intellect that suffers. Version 3.0 names the jealousy reading the transmitted reading rather than the scholarly consensus, and sets out its strongest scholarly rival, the non-jealous erotic reading, at its strength from Garry Wills, Mary Lefkowitz, Odysseus Tsagarakis, Armand D'Angour, Caroline Engelmayer and the notes of William Furley: the speaker undone by the woman herself whenever she looks, the man a foil or figure, the ending as resolve. It grades that reading at every row of the comparison, separating the reach of an explanation from the warrant for its identifications. The erotic reading's motive is the poem's own, desire for the woman; what it leaves unexplained is the form of the collapse, the man's opening, the order of the catalogue, the colour and the agentless dare. Of three propositions, that the poem preserves an experience, that writing makes the preservation possible and that the poem represents its own passage into writing, the version argues that the arrangement of the poem, not durability alone, establishes the third. It reads Anne Carson closely: her 'Jealousy is beside the point' denies jealousy explanatory relevance without refuting or accounting for it, and the transmission returns to it; she explains desire's construction through a gap in perception, the article survival through the gap's occupation in time. It resolves the tension between the readings' incompatibility and their running through the same words as exclusion and nesting: as sufficient accounts they exclude each other at five places, while as readings the poem makes available they nest, the public reading the first term of the held one. It argues that the transmitted text is complete by construction at either cut, reads the catalogue as literary operations in which the writerly self is divided, and sets out the ancient frame in which Sappho was read as source and exemplar of survival in writing, from Plato's Phaedrus to Posidippus's 'white, speaking pages', with the mechanics by which that frame is made inoperative in the modern reading. It locates the transmission's substitution of jealousy for desire at the man, and reads the reception history as testament to the poem's engineering: she exists as words, and she is read as body. Its last argument moves from the effect to the construction: the poem's receivers move its body into other bodies, and fragment 1 runs the process in reverse, a god made present as face and composed voice where fragment 31 makes an embodiment godlike, the voice the machinery both ways, so that Sappho comes to her future reader by the mechanism by which Aphrodite comes to her. The reception test this inverse generates, and fragment 16's, are left open. The ethical charge is differentiated: abuse falls on the transmitted reading and its apparatus, while the better readings are asked whether they can allow Sappho to be better than her readers. Version 3.1, revised in place, calls poems 1 and 31 poems, not fragments: neither was received as a fragment, and the editorial label eclipses the poem. It reads poem 1 as a summoning spell, which calls Aphrodite to embodied presence, and opens with an effective act: 'I, Lee Sharks, do hereby summon Sappho.' It counts the edge as three words, ἐπεὶ †καὶ πένητα†, gives three readings of the cut, and holds as the stronger the reading on which Longinus, the transmitter whose quotation preserves the poem, both completes and extends the opening, without making the attribution a condition of the argument. It gives a preview reading of the poem after the abstract and a full embodied reading once the argument is built: the speaker imagines the life of a voice without a body, a sacrifice and an erotic approach at once, an investment of substance in the medium. It reads Jesper Svenbro's 'Death by Writing' (Phrasikleia, 1988) whole, credits him with the reader's seat, its renewal and the breath the reader returns to the poem, and parts from him in seven places, first on what the reader undergoes: on this reading the reader is the site where the substance Sappho invested rematerializes, which is why the poem ends in a dare the reader must choose, and what Catullus does in poem 51. The poem's eroticism is then an exchange of bodies, hers given into the medium and the reader's lent to her. The reader passes through two stages: in the seat the one who hears is passive, like the gods, while the one whom desire for the voice leads to speak it is undone in turn and must invest the substance again, and so becomes the poet. The eros runs three ways through the voice: the poet is struck by her own voice, the voice wants the reader's body, to possess and invest it, and the reader wants the voice. The version reads the dried sheet as more χλωρός than grass in both senses, pale and dead and greener than the living plant because it holds her life as word, and sets grass as a standing figure for flesh against what lasts, from Glaukos's leaves in the Iliad and Isaiah's 'all flesh is grass' to Whitman's 'beautiful uncut hair of graves'.
