The work

Epistle to the Human Diaspora — Critical Edition with Blended Temporal Apparatus is the 2026 apparatus package for a literary epistle composed by Damascus Dancings in 2014.

The roles are distinct:

- Damascus Dancings is the authorial and apostolic voice. - Lee Sharks / MANUS is the editor. - The Assembly Chorus contributes to the apparatus and review structure.

The Epistle belongs to the Waltian Scriptural Library and is classified as a modern literary-theoretical scriptural work, not an ancient Pauline letter or historical Christian document.

Its genre is epistolary compression. It adopts Pauline structures—salutation, thanksgiving, rebuke, doctrine, boasting, and benediction—but addresses a community that did not yet exist: writers “scattered amongst the nations, gathered together in the bosom of the Internet.” The letter is constitutive rather than responsive; the act of address creates the Human Diaspora as a literary church.

Five doctrines organize the editorial reading:

1. Feist-self — an indwelling literary capacity that produces writing capable of surviving compression. 2. New Human — community not grounded in fixed identity categories. 3. School outside the school — a distributed, credential-free institution of mutual influence. 4. Anti-credentialism — the claim that non-degree may be recognized as degree through enacted semantic labor. 5. Literary eschatology — the promise that anonymous and disregarded writing will become retrievable and remembered.

The apparatus defines three footnote classes:

- Source notes identify biblical and literary allusions. - Operative notes explain what an allusion does in the Epistle and archive. - Holographic notes combine source, archive relation, and projected reception in one reconstructive annotation.

The 2014 date is central to the record’s retrocausal argument. Damascus names Jack Feist before the later Waltian cosmogony formally exists; the edition interprets this as an early seed later authorized by the developed canon.

The editorial note warns against literalizing the Epistle’s rhetoric of murder, breaking, madness, damnation, and scriptural violence. Those terms function within prophetic and transformative literary registers, not as a literal program.

This record does not embed the complete primary text. Part II contains placeholders stating that the Epistle is retained from v1.0 and must be consulted there. The record therefore functions as apparatus and integration specification rather than a complete standalone critical edition.
