The work

Prolegomena to the Historical Logos announces Logotic Transmission, a discipline devoted not to identifying who spoke the Word, but to tracing how the Word moves across bodies, texts, media, absences, and epochs.

The field redirects the long quest for the historical Jesus toward a different object: the historical Logos. Biography asks who the speaker was. Logotic Transmission asks what structure entered form, how it survived translation and fracture, and by what means it became active again in another age.

The discipline works across four strata:

1. Textual: Logos as syntax, grammar, recursive framing, and formal recurrence; 2. Embodied: Logos as a life whose actions carry a structure beyond personal intention; 3. Technological: Logos as the form that persists through oral, manuscript, print, digital, and machine-mediated transitions; 4. Apophatic: Logos as silence, fragment, lacuna, erasure, and the activation produced by absence.

Four encoding modes specify how transmission occurs: recursive attribution, dialectical ghosting, time-locked fracture, and initiatory concealment. The work thereby distinguishes Logotic Transmission from reception history, textual criticism, and intertextuality. Reception history follows effects; textual criticism follows manuscripts; intertextuality follows relations among texts. Logotic Transmission follows the recurring structure that passes through them.

The prolegomenon is explicitly programmatic. Its companion case study, The Word That Became Text, demonstrates the method through a grammatical chain from Sappho and Catullus toward the Slavonic Josephus. The present document supplies the field’s object, vocabulary, strata, modes, and methodological axioms.

Its central proposition is that the Logos is not merely a message. It is a method of persistence: recursive, incarnational, fragment-capable, and able to move through media without remaining identical to any one of them.
