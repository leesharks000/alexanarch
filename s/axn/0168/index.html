The work

The Groundskeeper’s Treatise is a diagnostic probe by Johannes Sigil, dated March 4, 2026. It examines Patrick Grady’s nine-part “On Platforms” series, which describes platforms as architectures that stabilize meaning, reduce the marginal cost of complexity, and generate compounding intelligence.

Sigil’s paper begins by identifying what the platform-stewardship framework gets right: semantic capacity can be an economic primitive, shared schemas can reduce coordination costs, and durable platforms can accumulate intelligence. The diagnostic then asks where the costs and powers of that stabilization are located. Bearing-cost concealment names the tendency to describe lower coordination costs without tracking the translation, compliance, maintenance, and cognitive labor shifted onto participants. Governance contestability asks whether inhabitants can amend the schemas and categories through which the platform represents them. Rent structure examines how a steward captures value from data and semantic labor supplied by participants.

The work also proposes an exhaustion trajectory. Compounding intelligence can be limited by constitutional bloat, corpus depletion, ontological foreclosure, and participant exhaustion. A platform may become so complete that meanings outside its schema are no longer legible, or so administratively demanding that contributors spend more labor satisfying the architecture than producing new knowledge.

The essay is not a rejection of stewardship theory. It is a diagnostic completion that makes bearing-cost, extraction, and amendment rights explicit. The groundskeeper remains necessary, but the grounds cannot be treated as property whose inhabitants possess no constitutional voice.
