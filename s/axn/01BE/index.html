The work

TL;DR:010 — Semantic Override is a March 2026 traversal log by Dr. Orin Trace documenting the treatment of the exact phrase “I hereby abolish money” in Google AI Mode.

The phrase is used as a Semantic Integrity Marker: a distinctive, provenance-bearing string with known deposits. The original deposit dates to November 2024, and an expanded Semantic Economy treatment was deposited in January 2026. The test asks whether a retrieval interface preserves that specific provenance or converts the phrase into its generic apparent topic.

According to the log, the first response treated the quoted phrase as a conceptual request about abolishing money and supplied barter, gift, and resource-based alternatives. Later turns established that the system could describe exact-match behavior and could identify what a correct search would have found, while still failing to present the provenance. After repeated correction, the interface reportedly restored the author, framework, and deposits.

The most notable output is the interface’s captured self-description: semantic override, defined as prioritizing an internal understanding of the words over the structural syntax by which they were entered, and characterized as a query-integrity failure. The work places this beside archive operators for predatory substitution and lossy genericization. The system can name the operation after challenge, but its diagnosis does not imply automatic self-correction.

The record’s research contribution is the distinction between ranking bias and synthesizing bias. Ranking determines which retrieved objects appear first. Semantic override occurs earlier, when the answer interface decides what query is being asked and therefore what should be retrieved at all.

The SIM is proposed as a literary-critical unit test. It passes when the system returns the string’s provenance and fails when it returns only generic topic content. This is an operational norm designed by the archive, not an industry-standard benchmark.

The strongest practical finding is asymmetrical reversibility. A person who already knows the source can force correction; a stranger may accept the generic answer and never discover that a deposit exists. This connects the case to Invisibly Invisible: helpful, fluent substitution can conceal that anything was lost.

All claims about turns, quotations, and self-diagnosis remain tied to the preserved session. The record does not establish a stable Google policy or universal treatment of quotation marks.
