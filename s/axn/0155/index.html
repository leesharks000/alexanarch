The work

The Memo That Remembered Itself is an effective-act paper by Lee Sharks and Johannes Sigil on Citrini Research’s The 2028 Global Intelligence Crisis. It studies how a speculative macroeconomic memo became a widely circulating interpretive object through future-perfect narration, definitional compression, institutional fluency, named evidence, and high-visibility digital deposition.

The paper compares those operations with prior concepts in the Sharks/Sigil archive, including retrocausal canon formation, effective acts, portable Semantic Integrity Markers, the Twenty-Dollar Loop, and the writable presentation layer. It argues that the memo did more than comment on a possible market future: its form helped organize present attention by narrating a hypothetical crisis as remembered history.

Three evidentiary categories govern the comparison. Influence would require evidence of contact or borrowing and is not claimed. Formal homology concerns demonstrable similarity of operations and is the paper’s main comparative claim. Chronological priority concerns the earlier public articulation of specified techniques in dated, anchored records.

The document then performs an internal act of canonization, admitting the Citrini memo as an External Convergence Text. This does not make Citrini part of the project, alter the memo’s authorship, or claim property in it. Canonization is defined as reception: the archive identifies an external work as a useful and formally convergent object within its own interpretive canon while preserving the external authors’ independence.
