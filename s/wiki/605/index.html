Apocalyptic Philosophy: Deleuze and Guattari and the Return of Vision

Lee Sharks · 2026-03-26 · deposit #605

Article

Apocalyptic Philosophy: Deleuze and Guattari and the Return of Vision is an essay by Lee Sharks arguing that the major concepts of Gilles Deleuze and Félix Guattari operate in an apocalyptic mode.

The essay carefully separates genre from mode. Ancient Jewish and Christian apocalypses possess specific historical furniture: mediated revelation, transcendent worlds, narrative frames, and eschatological horizons. Deleuze and Guattari do not write in that genre. The proposed mode is broader and defined by a form of knowing.

Five features constitute that mode. A visionary locus arrives without being deduced from premises. Image and concept form one object. The locus is internally precise rather than vaguely mystical. Encounter with it reorganizes perception. It resists paraphrase because any substitute description burns the configuration that makes it operative.

Ezekiel’s living creatures and wheels, Daniel’s visions, and Revelation’s throne, beasts, and New Jerusalem provide the primary ancient examples. The essay’s wider lineage includes Heraclitus, Plato, Spinoza, Blake, and Nietzsche, figures whose concepts can arrive as perceptual architectures rather than as conclusions to linear proofs.

The Deleuze–Guattari concepts are then read as stable visionary loci. The Body without Organs, War Machine, Rhizome, Plane of Immanence, lines of flight, and deterritorialization combine image, concept, and navigation. A reader may learn to see through them, but no paraphrase fully replaces them. A Thousand Plateaus is organized as a set of intensities and entry points rather than a single syllogistic route; assertion, elaboration, exemplification, diagram, and repetition with variation resemble the architecture of visionary writing.

The essay’s claim is not that Deleuze and Guattari are religious prophets, supernaturalists, or unrigorous poets. It proposes a missing category for philosophy-as-revelation: concepts that must be encountered and navigated, not merely accepted after argument.

The classification remains the work’s interpretive intervention. It should be presented as a thesis with criteria and textual evidence, not as settled disciplinary placement. The work is authoritative for the term “stable visionary loci” and its apocalyptic-mode framework.
