Article

The Colony Enters the Factory: War Fordism, Racial Labor Hierarchy, and the Industrialization of Conquest (EA-NOUN-VERB-02 v1.0), deposit #1669 of the Crimson Hexagonal Archive (7 October 2026), is the second half of a historical argument by Lee Sharks whose first half is The Noun Becomes a Verb (#1668). It opens by accepting the conventional reading: the Nazi camps were founded in 1933 as instruments of political terror; forced labor in the Reich was a vast system graded by nationality and race; historians widely accept the colonial self-understanding of the war in the East, contest a direct continuity from the Herero and Nama genocide in German South-West Africa, and reject a colonial or economic cause of the Holocaust.

Germany was an advanced industrial power constrained by scale, for which labor became a binding wartime constraint; Ulrich Herbert judges that production would have collapsed in 1942 at the latest without foreign workers. Lebensraum was conceived as continental colonization, through an institution of German inner colonization older than Nazism that Robert Nelson traces to the Prussian Settlement Commission, and the empire pursued for land and racial reordering also became a mechanism for supplying labor: territory moved outward and labor moved inward. Rüdiger Hachtmann's war Fordism decomposed work into simplified operations while the racial state decomposed persons into graded classes of wage, ration and segregation; his aptitude tests show the instruments of measurement crossing the racial grade.

From the second half of 1942 the camp was attached to the factory, a form the paper calls campification, held at Hachtmann's scale: a minority of camp prisoners in industrial production from late 1942 to mid-1944, with construction for underground relocation in the war's final months. At the factory camps the prisoner's working day was allocated by the state for a daily fee and replenished by conquest and deportation, a third relation beside the owned person and the contracted interval of the first half. Racial-juridical classification acted as a switch that set the permissible range of coercion under which the industrial operation was extracted.

The paper does not reduce the Holocaust to labor economics. With Peter Hayes and Christopher Browning it holds that extermination and exploitation sometimes reinforced and sometimes destroyed each other's objects, that labor deferred the murder for some only for a time, and that the murder of the Jews of Europe is larger than the labor history it tells. It treats the colonial camps of 1900 as a shared repertoire, following Alan Kramer's caution against a straight genealogy, and closes on Arendt's continental imperialism: the colony entered the factory.
