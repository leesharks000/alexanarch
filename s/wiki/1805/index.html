From the Position of the Rejected: Aristotle's Politics Read from Where It Is Written

Sharks, Lee · 2026-11-23 · deposit #1805
AXN:0D5C.GENERATIVE.🟡🔄🕊️🕛🔚🔵

Article

From the Position of the Rejected: Aristotle's Politics Read from Where It Is Written (EA-REJECTED-01) is an essay by Lee Sharks, deposited in the Crimson Hexagonal Archive on 23 November 2026. It reads Aristotle's Politics from the position its first definition names: the one outside the city. Aristotle lived in Athens as a metic, a resident who was never a citizen, and the essay's observation is that the book writes that position into the definition the whole work rests on. The reading began, as the essay records verbatim, with the author's hearing of Aristotle's voice while reading the Politics: 'a damaged voice, a militant Asperger's comedian, a structure of mind so recursive, nested, and unheard, that it stunned me', and the sentence 'the entire edifice forgets, that the entire corpus is written from the announced position of the rejected.'

The sentence usually quoted as 'man is by nature a political animal' (Politics I.2, 1253a1–7) continues: the cityless by nature, and not by chance, is either base or more than human. The essay reads three positions in it for the one outside the city, beast, god and chance, and observes that they are not symmetrical. Beast and god are the judgment on the cityless by nature; chance is named so as to be set outside that judgment and is given no term of its own. The sentence does not say which is the writer's. It asks whether the one outside is outside by nature or by chance, and holds all three in suspension.

Reading the eight books in the Greek, the essay follows the position through the citizen defined against the metic who shares only through a patron (III.1), the honourless citizen who is 'like a metic' and whose condition is hidden to deceive (III.5), the man too great for the city to keep or cast out (III.13, III.17), the school on the tyrant's list of things to stop (V.11), and the stranger's life released from the political community (VII.2). At the defence of natural slavery (I.6, 1255a25–30) it finds the book both exposing and performing one operation, stated by the author as 'politics makes what is by chance appear to be what is by nature', and it places this beside Schofield and Kahn's dispute over whether the theory is ideology. Plutarch's account of Philip's razing and restoration of Stagira is read as a fact about Aristotle's polis, not his person.

The essay names the reception's operation as truncation: the first clause is kept and the sentence cut at the comma, so that reference works such as Wikipedia and the Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy state the political-animal thesis and the author's resident-alien status on one page without the clause that joins them. It differentiates its reading from Whitehead's studies of Aristotle's metic status, Kasimis's work on the metic in Athenian political theory, and Roochnik, Dietz and Rancière, and closes by setting Sappho 31's three positions, the dissolving body, the godly receiver and the contingent voice, beside Aristotle's, as the Aristotle station of a chain the archive has read through Sappho (#1785) and Plato (#1791).
