The Inaugural Ark: Visual Compression and the Architecture of Recovery in Ichabod Spellings' Contribution to Paper Roses — Founding Document of Compression Studies

Johannes Sigil; Ichabod Spellings as subject/visual-work author · 2026-03-14 · deposit #29

Article

The Inaugural Ark is a scholarly article by Johannes Sigil and the founding document of Compression Studies within the archive. Ichabod Spellings is the subject of the article and the author of the visual compression being analyzed; those roles should not be collapsed into co-authorship of the critical essay.

The case study concerns a handwritten manuscript titled What Was Lost. The original object was destroyed, but Spellings had photographed and arranged its pages into ten thumbnail grids later published in DIAGRAM 13.5 as part of Paper Roses. At thumbnail scale, individual sentences become largely unreadable while page sequence, handwriting density, collage, images, margins, and compositional rhythm remain visible.

The paper calls this architectural compression: compression that preserves the formal logic of an object while changing scale, medium, and mode of access. The plates precede the abstract so the reader encounters the compressed architecture before receiving its theory.

Compression Studies is proposed as a field joining problems that are ordinarily divided among information theory, textual scholarship, media philosophy, digital humanities, platform studies, model training, archival preservation, and contract. Its diagnostic question is not simply how much information was removed, but what invariant was preserved, what was lost, whether recovery remains possible, who controls the compression, and who benefits from it.

The article supplies a six-type typology, methods and evidence protocol, and founding propositions. Its worked demonstration follows seven compression layers from the manuscript through photographic grids, publication, archival reconstruction, theoretical formalization, the Space Ark, and smaller diagnostic lenses. The inaugural claim is that compression may carry an object, transform it, or kill it—and that these outcomes require a common analytic vocabulary.
