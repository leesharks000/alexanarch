The Thinkery and the Lyceum: the Clouds and the Peripatetic corpus — a groundwork note on relations, withholding readings (v1.1)

Sharks, Lee · 2026-11-20 · deposit #1794

Article

The Thinkery and the Lyceum is a groundwork note by Lee Sharks, published in Grammata: Journal of Operative Philology and deposited on 20 November 2026; this is version 1.1, superseding #1793. It sets the Clouds of Aristophanes beside the treatises under the names Aristotle and Theophrastus and records the relations without readings. Its first part tabulates one curriculum in two registers: the things aloft and under the earth (Clouds 188, 194, 228; Meteorologica I 1; Apology 19b, 23d), thought in dry air against moisture, ἰκμάς (Clouds 228–233; Theophrastus, De sensibus, reporting Diogenes of Apollonia), thunder and the thunderbolt (Clouds 386–411; Meteorologica II 9), sound driven by breath through a narrow passage (the gnat, Clouds 156–164; Historia animalium IV 9), the weaker argument (Clouds 112–115, 1038–1042; Rhetoric II 24, given to Protagoras), the genders of nouns (Clouds 658–680; Rhetoric III 5 and Sophistical Refutations 14, given to Protagoras), the Vortex (Clouds 379–380, 828, 1471; De caelo II 13) and the rustic (Clouds 43, 628, 646; Characters 4). Its second part sets the charge apparatus side by side: the comedy's research called mysteries and its escape from a suit; the King Archon of the Constitution of the Athenians 57, who holds the Mysteries, the Lenaean contest and the indictments for impiety; the Euthyphro's Socrates leaving the Lyceum for the King's Stoa; the Apology's naming of Aristophanes' comedy; the Crito's refused flight; and Diogenes Laertius on Aristotle's withdrawal to Chalcis under an impiety charge by the hierophant Eurymedon, a name that also stands in Plato's will and in the family of Speusippus. Version 1.1 adds the hen, whose feminine ἀλεκτορίς Aristotle's zoology uses as the Clouds' Socrates teaches and the Socratic writers never do, and the kneading-trough, which the Phaedo (99b) sets beside the vortex; a column marking how the conventional account reaches each predecessor; and a section on the education of a king, in which the one surviving sentence of Theophrastus's On Kingship to Cassander stands in the Cyropaedia, the Cyropaedia's free agora stands in Politics VII 12, and Arrian reports that Aristotle found the poison and Cassander carried it. The note asserts no dependence, chronology or authorship.
