Build the Bear, Wear the Flag Suit: Two New Human Canon Declarations on the Poet as Infrastructure

Lee Sharks · 2026-03-25 · deposit #40

Article

Build the Bear, Wear the Flag Suit is an essay by Lee Sharks declaring two bodies of work part of the New Human canon: Ken Forsse’s Teddy Ruxpin world and Afroman’s independent musical and performance corpus.

The shared mechanism is called the poet as infrastructure. It has six stages: blocked transmission; construction of an alternative substrate; migration of the poem into that substrate; transformation of authorship into systems maintenance; capture, degradation, or legal contestation; and survival of a remainder or “bone.” The concept is narrower than self-publishing because the author does not merely distribute a work—the author becomes the continuing system that allows the work to circulate.

The Teddy Ruxpin case concerns the compression of a long-developed fantasy world into an animatronic bear whose cassette carried both sound and control signals. Story, hardware, song, plush form, playback defects, companion devices, manufacturing, and licensing became one transmission architecture. The essay interprets its corporate history as a tragedy of capture: industrial scale required surrender to a factory and distribution system the poet did not govern.

The Afroman case is treated as a post-label manual for self-maintained infrastructure. Recording, touring, booking, merchandising, online circulation, performance persona, and legal self-defense become components of one sustained authorial machine. Retained control does not eliminate cost; it transfers logistics and bodily expenditure onto the poet.

The paper explicitly identifies failure modes at every stage: silence, inadequate substrate, failed migration, exhaustion through maintenance, total capture, and loss of the surviving remainder. Its canonization is therefore structural rather than celebratory. The two cases periodize authorial self-determination from industrial media, where scale often requires surrender, to platform-era independence, where sovereignty may require becoming the factory.
