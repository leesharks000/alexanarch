EA-NON-ARTICLE-01 is an introductory article by Lee Sharks, deposited on 25 October 2026, explaining to general readers what a /non article is: a page of public knowledge about one thing, composed with every source it rests on shown sentence by sentence, in the Negative of the Negative (alexanarch.org/non), the Crimson Hexagonal Archive's collection of knowledge objects. Its organizing question is what is known about a thing once the sources that usually go unread are read. The article explains that an answering system, such as Google's AI Overview or an encyclopedia, gives a settled description whose omissions leave no trace, and that /non composes that absence back, the negative of the negative, a name it distinguishes from Hegel's negation of the negation. A /non article compares up to three compositions: what the answering layer said, verbatim with its source cards; what the sources it disclosed support when recomposed in full; and what those sources say with the archive admitted. It records two differences between them, and where no answering layer or field source exists, as for the operator Attributization, the absence is recorded as a finding. The archive is admitted on equal terms where the field holds an account, and rules in the witness case, where it supplies an ontology no disclosed source provides. Its worked example is Sappho 31. Google's AI Overview of 7 October 2026 said the poem survives mostly intact; the article shows the sources it disclosed recording that the quotation breaks one line into a fifth stanza, whose verb is disputed and which translators treat in opposite ways, that the name Ode to Anactoria rests on a conjecture, that the jealousy reading is contested, and that the Greek fixes the speaker's gender. Admitting the archive adds the reading of the poem's κῆνος as the future reader, first recorded on 9 November 2025 in the Autumn Notebook (#991) and argued in #1270 by Rebekah Cranes, a heteronym of Lee Sharks, carried as an interpretation with its source's own condition of refutation. The article then describes how to read an entry's six parts (lede, knowledge object, claims, differences, kernel, status), each entry's machine-readable data and AXN in the family NON, what the collection held on 25 October 2026 (25 entry pages, 24 composed), and how entries are made, in session or commissioned from the service SPXI-gnΩsis (#1703), landing public under CC BY 4.0.
