# Secondary Source: how the Apocalypse came to be received as derivative, secondary and marginal — a design

Working design, v0.1, 2026-11-05. Not deposited. Precedes a draft. The companion study to Legitimately Many (#1733), on the same method: strata of reception, loci read in seated texts, two counts kept apart, a locus table with a provisional coding, the operator's rulings in the apparatus. Loci marked [seated] were checked in the seat named; loci marked [to seat] are named from the received record and enter the study only when read in a seated text.

## 0. The question

0.1. The book does not present itself as secondary. It opens Ἀποκάλυψις Ἰησοῦ Χριστοῦ, ἣν ἔδωκεν αὐτῷ ὁ Θεός (Rev 1:1), names the act of writing and the address, Ὃ βλέπεις γράψον εἰς βιβλίον καὶ πέμψον ταῖς ἑπτὰ ἐκκλησίαις (1:11), blesses its reader, Μακάριος ὁ ἀναγινώσκων (1:3), and seals its own text against addition and subtraction, ἐάν τις ἐπιθῇ ἐπ᾽ αὐτά (22:18–19) [seated: gnt-nestle1904/text/Rev.txt]. It is the one book of the collection that calls itself a book, a prophecy and a revelation given by God, and that guards its words.

0.2. The book is nevertheless received as the last, the latest and the least: last in the codex, dated last or nearly last among the major works, disputed in the East for centuries, absent from the Byzantine lectionary, assigned to a second John, explained by its Jewish and pagan sources, and set apart as the strangest book of the collection. The study asks how the reading of the Apocalypse as derivative, secondary and marginal was set, held and hardened, through the textual, material, editorial and transmission history, over and above the difficulty the book itself carries.

0.3. The study holds the archive's thesis and states it: Revelation First, the Apocalypse as the generative seed from which the epistles and gospels unfold, with 66 CE as the archive's working proposed date (#1735; #1737; held as proposed until formally argued). The thesis is ontological as well as chronological: the cosmic Christ of Revelation 1 is the originary form (#407; #636; #902). The reception history does not have to prove the thesis. It records how its contrary became the received frame.

## 1. The received reading, stated once and in full

The Apocalypse was written late in the first century, c. 95, under Domitian, on the testimony of Irenaeus (Adv. haer. 5.30.3, as transmitted by Eusebius HE 3.18 and 5.8). Its author was a prophet named John, distinct from the author of the Gospel, as Dionysius of Alexandria argued from its Greek (HE 7.25). Its imagery draws on the Hebrew prophets, Daniel, the Jewish apocalypses and the mythology of the region, and its theology depends on the earlier Christian tradition of the gospels and Paul. Its acceptance was slow and contested: Gaius of Rome ascribed it to Cerinthus, some in the East rejected it, Eusebius listed it both among the acknowledged and among the spurious, several fourth-century lists omit it, and the Greek churches did not read it in the liturgy. It stands last in the canon because it concerns the last things. Each of these is a datum of the record, and the received reading is coherent with all of them.

## 2. Three things kept apart

The study counts three things and never lets one stand for another.

2.1. **Order:** where the book stands in a collection, a codex, a list or a lectionary.

2.2. **Standing:** how a list, a council, a father or an edition rates the book: acknowledged, disputed, spurious, read, unread, "in the second rank".

2.3. **Derivation:** what the book is said to come from or come after: its sources, its date, its author's relation to the apostle, its relation to the gospels and letters.

The study's governing observation, offered as the hypothesis of the reception history: order and standing were set by filing and liturgical practice, and derivation was later read off them. A book placed last and rated second is read as having come last and as depending on what came first.

## 3. The strata, and what each does to the three counts

3.1. **The book's self-disclosure** [seated]: Rev 1:1–3, 1:11, 22:7, 22:10, 22:18–19. The book names its origin, its act of writing, its address to seven churches and its finality.

3.2. **The earliest witnesses** [to seat]: Justin, Dialogue 81 (John, one of the apostles, prophesied in a revelation made to him); Irenaeus, Adv. haer. 4.20.11 and 5.30.3 (the vision "seen" at the end of Domitian's reign, the date's single source); Melito and Papias as Eusebius reports them; the Muratorian fragment, whose list receives the Apocalypse and, of Paul, says that he "following the order of his predecessor John" writes to seven churches by name (the wording to be read in a seated text before it enters). If the Muratorian sentence reads as remembered, the earliest list of the collection names John the predecessor and Paul's seven-church corpus the imitation, the order the archive's thesis proposes; the received reading files the sentence as the fragment's error or its rhetoric.

3.3. **The disputes** [to seat]: Gaius of Rome as Eusebius reports him (HE 3.28, the book ascribed to Cerinthus); the Alogi in Epiphanius, Panarion 51; Dionysius of Alexandria in HE 7.25, the stylistic argument that separated the book from the Gospel's author, made in the controversy over Nepos and the millennium. The study reads where the argument appears and what it answers: a reading of the book (chiliasm) was answered by lowering the standing of the book's author. Mechanics only: the operation, the incentive it serves and its function, with no claim about any person's intent.

3.4. **The ledgers** [to seat]: Eusebius HE 3.25, the book entered twice, among the acknowledged "if it seems right" and among the spurious "if it seems right", and HE 3.39, Papias's two Johns, which supplies the second John to whom the book can be given; the canon of Laodicea 60 and the Apostolic Canons 85, which omit it; Cyril of Jerusalem, Catecheses 4.36, which omits it and places "the rest" in a second rank (the wording to be read in seat); Gregory of Nazianzus, Carmina 1.1.12; Amphilochius, Iambi ad Seleucum (some approve it, most call it spurious); Athanasius, Festal Letter 39 (received, and last); the councils of Hippo and Carthage. The ledgers set standing, and a standing of "disputed" was carried forward as a property of the book.

3.5. **The codex and the lectionary** [to seat]: the book's place at the end of the collection in the codices that carry it (Sinaiticus, Alexandrinus; Vaticanus's New Testament breaks off before it); its transmission in a small fraction of the Greek manuscripts; its absence from the Byzantine lectionary. Order and use: a book read last and not read aloud in the church.

3.6. **The printed Bible and the Reformation** [to seat]: Erasmus's Annotationes on the book's authorship; Luther's 1522 preface to the Apocalypse and its 1530 replacement, and the September Testament's placement of Hebrews, James, Jude and the Apocalypse at the end without numbers; Zwingli's judgment at Bern; Calvin's commentaries on every New Testament book but this one. Standing becomes typography: the unnumbered tail of the September Testament.

3.7. **Criticism** [to seat]: the source-critical hypotheses of the 1880s (Vischer 1886, a Jewish apocalypse with Christian additions; Spitta; Weizsäcker), the history-of-religions readings (Gunkel 1895 on Revelation 12; Bousset 1896), Charles 1920 (ICC) on the book's Hebraic grammar and its sources; the c. 95 consensus as it rests on Irenaeus. Derivation becomes method: the book explained by what it is said to come from. Counter-witnesses in the same stratum are read as such (Robinson 1976 on the early date, recorded for dating only, since the priority thesis is the archive's own).

3.8. **The composition layer** [seated: the Capture Registry]: answer engines on the Apocalypse and on the archive's thesis, among them the Overviews that collapse Revelation First into Revelation Early (the operator's ruling of 2026-10-30: lead with the correction) and the composition at "3i atlas signal studies" that showed the same narrowing in another field (2026-11-04). The last edition, as in Legitimately Many §7.

## 4. What the study measures and what it withholds

4.1. **The locus table.** One row per locus: the stratum, the seat and the exact wording as seated, the slot (order, standing or derivation) and its filler, and two codes, one for the book's standing and one for its derivation, on the six-way coding of Legitimately Many (does not address; presupposes; argues for; considers and excludes an alternative; revises; holds open). The coding is the session's provisional reading; the operator rules on it.

4.2. **The prediction, stated before the table is built.** If order and standing were set by filing and use before argument, the early media should presuppose the book's place and leave its derivation unaddressed; argument about derivation should appear only where something put the book's standing in question, and should appear as a defence of a standing already given; and the later strata should read derivation off order and standing without arguing the step. The table tests this at the loci.

4.3. **Withheld.** The study does not decide the date, the author or the priority of the Apocalypse; it holds the archive's thesis and states it. It does not read motive: incentive and function only.
