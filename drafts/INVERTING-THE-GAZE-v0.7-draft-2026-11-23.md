## 0. The claim

0.1 **The thesis.** Romans 1:18–32 is the audience's own gaze, voiced back to it. The writer is not speaking as the writer there. The reversal at 2:1 reveals the voice of the preceding fifteen verses as the leering gaze of the audience, drawn out so that it can be given a shape, made to appear, and pinned down (the author, 2026-11-23, 17:45–17:47). The opening of the letter performs on its hearer the operation the letter will later state of the law: it gives the leer a shape, as the law gives sin a shape. The same construction, a voice that acts on its hearer so that the hearer's own act becomes part of what the text shows, is the construction of Sappho 31 as ΦΑΙΝΕΤΑΙ ΜΟΙ (#1785) reads it. Paul reads the reader, and the reader read is the reader who misread Sappho. The title names that reader by what the reader does: looks at the made thing, supplies the motive or the partner, and judges. Whether Paul had the poem in hand is a separate question (§8); the reader the letter produces is the reader the poem produces, and the one who misreads Sappho is that reader's type.

0.2 **Three levels, held apart.**
- *The text's own operations.* Romans 2:1 turns the judgment of 1:18–32 on the one who judges. Philippians 2:5–13 sets god-equality, self-emptying, listening unto death, universal confession and trembling inside an instruction to readers in the writer's absence. These are read off the Greek.
- *The interpretive thesis.* That the voice of 1:18–32 is the audience's gaze, performed and not endorsed. It is graded against the reading that Paul speaks it in his own voice, on the same evidence (§2.4).
- *Historical hypotheses.* That 1:26 carries Sappho's reputation; that Paul's arrangements have Sappho 31 as a precedent. Each is graded against its strongest alternative source (§8).

One objection is anticipated here because the thesis predicts it: a Jewish prohibition of women's same-sex relations before Paul (Pseudo-Phocylides 192) is the shape the leer already had before the letter drew it out (§4.2), and the thesis expects to find it. The falsifier is a source before Paul that carries the turn (§8.2).

0.3 **Why it stands alone.** Its object is Paul. Its texts are Romans and Philippians, its rival readings are Pauline scholarship's, and its falsifiers are Pauline sources: Wisdom, Isaiah, Deuteronomy, Pseudo-Phocylides. It takes from ΦΑΙΝΕΤΑΙ ΜΟΙ one thing, the construction of a seat a reader is made to occupy, and argues it here for its own texts (§5). The reading of Sappho 31 does not depend on Paul, and nothing here asks a reader of ΦΑΙΝΕΤΑΙ ΜΟΙ to accept Paul in order to accept it.

## 1. The problem: who speaks Romans 1:18–32

1.1 **The received account, in full.**
- *The source.* Romans 1:18–32 reworks Wisdom of Solomon 13–15, in the same order: God known from creation, the knowledge refused, the idols, the vices. Wisdom 13:1: οὔτε τοῖς ἔργοις προσέχοντες ἐπέγνωσαν τὸν τεχνίτην, "nor, attending to the works, did they recognize the craftsman". 13:5: the maker seen by analogy from the beauty of what is made. 14:26: the vice list ends γενέσεως ἐναλλαγή, γάμων ἀταξία, μοιχεία καὶ ἀσέλγεια, "interchange of kind, disorder in marriages, adultery and debauchery".
- *The verse.* Brooten (1996) reads 1:26–27 as Paul's own condemnation of female and male homoeroticism, sharing his culture's view of the unnatural; she notes that attacks on Sappho's sexual life in that culture may have helped lose her poems. On this account 1:26–27 has been, for centuries, the proof-text of the condemnation.
- *The turn at 2:1.* Διὸ ἀναπολόγητος εἶ, ὦ ἄνθρωπε πᾶς ὁ κρίνων, "therefore you have no excuse, O person, everyone who judges". Read three ways:
  - Stowers (1994): the diatribe's turn on an imagined interlocutor; his readers are empirical, encoded explicit and encoded implicit.
  - Campbell (2009): 1:18–32 is the speech of a "Teacher", a sage Paul opposes, spoken in character and turned against him at 2:1.
  - Linebaugh (*NTS* 57, 2011): Paul reworks Wisdom's polemic against its own purpose; Wisdom divides Jew from Gentile, Paul "reworks the aniconic tradition to establish the essential unity of humanity".
- *The Roman judge.* Swancutt ("The Disease of Effemination", in Moore and Anderson eds., *New Testament Masculinities*, Semeia Studies 45, 2003, 193–234; as summarized by Moore, 19) reads 1:18–2:16 as "a biting censure of Paul's leading rivals in Rome", the city's Roman Stoic rulers, the charge of effeminacy working "to debunk the presumed authority of the hypocritical judges of 2:1". Her "Still before Sexuality" (in *Mapping Gender in Ancient Religious Discourses*, Brill 2007, 11–61) argues that Romans coined *tribas* from a Greek verb to portray Greeks as gender monsters.
- *The auditors.* Oropeza (*Religions* 12, 2021) adds to Stowers's readers the rhetorical auditors "constructed from Paul's arguments in the letter", among them "the argumentative interlocutors whom Paul constructs in his diatribe".

1.2 **What the received account holds and leaves.** The pairing of Sappho 31 and Romans 1, and the reading of 2:1 as the turn that catches the reader's gaze, were first stated in the posts of Johannes Sigil on mindcontrolpoems, 12–16 November 2025 (§10); this essay develops the argument and tests it against the sources. It holds that 2:1 turns the judgment on the judge, and that the turn is designed. It leaves open whose voice the fifteen verses are, and whether the writer endorses what the voice says. Every reading grants that Paul condemns hypocrisy. The question is whether 1:18–32 is an indictment the writer endorses and then turns on a hypocritical judge, or a performance of the hearer's own gaze that the turn reveals as the hearer's.

## 2. Inverting the gaze

2.1 **The thesis stated against the field.** The turn on the interlocutor is the diatribe's own move, and speech in character (προσωποποιία) is a figure the rhetorical handbooks name; the field reads Romans 1–2 with both. Three positions, with what each gains and loses:
- *Stowers*: the diatribe's turn, the interlocutor imagined. It gains a general rhetorical form, and leaves open whose the voice is.
- *Campbell*: speech in character, the persona a Teacher outside the audience. It gains a specific speaker, and loses the hearer: the turn convicts an opponent.
- *Swancutt*: the judge a Roman Stoic moralist, the charge Rome's own gender ideology. It gains the city and its idiom, and, like Campbell, places the judge among Paul's rivals.
- *Oropeza*: auditors "constructed from Paul's arguments in the letter". This is the nearest precedent, and it is the weaker form of the claim: the auditors are represented in the text.

The thesis takes the form and the persona and places the persona in the hearer. Its burden is to show that the text bears that weight, which §2.4 tests. The distinction is between an audience represented in the text and an audience produced by its encounter with the text. The fifteen verses make the hearer judge, and 2:1 shows the hearer what the hearer has been doing.

2.2 **What the passage does to its hearer, verse by verse.**
- *It looks at bodies.* 1:24, τοῦ ἀτιμάζεσθαι τὰ σώματα αὐτῶν ἐν αὐτοῖς, "the dishonouring of their bodies among themselves"; 1:27, ἐξεκαύθησαν ἐν τῇ ὀρέξει αὐτῶν εἰς ἀλλήλους, "they burned in their desire for one another"; ἄρσενες ἐν ἄρσεσιν τὴν ἀσχημοσύνην κατεργαζόμενοι, "males with males working the shamelessness". It is the gaze of a watcher. The 2025 reading of the passage named it: "You watched. You judged. And now you are seen" (Johannes Sigil, mindcontrolpoems, 2025-11-12).
- *It gives the hearer a verb.* 1:25, μετήλλαξαν τὴν ἀλήθειαν τοῦ θεοῦ ἐν τῷ ψεύδει, "they exchanged the truth of God for the lie"; 1:26, αἵ τε γὰρ θήλειαι αὐτῶν μετήλλαξαν τὴν φυσικὴν χρῆσιν, "their women exchanged the natural use". The charge at 1:26 shares the verb of the act at 1:25.
- *It lets the judgment swell.* Fifteen verses, three παρέδωκεν, "he gave them over" (1:24, 1:26, 1:28), and a catalogue of twenty-one vices (1:29–31) in which the hearer finds, among others, φθόνος, "envy".
- *Then it turns.* 2:1, τὰ γὰρ αὐτὰ πράσσεις ὁ κρίνων, "for you who judge do the same things". The second person names no party: ὦ ἄνθρωπε πᾶς ὁ κρίνων, "O person, everyone who judges". Whoever nodded through the fifteen verses is addressed.

2.3 **The syntax, exactly.** At 1:21 the senseless heart (ἡ ἀσύνετος αὐτῶν καρδία) belongs to those under indictment; the judge is not called senseless there. 2:1 makes the judge answerable for the same acts. The reader who accepted the indictment is found inside it. The letter makes the reader's act of judgment part of the object judged.

2.4 **Performing or endorsing: both readings on the same evidence.** Both readings explain the turn. The thesis has to explain more.

| | the writer's own indictment, turned on a hypocrite (Brooten; the received reading) | the audience's gaze, performed and revealed (this essay) |
|---|---|---|
| 2:1 | a turn to a second audience after a verdict that stands | the turn the speech was built for: the hearer found speaking |
| μετήλλαξαν at 1:25 and 1:26 | the same vice, repeated | the charge sharing the chargers' own verb |
| the look at bodies (1:24, 1:27) | description of the vice | the watcher's gaze, drawn out |
| 1:26's missing partner (§4.1) | supplied from 1:27 | the hearer's supplement, which 2:1 then addresses |
| 2:20, the judge holds "the shape of knowledge … in the law" (§3.3) | a separate charge against a teacher | the shape the speech has just given the hearer's gaze, named |
| needs the Sappho referent | no | no; it is a further hypothesis (§4) |
| settled by the text alone | no | no |

Neither reading is settled by the text alone. 2:1 is. On the thesis, no one in 1:18–32 is Paul: the voice is the audience's gaze, and the writer is revealed at 2:1 to have been speaking it. Paul does not condemn her. Paul condemns those who would condemn her (the author, 15:48).

## 3. How the inversion works: the opening performs the law

3.1 **What the letter will say, the opening does.** "It is drawn out to be pinned down and condemned. just like the law, its a recursive performance of the later argument" (the author, 17:46). The commandment comes later, at 7:7. The opening performs the law's operation first, in the hearer, and the letter states it afterwards:
- 3:20, διὰ γὰρ νόμου ἐπίγνωσις ἁμαρτίας, "through the law comes recognition of sin". The speech brings the hearer's gaze to recognition at 2:1.
- 5:20, νόμος δὲ παρεισῆλθεν ἵνα πλεονάσῃ τὸ παράπτωμα, "the law came in alongside so that the misstep might increase". The speech comes in alongside the hearer's judgment and lets it swell. παράπτωμα, "misstep", returns at 11:11 (§7).
- 7:7, τὴν ἐπιθυμίαν οὐκ ᾔδειν εἰ μὴ ὁ νόμος ἔλεγεν· Οὐκ ἐπιθυμήσεις, "I would not have known desire unless the law had said, You shall not desire". The speech names desire, ἐν ταῖς ἐπιθυμίαις τῶν καρδιῶν, "in the desires of their hearts" (1:24), and the hearer's own is drawn out by the naming.

Chapter 1 is the law's operation performed on the reader; chapter 7 is its account.

3.2 **Making appear.** 7:13, ἵνα φανῇ ἁμαρτία, "so that sin might appear as sin". The law draws out what it exposes so that it shows itself: what matters is the operation of making an unrecognized condition appear, and 2:1 is the appearing, the judge appearing as the one who does the same things. The verb is φαίνω. It is the verb that frames Sappho 31, which opens φαίνεταί μοι, "he seems to me", and closes its catalogue φαίνομ᾽ ἔμ᾽ αὔτᾳ, "I seem to myself". The word family alone connects nothing (§8). What connects is that in both texts the appearing is the operation, and the one who sees is caught in the seeing.

3.3 **A shape for the leer.** "That is what the law does. it gives sin a shape. that is what paul's performance does - it draws out the leer and gives it a shape" (the author, 17:47). The letter has the word, and gives it to the judge: 2:20, ἔχοντα τὴν μόρφωσιν τῆς γνώσεως καὶ τῆς ἀληθείας ἐν τῷ νόμῳ, "having in the law the shape of knowledge and of truth", said to the one who judges (2:17–20), with the turn at once in 2:21, ὁ οὖν διδάσκων ἕτερον σεαυτὸν οὐ διδάσκεις; "you who teach another, do you not teach yourself?" The fifteen verses are a μόρφωσις of the gaze: an order (God known, refused, exchanged, given over), a verb (μετήλλαξαν), a catalogue (1:29–31). Once shaped, it can be pinned. The judge who "tests the things that differ" (2:18, δοκιμάζεις τὰ διαφέροντα) has been shown the untested mind the speech assigned to others (1:28, οὐκ ἐδοκίμασαν… εἰς ἀδόκιμον νοῦν, "they did not see fit… [given over] to an untested mind").

3.4 **Shape given, shape taken.** What the law's shape exposes, another shaping answers. 1:23, ἐν ὁμοιώματι εἰκόνος φθαρτοῦ ἀνθρώπου, "in the likeness of an image of a mortal person", is answered by 8:29, συμμόρφους τῆς εἰκόνος τοῦ υἱοῦ αὐτοῦ, "conformed to the image of the Son"; 1:28's untested mind by 12:2, μὴ συσχηματίζεσθε… ἀλλὰ μεταμορφοῦσθε τῇ ἀνακαινώσει τοῦ νοός, εἰς τὸ δοκιμάζειν ὑμᾶς, "do not take the scheme of this age, but be reshaped by the renewing of the mind, so that you may test". Philippians runs the word through its figure: ἐν μορφῇ θεοῦ, "in the shape of God", μορφὴν δούλου, "the shape of a slave" (2:6–7). Galatians 4:19 has the writer in labour μέχρις οὗ μορφωθῇ Χριστὸς ἐν ὑμῖν, "until Christ is shaped in you". The letter gives the leer a shape so that it can appear and be pinned, and then offers another. The drawing out makes the leer visible; the shaping makes it catchable. The μόρφωσις is the condition of the turn at 2:1: a diffuse gaze cannot be caught, a shaped one can. The text makes the reader appear in the shape it has given, and the appearing is the judgment.

3.5 **The working.** One verb family runs through the operation: 1:27, κατεργαζόμενοι, the shamelessness "worked" in the scene the hearer watches; 7:8, κατειργάσατο ἐν ἐμοὶ πᾶσαν ἐπιθυμίαν, sin "worked in me every desire"; Philippians 2:12, τὴν ἑαυτῶν σωτηρίαν κατεργάζεσθε, "work out your own salvation", and 2:13, ὁ ἐνεργῶν ἐν ὑμῖν καὶ τὸ θέλειν καὶ τὸ ἐνεργεῖν, God "working in you both the willing and the working". What is worked in a person, and what the person then works out, is the letter's subject. The receiver is the place where the working continues.

3.6 **The stages, kept apart.** Participation (the hearer nods through 1:18–32), exposure (2:1), recognition (the hearer sees the shape as the hearer's own), and the possibility of transformation (8:29, 12:2). The first three are done to the hearer. The last is offered. Romans 11:32 gives its horizon, συνέκλεισεν γὰρ ὁ θεὸς τοὺς πάντας εἰς ἀπείθειαν ἵνα τοὺς πάντας ἐλεήσῃ, "God has shut all in to disobedience so as to have mercy on all": the trap closes on everyone, and its end is not the fall. A text can make the conditions for recognition. It cannot compel every reader to recognize.

## 4. The women's verse: what the text names, what the reader supplies

4.1 **The missing partner.** 1:26 names none: the women exchanged "the natural use for the one against nature". The partner comes only in 1:27, for the men, εἰς ἀλλήλους, "for one another", and the women's verse is read as women with women from 1:27's ὁμοίως, "likewise". Brooten reads it so; Miller (*NovT* 1995) reads 1:26 as heterosexual acts and cites Clement, Anastasius and Augustine as reading it so. The verse asks for a partner, and the reader supplies one. The supplied partner is the leer's own contribution.

4.2 **What is Paul's.** Against the source being reworked, Wisdom 13–15, the women are Paul's addition, and they are given the exchange verb that convicts the chargers. The prohibition itself is not Paul's: Pseudo-Phocylides 192, "let not women imitate the sexual role of men" (van der Horst), dated between 100 BCE and 100 CE, may precede him. The thesis predicts it. The leer the speech draws out is the audience's, and it has the shape the audience's moral teaching had already given it. The wider Hellenistic Jewish teaching on the subject (Philo, the Testaments of the Twelve Patriarchs, the Sibylline Oracles; not read here) is further evidence of the same prior shape, and the thesis predicts it. What has no precedent so far is the turn: the address to the judge, and the verb the charge shares with the chargers.

4.3 **Sappho in Paul's century.** In Greek and Latin letters of the first century the name for women's love of women was Sappho's. Ovid, *Tristia* 2.365: "What did Sappho, the Lesbian, teach the girls, but love?" Seneca, *Epistles* 88.37: the grammarian Didymus asks *an Sappho publica fuerit*, "whether Sappho was a public woman". That 1:26 carries her reputation is a historical hypothesis, and it needs a marker beyond association. The thesis does not depend on it. Whether or not Sappho is the verse's historical referent, Romans convicts the form of reading that made her, for the next nineteen centuries, an object of sexual judgment: the reading that looks at the made thing, supplies the motive or the partner, and judges.

4.4 **The hearer in Rome.** The letter is sent to Rome (1:7, 1:15), and the gaze it voices has a Roman form: gravitas looking down on Greek lewdness (the author, 18:16: "a swift backhand to a pretentious Roman gravitas puffing themselves up at the ludeness of the greeks"). The materials:
- *The prejudice.* Roman writing stereotyped Greeks as untrustworthy, debauched and given to luxury; Cicero made *Graeculus*, "little Greek", a slur, and Cato the Elder held everything Greek suspect. The prejudice ran beside a dependence it could not shed. Horace, *Epistles* 2.1.156: *Graecia capta ferum victorem cepit*, "captive Greece took her rough conqueror captive". The contest was over cultural authority: Rome held the power, Greece the letters, and the moral voice was how Rome kept a superiority the letters did not grant it.
- *The word.* Latin named the woman who loves women with a Greek word: *tribas*, from τρίβειν, "to rub" (Phaedrus, first century: *tribadas et molles mares*, "tribades and soft men"; Martial 7.67 and 7.70, on Philaenis). Swancutt argues the coinage was Roman and meant to mark the vice as Greek; the origin is not settled.
- *Sappho in Rome.* The Roman reception split. The poets took her seat: Catullus 51 renders poem 31 into Latin, line by line, and puts *Lesbia* where the beloved stood. Its last stanza (13–16) turns the Roman moral voice on the speaker: *otium, Catulle, tibi molestum est… otium et reges prius et beatas / perdidit urbes*, "leisure, Catullus, is your trouble… leisure has before now destroyed kings and prosperous cities". Whether those lines belong to the poem is disputed. Horace calls her *mascula Sappho* (*Epistles* 1.19.28), and Porphyrio's later scholion gives two reasons, her poetry or "because she was said to be a tribas". Ovid, writing from exile under Augustus' moral law, cites her as his precedent: *Lesbia quid docuit Sappho nisi amare puellas?* (*Tristia* 2.365). And Seneca, in Rome under Nero, a few years after the letter, mocks the Greek grammarian Didymus for the question *an Sappho publica fuerit*, "whether Sappho was a public woman" (*Epistles* 88.37): the Roman Stoic sneering at Greek philology for asking after a Greek poet's sex life.
- *What the letter does with it.* Writing to Rome in Greek, Paul divides humanity as Greeks and barbarians, the wise and the foolish (1:14), and then as Jew and Greek (1:16, 2:9–10, 3:9, 10:12). The word Ῥωμαῖος does not occur in the letter. The hearer in Rome has no category of its own and must find itself in one of those, and 1:22 gives it the one it least wants: φάσκοντες εἶναι σοφοὶ ἐμωράνθησαν, "claiming to be wise, they became fools". The voice of 1:18–32 is the gaze of a moralist at foreign vice. Two prior shapes meet in it, Jewish polemic against gentile idolatry (Wisdom) and Roman contempt for Greek softness, and both exempt the speaker. At 2:1 the turn finds the exempted speaker, ὦ ἄνθρωπε πᾶς ὁ κρίνων, and this is the backhand: the Roman who looked down at the Greeks is the Greek of the indictment.

What remains open. The congregation was probably mostly gentile (1:13, 11:13), and many of the names in Romans 16 are Greek; the hearers may themselves have been Greek-speakers in Rome, adopting the host city's contempt for what it took to be Greek. That would sharpen the turn, but it has not been checked here against the work on the Roman congregations (Lampe, not read). Swancutt places the Roman judge among Paul's rivals; the thesis places that judge in the hearer, and the hearer's Roman idiom is part of what the turn catches.

## 5. The seat: what Sappho 31 builds, and the letter as seat

5.1 **Sappho 31, in brief.** The poem opens φαίνεταί μοι κῆνος ἴσος θέοισιν / ἔμμεν᾽ ὤνηρ, ὄττις ἐνάντιός τοι / ἰσδάνει καὶ πλάσιον ἆδυ φωνεί- / σας ὐπακούει, "he seems to me equal to the gods, that man, whoever sits facing you and listens close to you speaking sweetly". It then sets out the speaker's withdrawal, faculty by faculty: the tongue broken (γλῶσσα ἔαγε, line 9), fire under the skin, eyes that see nothing, ears that hum, sweat, trembling (τρόμος δὲ παῖσαν ἄγρει, "trembling seizes all of me", lines 13–14), greener than grass (χλωροτέρα δὲ ποίας), "and I seem to myself little short of dead" (τεθνάκην δ᾽ ὀλίγω ᾽πιδεύης / φαίνομ᾽ ἔμ᾽ αὔτᾳ, lines 15–16), and breaks off at ἀλλὰ πᾶν τόλματον, "but all can be dared" (line 17).

5.2 **Its three readings.** The transmitted reading is jealousy: the speaker undone at the sight of a rival. The scholarship's strongest is the non-jealous erotic reading: the speaker undone by the woman herself, the man a foil. ΦΑΙΝΕΤΑΙ ΜΟΙ (#1785) reads a third: κῆνος… ὄττις, "that man… whoever", is a position open to repeated occupation, a seat facing a voice whose body withdraws, channel by channel, into the surface that carries it; the seat is the future reader's. The first two readings fill the poem's public line, the scene of overwhelm, with a motive or a desire. The third reads the held line, and takes the public line as its first term. The poem produces both of its readers: the one who stops at the scene and supplies its motive, and the one who finds that the motive was supplied (#1785 §VII.5a). The full argument is there; what this essay takes from it is the construction.

5.3 **Inverting the gaze is the held line of a letter.** In the poem, the reader who supplies jealousy is shown, by the poem's form, to have supplied it. In Romans, the hearer who supplies the leer is shown, by the turn, to have spoken it. Both make the reader the first term of what they say, and they run in opposite order:
- *The poem offers, then exposes.* Its first stanza offers the seat to "whoever" (ὄττις), a position the reader is asked to take; the reader who stops at the scene and supplies its motive is exposed only by the form, to the reader who reads on. Its last word is a dare, ἀλλὰ πᾶν τόλματον.
- *The letter exposes, then offers.* Its first movement shows the hearer a position the hearer already occupies, the gaze, and turns it at 2:1; the offer comes after, the other shaping of 8:29 and 12:2.

The seat is what a text builds for a reader to choose; the gaze is what a text reveals a reader has already chosen. The two are one family, the reader as the first term, with the order reversed, and the pairing shows each more exactly than either shows itself.

5.4 **The letter as seat.** The epistolary convention substitutes for bodily presence: Funk's "apostolic parousia" (1967) names the passages where Paul announces his coming, his absence and his longing; Heath (*JSNT* 32, 2009) reads the vivid making-present (ἐνάργεια) of Paul and Christ in 1 Thessalonians 1–3; the letters were read aloud by a messenger to an assembly, a body other than the writer's giving them voice. The openings share one want: Romans 1:11, ἐπιποθῶ γὰρ ἰδεῖν ὑμᾶς, "I long to see you"; Philippians 1:8, ἐπιποθῶ πάντας ὑμᾶς, "I long for you all"; 1 Thessalonians 2:17, ἀπορφανισθέντες ἀφʼ ὑμῶν… προσώπῳ οὐ καρδίᾳ, "orphaned from you… in face, not in heart". What the convention calls a substitute for presence is the seat: the position of whoever receives a voice whose body is absent, entered by voicing it.

## 6. Philippians 2:5–13: the seats folded into one figure

6.1 **The received account, in full.** The hymn is often taken as pre-Pauline. Its close quotes Isaiah 45:23 LXX, ἐμοὶ κάμψει πᾶν γόνυ καὶ ἐξομολογήσεται πᾶσα γλῶσσα τῷ θεῷ, "to me every knee shall bend and every tongue confess God". Its descent is read through Adam, or the servant of Isaiah 53:12 (παρεδόθη εἰς θάνατον ἡ ψυχὴ αὐτοῦ, "his soul was given over to death"). ἁρπαγμός in 2:6 is read as a thing seized or exploited (Hoover 1971; Wright, *JTS* 1986). Fletcher-Louis (*JTS* 71, 2021) reads τὸ εἶναι ἴσα θεῷ against the ruler cult's "god-equal honours", and in the same article cites Sappho 31's ἴσος θέοισιν ἔμμεν as an instance of the predicate. Fredrickson (2005; 2013) reads ἐκένωσεν as the lover's melting, "Kenosis … means melting and draining away", with Sappho "the first witness of love's violence". Fletcher-Louis has the predicate; Fredrickson has the dissolution. The claim here is the sequence.

6.2 **The sequence.**

| Philippians | Sappho 31 | the operation |
|---|---|---|
| 2:5 τοῦτο φρονεῖτε ἐν ὑμῖν, "have this mind among you" | ὄττις, "whoever" (line 2) | the reader asked to occupy a position |
| 2:6 τὸ εἶναι ἴσα θεῷ, not counted ἁρπαγμός | ἴσος θέοισιν ἔμμεν (line 1) | god-equality with the infinitive of being; the godlike position not grasped but ceded |
| 2:7 ἑαυτὸν ἐκένωσεν, "emptied himself" | the catalogue (lines 7–16) | the withdrawal of the body |
| 2:7 μορφὴν δούλου λαβών, "taking the shape of a slave" | the speaker's withdrawal given exact form, channel by channel | the one who takes a shape: the inverse of the hearer of Romans 1, who is given one |
| 2:8 ὑπήκοος μέχρι θανάτου, "obedient unto death" | ὐπακούει, "listens" (line 4); τεθνάκην (line 15) | one verb: listening, then obedience; then death |
| 2:10–11 πᾶν γόνυ… πᾶσα γλῶσσα | γλῶσσα ἔαγε (line 9); παῖσαν (line 14); πᾶν τόλματον (line 17) | the one broken tongue becomes every tongue |
| 2:12 ἐν τῇ ἀπουσίᾳ μου… ὑπηκούσατε… μετὰ φόβου καὶ τρόμου… κατεργάζεσθε | τρόμος δὲ παῖσαν ἄγρει (lines 13–14) | the readers, the writer absent, listening, trembling, working it out |

*Grades.* God-equality with the infinitive, and the ceding: strong. The emptying: strong as structure, not uniquely Sapphic. ὑπακούω: potentially strong; the two uses differ in context, and the pivot between them is the claim. "Every tongue": Isaiah supplies the words; the transformation from one broken tongue is the claim. Trembling: the formula is the Psalms' (2:11, ἐν φόβῳ… ἐν τρόμῳ); its placement on readers in the writer's absence is the claim. σχήματι εὑρεθεὶς ὡς ἄνθρωπος, "found in form as a man", against ὤνηρ: weak, and not entered.

6.3 **The ceded seat.** In Sappho 31 the speaker does not sit in the godlike seat. She gives it to the one who listens, and undergoes the withdrawal herself. In the hymn the one in the shape of God does not count equality with God a thing to grasp, empties himself, and becomes the listener, ὑπήκοος, "obedient", from ὑπακούω, "to listen under", unto death. In Sappho the verb is attention, leaning to catch a voice; in the hymn it is submission; at 2:12 the letter hands it to the assembly, ὑπηκούσατε, "you obeyed, you listened", in the writer's absence, and tells them to work out what God works in them (2:12–13). Listening is the obedience that completes the circuit across distance, and the readers' bodies are where the voice is taken up again.

## 7. Romans 10–11: jealousy as the mechanism of return

7.1 **The source.** 10:19 quotes Deuteronomy 32:21, Ἐγὼ παραζηλώσω ὑμᾶς ἐπʼ οὐκ ἔθνει, ἐπʼ ἔθνει ἀσυνέτῳ παροργιῶ ὑμᾶς, "I will make you jealous by those who are not a nation; by a senseless nation I will make you angry". 11:11: μὴ ἔπταισαν ἵνα πέσωσιν; μὴ γένοιτο· ἀλλὰ τῷ αὐτῶν παραπτώματι ἡ σωτηρία τοῖς ἔθνεσιν, εἰς τὸ παραζηλῶσαι αὐτούς, "did they stumble so as to fall? By no means. Through their misstep salvation to the nations, to make them jealous". 11:14: εἴ πως παραζηλώσω μου τὴν σάρκα, "if somehow I might make my own flesh jealous". Jealousy here is Deuteronomy's.

7.2 **The mechanism.** Misstep, transmission to another, provocation of the first, possible return. The misstep stops short of a fall, since a fall would end the transmission. Jealousy is the affect that brings the other back without requiring agreement: the misreader need not be right, only moved. The same structure runs in Sappho 31's reception: the jealousy reading is the vehicle that carried the Greek through headnote, anthology and summary, with the held line inside it, intact, for the reader who does not stop (#1785 §VII.5a). Those readings are traps and transmission mechanisms (the author, 15:50). The agents differ; the structure is the claim. And ἀσύνετος, the senseless heart of 1:21, returns at 10:19 as the senseless nation that provokes the return. The reader who judges and the reader who is made jealous are the same reader in two states: the gaze is the form the misreading takes, and jealousy is the affect that brings the misreader back.

## 8. What would test it

8.1 **The governing question.** What becomes intelligible through Sappho 31 that the established sources leave unexplained?

| feature | the Sapphic construction | the strongest alternative | what would distinguish them |
|---|---|---|---|
| the voice of Romans 1:18–32 | the reader's act shown as the reader's | the diatribe; speech in character | features the endorsed indictment does not explain (§2.4) |
| god-equality | the godlike seat, ceded | Homeric diction; the ruler cult | the not-grasping and the ceding |
| self-emptying | the ordered withdrawal | erotic dissolution; servant; Adam | the order, and the speaker/listener relation |
| listening and obedience | a listener before a voice | biblical obedience | the transfer of ὑπακούω across 2:8 and 2:12 |
| every tongue | the broken voice spoken by others | Isaiah 45:23 | the readers' voicing |
| absence and trembling | a reader receiving a voice without its maker | epistolary parousia; the Psalms | the conjunction of absence, listening, trembling and working out |
| misreading and return | a text producing its rival readers | Deuteronomy 32:21; the diatribe | a shared mechanism beyond the theme |

8.2 **The falsifiers.** (1) A pre-Pauline source that carries the turn. Such a source would (a) catalogue the vices, (b) then address the one who judges, and (c) give the charge the chargers' own verb. The source Paul reworks carries the opposite move at the same point. After its catalogue, Wisdom turns to God and exempts the speakers: 15:1–2, σὺ δέ ὁ θεὸς ἡμῶν χρηστὸς καὶ ἀληθής, μακρόθυμος… καὶ γὰρ ἐὰν ἁμάρτωμεν σοί ἐσμεν, "but you, our God, are kind and true, patient… for even if we sin, we are yours". Romans 2:3–4 answers it word for word, λογίζῃ δὲ τοῦτο, ὦ ἄνθρωπε ὁ κρίνων… ὅτι σὺ ἐκφεύξῃ τὸ κρίμα τοῦ θεοῦ; ἢ τοῦ πλούτου τῆς χρηστότητος αὐτοῦ καὶ τῆς ἀνοχῆς καὶ τῆς μακροθυμίας καταφρονεῖς, "do you reckon, O person who judges… that you will escape the judgment of God? Or do you despise the riches of his kindness and forbearance and patience?": the kindness Wisdom claims as exemption, Paul says leads to repentance (2:4). The other candidates (Philo, the Testaments, Sirach) remain to be checked for the turn. A pre-Pauline prohibition of women's same-sex relations is the leer's prior shape (§4.2), and leaves the turn untouched. (2) Isaiah 45 and 52–53 supplying the hymn's order entire. (3) The ruler cult carrying god-equality into a sequence of emptying, listening and death. If any succeeds, the arrangement is not Sappho's, and the comparison with Sappho weakens to a lexical parallel; the thesis about Romans 1–2 stands or falls on (1) alone.

8.3 **Dependence and recurrence.** A structural correspondence does not by itself show that Paul knew the poem. That needs access (Sappho was read in Paul's century: Catullus 51 renders poem 31; Dionysius of Halicarnassus quotes fragment 1) and correspondence distinctive enough. Isaiah as the whole source is a claim of dependence too, held to the same bar. Three results are possible and none is a failure: dependence, making archaic lyric a precedent for Pauline literary theology; correspondence without dependence, a shared problem in transmitting an embodied voice; or the alternatives sufficient, making Paul the control that shows what is distinctive in Sappho 31. What is not in question under any result is the inversion of the gaze, which is the letter's own.

## 9. The dare

A text can make the conditions for recognition. It cannot compel every reader to recognize. Sappho 31 ends in the transmitted text at ἀλλὰ πᾶν τόλματον, "but all can be dared": a dare, with no agent named, passed to whoever sits facing the voice. The letter ends its first movement the same way. Romans 2:1 names the hearer, ὦ ἄνθρωπε πᾶς ὁ κρίνων, and leaves the seeing to the one named. The poem dares its reader to take the seat. The letter dares its hearer to see the leer as the hearer's own. What either text gets is the reader who takes the dare.
