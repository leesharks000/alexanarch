# The Portents Before the Fall: Josephus, Tacitus and the Apocalypse — a groundwork note on relations, with the archive's dates for Revelation (v0.1)

Lee Sharks · v0.1 · draft of 2026-11-04 · relations established, readings withheld · Greek of Josephus from the seated *Bellum Iudaicum* (Perseus tlg0526.tlg004, seat josephus, text/BJ.txt); Latin of Tacitus from the *Historiae* (Perseus phi1351.phi004.perseus-lat2, Fisher 1911, seat EA-CORPORA-33/01 tacitus, text/historiae.txt); Greek of the Apocalypse from the seated Nestle 1904 (EA-CORPORA-01/04, Rev.txt)

### Abstract

Two historians of the war of 66–70 report the signs that preceded the fall of the Temple. Josephus dates them by festival, at the eve of the revolt. Tacitus gives the same signs without a date, inside his narrative of the year 70. Set beside the Apocalypse, the two accounts share its furniture item by item: a sword over the city, a light at the altar for half an hour, a birth in the sanctuary, a door opened of itself, chariots and armies in the sky, a crash and a voice saying *we are going*, the four winds, the voice of bridegroom and bride, *woe* to the city, a sanctuary made square, and an oracle that one from that country would rule the world. This note sets the correspondences at the word, records the date Josephus gives, sets down the archive's own history of dates for Revelation, and records the held date of 66 CE. The reading the correspondences invite is withheld: whether the two historians are describing the book is the argument a later paper will make, and it is not made here.

---

## 1. The conventional reading, first and in full

The received account treats both passages as prodigy lists. Roman historiography reports portents before a disaster as a matter of form, and Tacitus's *Evenerant prodigia* (Hist. 5.13) belongs to that genre: armies in the sky, a temple lit, a door, a voice. Josephus writes for a Flavian readership, and his list serves an argument stated in it: that God warned the people and the people would not read the signs (BJ 6.288, 6.310, 6.315), and that the ambiguous oracle meant Vespasian (6.313). Tacitus is commonly read as dependent on Josephus or on a source the two share, since his items are a subset of Josephus's and he draws the same conclusion about the oracle. On this reading the overlap with the Apocalypse is an overlap of a shared stock — prophetic, apocalyptic and Roman — and needs no further account. The Apocalypse, on the majority dating, is written a generation after the events, under Domitian.

That reading is set down whole because the relations below must be checked against it. Every row of the table is consistent with it. What the table adds is the density of the overlap and its order, at the word.

## 2. The two accounts

Josephus, BJ 6.288–315, as the seated text has it. The people, ὡς ἐμβεβροντημένοι, *as if thunderstruck*, did not attend to the signs (6.288). Then the list:

1. ὑπὲρ τὴν πόλιν ἄστρον ἔστη ῥομφαίᾳ παραπλήσιον καὶ παρατείνας ἐπ' ἐνιαυτὸν κομήτης — a star like a sword stood over the city, and a comet a year long (6.289).
2. πρὸ τῆς ἀποστάσεως, at the gathering for the feast of unleavened bread, ὀγδόη δ' ἦν Ξανθικοῦ μηνός, at the ninth hour of the night, so great a light shone round the altar and the sanctuary that it seemed bright day, καὶ τοῦτο παρέτεινεν ἐφ' ἡμίσειαν ὥραν — and it lasted half an hour (6.290).
3. At the same feast a cow led to sacrifice ἔτεκεν ἄρνα ἐν τῷ ἱερῷ μέσῳ — gave birth to a lamb in the middle of the sanctuary (6.292).
4. The eastern gate of the inner sanctuary, bronze, barred with iron, closed with difficulty by twenty men, ὤφθη κατὰ νυκτὸς ὥραν ἕκτην αὐτομάτως ἠνοιγμένη — was seen at the sixth hour of the night opened of itself (6.293). The common people took it for the gate of good things opening; the learned for the sanctuary's security undone, a gift to enemies (6.295).
5. μιᾷ καὶ εἰκάδι Ἀρτεμισίου μηνός, on the twenty-first of Artemisius, before sunset, ὤφθη μετέωρα περὶ πᾶσαν τὴν χώραν ἅρματα καὶ φάλαγγες ἔνοπλοι διᾴττουσαι τῶν νεφῶν — chariots and armed battalions seen aloft over the whole country, darting through the clouds and encircling the cities (6.296–298).
6. At the feast called Pentecost the priests entering the inner sanctuary at night πρῶτον μὲν κινήσεως ἔφασαν ἀντιλαβέσθαι καὶ κτύπου, μετὰ δὲ ταῦτα φωνῆς ἀθρόας μεταβαίνομεν ἐντεῦθεν — said they perceived first a movement and a crash, and after that a voice all together: *we are going from here* (6.299). The seated text reads μεταβαίνομεν, the indicative.
7. Jesus son of Ananias, a countryman, πρὸ τεσσάρων ἐτῶν τοῦ πολέμου, at the feast of Tabernacles, began to cry: φωνὴ ἀπὸ ἀνατολῆς, φωνὴ ἀπὸ δύσεως, φωνὴ ἀπὸ τῶν τεσσάρων ἀνέμων, φωνὴ ἐπὶ Ἱεροσόλυμα καὶ τὸν ναόν, φωνὴ ἐπὶ νυμφίους καὶ νύμφας, φωνὴ ἐπὶ τὸν λαὸν πάντα (6.300–301); flayed to the bone before Albinus, he answered each stroke αἰαὶ Ἱεροσολύμοις (6.305); he cried it ἐφ' ἑπτὰ ἔτη καὶ μῆνας πέντε, until the siege, and was killed by a stone from an engine as he added αἰαὶ δὲ κἀμοί (6.308–309).
8. After the Antonia was pulled down the Jews made the sanctuary τετράγωνον, having it written in the oracles that the city and sanctuary would be taken ἐπειδὰν τὸ ἱερὸν γένηται τετράγωνον — when the sanctuary became four-square (6.311).
9. What most drove them to the war was χρησμὸς ἀμφίβολος ὁμοίως ἐν τοῖς ἱεροῖς εὑρημένος γράμμασιν — an ambiguous oracle, likewise found in the sacred writings — that at that time one from their country would rule the inhabited world (6.312); Josephus applies it to Vespasian (6.313).

Tacitus, Hist. 5.13, as the seated text has it. *Evenerant prodigia, quae neque hostiis neque votis piare fas habet gens superstitioni obnoxia, religionibus adversa. visae per caelum concurrere acies, rutilantia arma et subito nubium igne conlucere templum. apertae repente delubri fores et audita maior humana vox excedere deos; simul ingens motus excedentium. quae pauci in metum trahebant: pluribus persuasio inerat antiquis sacerdotum litteris contineri eo ipso tempore fore ut valesceret Oriens profectique Iudaea rerum potirentur. quae ambages Vespasianum ac Titum praedixerat.*

Prodigies had occurred: armies seen clashing in the sky, weapons glowing red, the temple suddenly lit by fire from the clouds; the doors of the shrine suddenly opened, and a voice greater than human was heard, *that the gods were departing*; at the same moment a great movement of their departure. Most believed it was contained in the ancient writings of the priests that at that very time the East would grow strong and men setting out from Judaea would possess the world. The riddle had foretold Vespasian and Titus.

## 3. The date

Josephus dates the signs by feast and month, without a year: the eighth of Xanthicus, at unleavened bread (6.290); the same feast (6.292); the twenty-first of Artemisius, *not many days after the feast* (6.296); Pentecost (6.299). The frame he gives is πρὸ τῆς ἀποστάσεως καὶ τοῦ πρὸς τὸν πόλεμον κινήματος, before the revolt and the movement to war (6.290). The revolt he dates at 2.284: προσελάμβανεν τὴν ἀρχὴν ὁ πόλεμος δωδεκάτῳ μὲν ἔτει τῆς Νέρωνος ἡγεμονίας — the war took its beginning in the twelfth year of Nero's rule, in Artemisius: the spring of 66 CE. The festal sequence of 6.290–299 runs from Passover to Pentecost of that spring. The one sign with its own span is Jesus son of Ananias: from four years before the war, seven years and five months, to the siege of 70 (6.300, 6.308).

Tacitus gives no year. The prodigies stand in the pluperfect, *evenerant*, inside his narrative of the year that opens Book 5 — *Eiusdem anni principio Caesar Titus, perdomandae Iudaeae delectus a patre* (5.1) — the year 70. The signs are prior to the narrative, undated within it.

So the two witnesses stand in this relation: **one dated, one undated, the same signs.** Josephus gives the spring of 66; Tacitus gives the signs as already past when Titus came.

## 5. Three relations, set down

**The witnesses agree on what was seen and differ on when it is placed.** Josephus places the signs at the feasts of the spring of 66, before the revolt; Tacitus places them before the siege, undated; the Apocalypse gives them no historical date and sets them as what John was shown (4:1, Ἀνάβα ὧδε, καὶ δείξω σοι ἃ δεῖ γενέσθαι μετὰ ταῦτα).

**Both historians locate the sign in a writing.** Josephus: the square sanctuary was written ἐν τοῖς λογίοις, the oracle found ἐν τοῖς ἱεροῖς γράμμασιν. Tacitus: *antiquis sacerdotum litteris contineri*. Both say the writing was read wrongly, and both say it meant Vespasian. Neither names the writing.

**The interpreters split in both accounts.** In Josephus the common people read the light and the gate as good and the learned as desolation (6.291, 6.295); in Tacitus *pauci in metum trahebant*, the few took them to fear and the many to their own greatness. The split is between a reading in which the signs promise and a reading in which they warn.

The reading these relations invite is the operator's: that the two historians are describing the book, its signs held in a writing of that spring. It is withheld here. What a paper making it will have to account for is the whole table, the conventional reading of §1, and the direction of every row, which this note does not assert.

## 7. The held date

The archive holds 66 CE as its working proposed date of composition, provisionally, subject to the case being formally argued (the operator's ruling of 2026-11-04). The date is the one Josephus gives the signs. It is held, and this note does not argue it. No earlier date in the archive is corrected by it; the two entries at c. 95 are recorded as errata because they set down the conventional date as the archive's own.

## 8. What is not claimed

No dependence in either direction between the historians and the Apocalypse is asserted. That Tacitus depends on Josephus is the received view and is left standing. The thirteen rows are correspondences at the word and the motif, many of which have older relatives in the prophets (the four winds in Zech. 6 and Dan. 7; the measured sanctuary in Ezek. 40–48; the silenced bridegroom and bride in Jer. 7:34, 16:9 and 25:10), which this note does not trace, because tracing them is the reading. The date is the date of the signs as Josephus gives it, and its use as the date of the book is held, not argued.
