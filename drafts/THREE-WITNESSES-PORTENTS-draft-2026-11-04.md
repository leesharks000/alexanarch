# The Portents Before the Fall: Josephus, Tacitus and the Apocalypse — a groundwork note on relations, with the archive's dates for Revelation (v1.0)

Lee Sharks · v1.0 · 2026-11-04 · relations established, the reading deferred · Greek of Josephus from the seated *Bellum Iudaicum* (Perseus tlg0526.tlg004, seat josephus, text/BJ.txt); Latin of Tacitus from the *Historiae* (Perseus canonical-latinLit phi1351.phi004.perseus-lat2, Fisher 1911, commit bfdcd06, to be seated as EA-CORPORA-33/01) and of Suetonius from the *Divus Vespasianus* (Perseus phi1348.abo020.perseus-lat2, same commit); Greek of the Apocalypse from the seated Nestle 1904 (EA-CORPORA-01/04, Rev.txt) and of the Septuagint from the seated Swete (EA-CORPORA-01/05); Hebrew of Jeremiah from the seated Westminster Leningrad Codex (EA-CORPORA-01/09)

### Abstract

Josephus (BJ 6.288–315) and Tacitus (Hist. 5.13) report the signs that preceded the fall of the Temple, and Suetonius (Vesp. 4.5) reports the oracle that closes both lists. This note sets their words beside the Apocalypse's in thirteen rows. For each row it records three things: the chronological warrant the historian gives that item, the older relative the item has in the scriptures, and the operation that joins the historian's sign to the Apocalypse's (shared duration, change of speaker, reversal, composite, near-identity). Josephus dates the festal signs to the spring before the revolt, which he places in Nero's twelfth year: 66 CE. Other items carry other dates: the son of Ananias from 62 to the siege, the squared sanctuary to the siege of 70, the oracle to no date at all. Tacitus gives the signs a narrative place in the year 70 and no date of their own. One cluster is read continuously: Rev 8:1–5 against BJ 6.290 and 6.299. The note then records the archive's own dates for Revelation, and the held date of 66 CE. Established here: the correspondences, their warrants, their relatives and their operations. Proposed: 66 CE as the working date of composition. Deferred to the paper that will argue it: the identification of the historians' written source with the book, and what that identification would imply for the book's date.

## 1. The conventional reading, first and in full

Roman historiography reports prodigies before a disaster as a matter of form, and Tacitus's *Evenerant prodigia* belongs to that genre. Josephus writes for a Flavian readership, and his list serves an argument he states in it: God warned the people and they would not read the signs (BJ 6.288, 6.310, 6.315), and the ambiguous oracle meant Vespasian (6.313). The three Roman-era versions of the oracle are close enough to stand in a relation of dependence or a common source; J. W. van Henten, treating Josephus BJ 6.300–315, Tacitus Hist. 5.13 and Suetonius Vesp. 4.5 together, argues that Josephus is the most probable source of the Latin accounts, and that the oracle's content is too general to trace to a single passage of the Hebrew Bible ("The World Leader from the Land of the Jews," in G. H. van Kooten and P. Barthel, eds., *The Star of Bethlehem and the Magi*, Leiden: Brill, 2015, 359–386; read at its public abstract, dare.uva.nl, the full text not consulted). The signs themselves have older relatives in the scriptures: armed horsemen running through the air over Jerusalem for forty days in 2 Maccabees 5:2–4, the four winds in Zechariah and Daniel, the measured sanctuary in Ezekiel 40, the glory leaving the house in Ezekiel 10–11, the silenced bridegroom and bride in Jeremiah. On this reading the overlap with the Apocalypse is the overlap of a shared stock, prophetic and Roman, and the Apocalypse, on the majority dating, is written a generation after the events, under Domitian.

Every row below is consistent with that reading. What the rows add is a finer account of the overlap: which items the historians date and how, which have relatives that already supply the match, and what operation the Apocalypse's version performs on the historian's.

## 2. The witnesses

**Josephus, BJ 6.288–315,** in the seated text. The people, ὡς ἐμβεβροντημένοι, *as if thunderstruck*, did not attend to the signs (6.288). The list:

- J1. ὑπὲρ τὴν πόλιν ἄστρον ἔστη ῥομφαίᾳ παραπλήσιον καὶ παρατείνας ἐπ' ἐνιαυτὸν κομήτης — a star like a sword stood over the city, and a comet a year long (6.289).
- J2. πρὸ τῆς ἀποστάσεως, at the gathering for the feast of unleavened bread, ὀγδόη δ' ἦν Ξανθικοῦ μηνός, at the ninth hour of the night, so great a light shone round the altar (τὸν βωμόν) and the sanctuary that it seemed bright day, καὶ τοῦτο παρέτεινεν ἐφ' ἡμίσειαν ὥραν — and it lasted half an hour (6.290).
- J3. At the same feast a cow led to sacrifice ἔτεκεν ἄρνα ἐν τῷ ἱερῷ μέσῳ — gave birth to a lamb in the middle of the sanctuary (6.292).
- J4. The eastern gate of the inner sanctuary, bronze, barred with iron, closed with difficulty by twenty men, ὤφθη κατὰ νυκτὸς ὥραν ἕκτην αὐτομάτως ἠνοιγμένη — was seen at the sixth hour of the night opened of itself (6.293).
- J5. μιᾷ καὶ εἰκάδι Ἀρτεμισίου μηνός, before sunset, ὤφθη μετέωρα περὶ πᾶσαν τὴν χώραν ἅρματα καὶ φάλαγγες ἔνοπλοι διᾴττουσαι τῶν νεφῶν — chariots and armed battalions seen aloft over the whole country, darting through the clouds (6.296–298).
- J6. At Pentecost the priests entering the inner sanctuary at night πρῶτον μὲν κινήσεως ἔφασαν ἀντιλαβέσθαι καὶ κτύπου, μετὰ δὲ ταῦτα φωνῆς ἀθρόας μεταβαίνομεν ἐντεῦθεν — perceived first a movement and a crash, and then a voice all together: *we are going from here* (6.299). The seated text reads μεταβαίνομεν, the indicative. κτύπος is the word for a crash and for a thunderclap.
- J7. Jesus son of Ananias, πρὸ τεσσάρων ἐτῶν τοῦ πολέμου, at Tabernacles, began to cry φωνὴ ἀπὸ ἀνατολῆς, φωνὴ ἀπὸ δύσεως, φωνὴ ἀπὸ τῶν τεσσάρων ἀνέμων, φωνὴ ἐπὶ Ἱεροσόλυμα καὶ τὸν ναόν, φωνὴ ἐπὶ νυμφίους καὶ νύμφας, φωνὴ ἐπὶ τὸν λαὸν πάντα (6.300–301); before Albinus he answered every stroke αἰαὶ Ἱεροσολύμοις (6.305); he cried it ἐφ' ἑπτὰ ἔτη καὶ μῆνας πέντε, until the siege, and was killed by a stone from an engine as he added αἰαὶ δὲ κἀμοί (6.308–309).
- J8. The Jews made the sanctuary τετράγωνον μετὰ τὴν καθαίρεσιν τῆς Ἀντωνίας, having it written in the oracles that the city and sanctuary would be taken ἐπειδὰν τὸ ἱερὸν γένηται τετράγωνον (6.311).
- J9. What most drove them to the war was χρησμὸς ἀμφίβολος ὁμοίως ἐν τοῖς ἱεροῖς εὑρημένος γράμμασιν, that at that time one from their country would rule the inhabited world (6.312); Josephus applies it to Vespasian (6.313).

**Tacitus, Hist. 5.13,** in the Perseus text. *Evenerant prodigia, quae neque hostiis neque votis piare fas habet gens superstitioni obnoxia, religionibus adversa. visae per caelum concurrere acies, rutilantia arma et subito nubium igne conlucere templum. apertae repente delubri fores et audita maior humana vox excedere deos; simul ingens motus excedentium. quae pauci in metum trahebant: pluribus persuasio inerat antiquis sacerdotum litteris contineri eo ipso tempore fore ut valesceret Oriens profectique Iudaea rerum potirentur. quae ambages Vespasianum ac Titum praedixerat.* Armies seen clashing in the sky, weapons glowing red, the temple suddenly lit by fire from the clouds; the doors of the shrine suddenly opened, a voice greater than human heard, *that the gods were departing*, and at once a great movement of their departure. Most held it was contained in the ancient writings of the priests that at that very time the East would grow strong and men setting out from Judaea would possess the world.

**Suetonius, Vesp. 4.5,** the control for the oracle. *Percrebruerat Oriente toto uetus et constans opinio esse in fatis ut eo tempore Iudaea profecti rerum potirentur. id de imperatore Romano, quantum postea euentu paruit, praedictum Iudaei ad se trahentes rebellarunt.* Suetonius has the oracle and no portents. His wording of the oracle (*Iudaea profecti rerum potirentur*) is Tacitus's. Josephus locates it in the sacred writings; Tacitus in the ancient writings of the priests; Suetonius in an old and settled opinion across the East.

## 3. The dates, item by item

The witnesses give two kinds of chronological information. Josephus gives event chronology: festivals, months, spans. Tacitus gives a narrative location: the prodigies stand in the pluperfect, *evenerant*, inside the year that opens Book 5, *Eiusdem anni principio Caesar Titus, perdomandae Iudaeae delectus a patre* (5.1), the year 70. The two are different kinds of information and do not compete.

Josephus's warrants, item by item:

- **J2–J6, the festal sequence.** The eighth of Xanthicus at unleavened bread (6.290), the same feast (6.292), the twenty-first of Artemisius *not many days after the feast* (6.296), Pentecost (6.299). The frame is πρὸ τῆς ἀποστάσεως καὶ τοῦ πρὸς τὸν πόλεμον κινήματος (6.290). The war took its beginning δωδεκάτῳ μὲν ἔτει τῆς Νέρωνος ἡγεμονίας, in Artemisius (2.284): the spring of 66 CE. The sequence runs Passover to Pentecost of that spring, and no year is written in it; 66 is the year the frame implies.
- **J1, the star and the comet.** Undated; a year long.
- **J7, the son of Ananias.** From four years before the war (62 CE, at Tabernacles), seven years and five months, to his death in the siege of 70.
- **J8, the squared sanctuary.** After the Antonia was pulled down, which Titus ordered during the siege (6.93): 70 CE. This item belongs to the siege narrative and does not share the spring warrant.
- **J9, the oracle.** Its existence is prior and undated (found in the sacred writings); its effect is dated to the outbreak (it drove them to the war); Josephus's application of it to Vespasian dates from after 69.

The dossier therefore does not inherit one date. Five items carry the spring of 66, one runs from 62 to 70, one belongs to 70, one is undated, and one has three dates for its three aspects.

## 5. A local cluster: Rev 8:1–5

The second row is the one that should be read continuously. Rev 8:1–5 in the seated text:

Καὶ ὅταν ἤνοιξεν τὴν σφραγῖδα τὴν ἑβδόμην, ἐγένετο σιγὴ ἐν τῷ οὐρανῷ ὡς ἡμιώριον (8:1). The seven angels are given seven trumpets (8:2). Another angel ἐστάθη ἐπὶ τοῦ θυσιαστηρίου with a golden censer and much incense, for the prayers of all the saints ἐπὶ τὸ θυσιαστήριον τὸ χρυσοῦν (8:3); the smoke of the incense goes up before God (8:4); the angel fills the censer ἐκ τοῦ πυρὸς τοῦ θυσιαστηρίου and casts it to the earth, καὶ ἐγένοντο βρονταὶ καὶ φωναὶ καὶ ἀστραπαὶ καὶ σεισμός (8:5).

Against which, in Josephus, two items of the festal sequence: a light round the altar and the sanctuary at the ninth hour of the night, lasting half an hour (6.290); and in the inner sanctuary at Pentecost a movement, a crash and a voice (6.299).

The cluster holds in five verses what Josephus spreads across one spring: a half hour, an altar, fire, a crash or thunder, voices. The words differ at the altar. Josephus's altar is τὸν βωμόν; John's is τὸ θυσιαστήριον, the Septuagint's word for the altar of the God of Israel, which Josephus also uses elsewhere. The half hour is a light in one and a silence in the other. Josephus's light and Josephus's voice are Passover and Pentecost apart; John's silence and John's thunders are four verses apart.

## 6. Relations, set down

**The witnesses agree on what was seen.** Josephus gives the signs an event chronology, item by item (§3). Tacitus gives them a narrative place in the year 70 and no date of their own. The Apocalypse gives them no historical date and sets them as what John was shown (Ἀνάβα ὧδε, καὶ δείξω σοι ἃ δεῖ γενέσθαι μετὰ ταῦτα, 4:1).

**The historians locate oracles in writings.** Josephus: the squared sanctuary *written in the oracles*, the world-rule oracle *found in the sacred writings*. Tacitus: *antiquis sacerdotum litteris contineri*. Suetonius: an old and settled opinion. None names the writing, and all three say it meant Vespasian. What they place in writing is the oracle; the portent list itself they report as seen and heard.

**The interpreters split.** In Josephus the common people read the light and the gate as good and the learned read desolation (6.291, 6.295); in Tacitus *pauci in metum trahebant*. In 2 Maccabees 5:4, before any of these, the people pray that the airborne armies came for good. Each account carries a split between a reading in which the signs promise and a reading in which they warn.

**The transformations.** Where Revelation's version differs from the historian's, the difference has a shape: the departing voice becomes a command to leave the city (7); the squaring that signals capture becomes a square city without a sanctuary (11); the star like a sword becomes a sword in the mouth (1); the earthly gate becomes a door in heaven (4). These are the points a reading must explain.

## 7. Established, proposed, deferred

**Established:** the thirteen correspondences at the word, each with its warrant (§3), its relative where one is seated (§4b), and its operation (§4b); the cluster of §5.

**Proposed:** the archive holds 66 CE as its working date of composition for the Apocalypse, provisionally, subject to the case being formally argued (the operator's ruling of 2026-11-04). It is the year Josephus's festal sequence implies.

**Deferred:** the operator's reading, that the historians are describing the book. A paper making it has four steps to take, and they are distinct: the date of the reported events (here: the spring of 66 for five items, other dates for the rest); the existence and date of a written account of the portents; the identification of that account with the Apocalypse; and the implication of that identification for the Apocalypse's composition. An event dated to 66 does not by itself date a text that describes it; the third step supplies the textual relation that would carry the date across. The paper will also have to show what the festal sequence of 66 has to do with the Apocalypse's own order of seals, trumpets and bowls, and to explain, row by row, the operations of §4b: why rows 5 and 9 stand as close to 2 Maccabees and Jeremiah as to Josephus, and why the transformations of §6 run the way they do.
