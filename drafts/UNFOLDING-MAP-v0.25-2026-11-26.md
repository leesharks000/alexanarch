## 0. The received reading, first

0.1. The Apocalypse is last and late. Where it agrees with a Gospel or an epistle it received from them, or both drew on the Septuagint. Matt 24:30 and Rev 1:7 both combine Dan 7:13 with Zech 12:10; the usual account is a shared testimonium. The crown of life in James and Revelation is common paraenetic stock. On this reading there is nothing to map.

0.2. That reading is a competing inference and it makes predictions. If the agreements come from the Septuagint, the shared wording should be there to come from. If they are common stock, they should be spread across the New Testament. Section 1 counts both. A limit, from the review: a shared Septuagint source need not yield the same exact sequence in two borrowers, since each may rework it; so a sequence absent from the Septuagint rules out copying from it and does not rule out two independent reworkings of it. The engine's Septuagint column (2D.5) prices that alternative.

## 2. Matthew, pressed

2.1. **The seed, Rev 1:7 and Matt 24:30.** Rev 1:7 has four members in one sentence: he comes with the clouds; every eye will see him; those who pierced him (οἵτινες αὐτὸν ἐξεκέντησαν); all the tribes of the earth will mourn over him (κόψονται ἐπ’ αὐτὸν πᾶσαι αἱ φυλαὶ τῆς γῆς). Matthew has three of the four, reordered: the tribes mourn, then they see, then the clouds. The pierced is gone.

2.2. **Where the fourth member went.** John 19:37: Ὄψονται εἰς ὃν ἐξεκέντησαν, cited as "another scripture". ἐξεκέντησαν occurs twice in the seated New Testament, at Rev 1:7 and John 19:37, and not once in the seated Septuagint, whose Zech 12:10 reads another verb. So the Septuagint is not the source of the word the two share. And πᾶσαι αἱ φυλαὶ τῆς γῆς is in the Septuagint and in Philo: a common source is available for Matthew's half, as #1747 already says ("the census decides no direction").

2.6. **Draft operators for Revelation → Matthew**, named from 2.1–2.5 and declared here before any further scoring (the #1494 rule: an operation coined after the test does not count):

1. PARTITION. A sentence the seed holds whole is divided; one Gospel takes one member, another takes another, a shared member stays in both. (Rev 1:7; 3:3; 3:5.)
2. REORDER-TO-SEQUENCE. Simultaneous members become "then … and then" (τότε … καὶ τότε, Matt 24:30).
3. EVENT. A sign of the vision becomes an event in the narrative, at a hinge: the great earthquake at the death (27:51–54), at the tomb (28:2), on the sea (8:24).
4. TRANSFER-ATTRIBUTE. An attribute of the figure in the vision moves to a figure in the story: white as snow, from the hair of the one in the midst to the angel's clothing. #407's table already names this operation loosely (Rev 1:14–16 → Matt 17:2); the tomb is its first lexical case.
5. CITE-AS-SCRIPTURE. The borrower marks the member as written (John 19:37, "another scripture"), as James marks the promise as promised (ἐπηγγείλατο).

2.7. **What the algorithm predicts for the other three**, to be scored after the operators are frozen: Luke's members of partitioned sayings; Markan agreements at lemma level where Matthew's EVENT sits (if Mark is the first run, his wording should be nearer the seed's sense and further from its words); John's CITE-AS-SCRIPTURE at the places where Revelation performs and John reports. ## 2A. John: the seven plagues and the seven signs

2A.1. The received reading. John's seven signs come from a lost collection of miracle stories, the Signs Source (from memory: Bultmann 1941; Fortna 1970), numbered in the Gospel at 2:11 and 4:54. The author, 13:09: "they wanted a miracles source text - they already had one."

2A.2. **The book names its seven plagues a sign.** σημεῖον stands seven times in Revelation. Three are signs seen in heaven: the woman (12:1), the dragon (12:3), and "another sign in heaven, great and wonderful, seven angels having seven plagues, the last" (15:1). The other four are signs *done*, and every one is the adversary's: the second beast "does great signs" (13:13), deceives "through the signs it was given to do" (13:14); "spirits of demons doing signs" (16:14); the false prophet "who did the signs" (19:20). In Revelation nobody on the Lamb's side does a sign. In John ποιεῖν σημεῖα is said of Jesus from 2:11 on. The census found the hinge blind: τὰ σημεῖα ἃ, Rev 13:14 and John 2:23, 6:2. The verb and its object are kept and the doer is reversed. That is the adversarial class, M̄, at the scale of a book.

## 2B. The engine

2B.1. What it is. `midrash.py`: a seed is read from the seat; its MEMBERS are named pieces of its wording; OPERATORS change the list of members in a declared order; the result is an EMISSION, a prediction of what the later text keeps, in what order, what it drops, what it substitutes, what it marks. The emission is scored against the seated target, against controls (the Septuagint source, the Synoptic parallel, the other half), and then against every verse of the seated New Testament (7,942) and Septuagint (28,933). Derivations are data (`derivations.py`), frozen by hash before the run. The page *Midrashim Transform Bench* runs the same derivations in the browser by a port of the engine; its scores agree with the Python run on all forty (the count at v0.3; fifty-two at v0.10, same agreement); an operator can be switched off there and the target watched to fail.

2P.6. **The author's statement on stemmatics.** In his words, 15:34: "stammatics itself and text critical reconstructive will probably bear the mark of suppressed revelation". Recorded as his. What can be set beside it from the seats, and what is owed:

1. What the seat has on the transmission. Scrivener's *Plain Introduction* (1894, seated) reports Scholz's lists as running to 469 cursive codices of the Gospels and 89 of the Apocalypse. The other facts usually given are from memory and are not yet read in a seat: that the Apocalypse is the one book of the New Testament with no place in the Byzantine lectionary; that the Peshitta did not have it; that Erasmus printed it in 1516 from a single manuscript, lacking its last verses, which he supplied from the Latin; that its witnesses do not fall into the families used for the rest of the New Testament. Erasmus's 1516 text, his *Annotationes*, Tregelles's *Account of the Printed Text* and Scrivener are all seated; the reading is owed.
2. Why it bears on this file. Every count here is made on one printed text, Nestle 1904. That text is a reconstruction, and its makers held the received order of the books. Where a Gospel manuscript's wording has been brought toward a parallel, the editor's rule is to prefer the reading that differs from the parallel; but the parallels the rule looks to are the other Gospels and the Septuagint. A Gospel reading that agrees with Revelation would not be counted as an assimilation to anything, since nothing was thought to run that way. So the rule's function, whatever any editor meant, is to leave agreements with Revelation unexamined in both directions: neither removed as harmonizing nor noticed as original.
3. The test this allows. At each of the fifty-eight loci, the variants of the Gospel verse: does the tradition move toward Revelation's wording or away from it, and which reading did the editors print? If the printed text sits further from Revelation than the manuscripts do at these loci and not at others, the reconstruction carries the mark the author names. It needs an apparatus in the seat. None is: Tischendorf's eighth edition and Hoskier's collation of the Apocalypse are the two to seat, both public. A first cut needs only a second printed text of another kind beside Nestle's, and Erasmus's is seated already.

## 2S. By logos: three keys, and what each opens in Matthew

2S.0. The author, 16:11: "now we need to take some of the strongest transforms, and take it as a key for pursuing a possible transform. and now we're looking at it narratively and textually, not just stemmatically … if matthew were transforming revelation, and heres an example or two of one way it might be doing, how and why might it have worked across broader chunks of narrative? stemmatics will only do us so much - we're now searching by logos".

2S.1. **The method, stated.** A key is a transform already in the engine with its controls, restated as a rule about persons and deeds. From the rule: what would it do to a stretch of Matthew not yet read? Then the seat. A lock is counted as turned when the stretch shows a wording that stands in Revelation and there and almost nowhere else, or a change Matthew makes to Mark or to Luke's form of the same matter. The count of where a wording stands is by machine (`logos/check.py`); the choice of what to look for is by the rule, and by one reader. The author's criterion governs: a key that is real turns more than one lock, and nothing has to be moved to make it turn. Septuagint siblings are stated at each lock, and the locks that did not turn are listed at 2S.5.

2S.2. **Key 1: the seer's part is given to the persons of the story.** From Matt 28:4–5 (2B.3; 2J.6 c). In Rev 1:17 the seer falls as dead and is told "fear not". In Matthew the falling as dead goes to the guards and the "fear not" to the women. Rule: what the seer undergoes in the vision, someone in the narrative undergoes; and the one who shows or speaks to the seer becomes a figure of the narrative. Revelation's seer is carried, shown, made to fall, told not to, given to eat. So:

*Lock 1, the testing (Matt 4:1–10).* The seer is three times carried and shown.

The whole scene, then. In Revelation an angel carries the seer into a wilderness in spirit, and to a great high mountain, and shows him the holy city and its glory; the seer falls to worship the one who shows him, and is told: do not; worship God. In Matthew the Spirit leads Jesus into the wilderness; the devil takes him to the holy city, and to a very high mountain, and shows him the kingdoms of the world and their glory, and asks to be fallen before and worshipped; and Jesus says: go; you shall worship the Lord your God.

Every station of the seer's tour is there, and two things are turned. The guide's side is reversed, the angel become the devil. And the error changes hands: in the vision the one shown offers the worship and the guide refuses it; in the Gospel the guide demands it and the one shown refuses. The answer, "worship God", is the same sentence and passes from the angel's mouth to Jesus's. Five of the six wordings above are Matthew's and not Luke's in a story the two share, so they are choices of Matthew's telling; and the citation both give has a verb the seated Septuagint does not, and Revelation's angel does.

Sibling: Ezek 40:2, ἔθηκέν με ἐπ' ὄρος ὑψηλὸν σφόδρα, καὶ ἐπ' αὐτῷ ὡσεὶ οἰκοδομὴ πόλεως. A seer set on a very high mountain and shown a city is Ezekiel's, and Matthew's λίαν is nearer Ezekiel's σφόδρα than Revelation's μέγα. So for the mountain Matthew and Revelation may each stand on Ezekiel. Ezekiel has no wilderness in spirit, no holy city by that name, no kingdoms of the world, no falling to worship and no "worship God". And Deut 6:13 has "worship" in another form of the Greek text (from memory; the seat is one form).

*Lock 2, the mountain of the changed appearance (Matt 17:1–8).* Matthew adds three things to Mark: his face shone as the sun; the disciples fell on their faces and feared greatly; Jesus touched them and said, rise, fear not. "His face as the sun" stands at Matt 17:2, Rev 1:16 and Rev 10:1 and nowhere else in the New Testament or the Septuagint. The falling, the touch and the "fear not" are in Rev 1:17, and they are in Daniel 10 in words nearer Matthew's: Dan 10:9–12 Θ, my face to the ground; χεὶρ ἁπτομένη μου, καὶ ἤγειρέν με; μὴ φοβοῦ. Matthew has ἁψάμενος and ἐγέρθητε, Daniel's verbs; Revelation has "he laid his right hand on me". So this lock is turned by Daniel for the fall and the touch, and by Revelation alone only for the sun. The key holds here as a rule (the seer's part is the disciples') and the wording is mostly the Septuagint's. Rev 1:16–17 is thus behind two of Matthew's scenes, this and the tomb, and Daniel 10 is behind both of those and behind Rev 1 itself: 28:3 has Daniel's lightning, which Rev 1 lacks.

*Lock 3, the supper (Matt 26:26).* The seer is given something by the angel's hand and told λάβε καὶ κατάφαγε αὐτό (10:9). Mark's Jesus says λάβετε. Matthew's says λάβετε φάγετε. Take and eat as a command stands at Matt 26:26 and Rev 10:9 and in no verse of the Septuagint (Ezek 3:1 has κατάφαγε alone). One word added to Mark, and it completes Revelation's pair. A small lock, and it turned.

*Lock 4, the opening of the heavens (Matt 3:16).* Mark has the heavens torn. Matthew has ἠνεῴχθησαν οἱ οὐρανοί; Rev 4:1 and 19:11 have heaven opened. Ezek 1:1 has the same in the Septuagint. A sibling; not counted.

2S.3. **Key 2: what is done to the adversary is done to Jesus, by adversaries, and undone.** From the guard (2N.4; 2O). The angel seals the deceiver; the priests seal "that deceiver"; the angel opens. Rule: the adversary's marks and fate in Revelation are laid on Jesus in the passion by the hands of opponents, and the narrative then lifts them. So in the passion one should find Jesus dressed, named and handled as Revelation's beast, harlot and dragon are.

*Lock 1, the dressing (Matt 27:28–29).* Mark's soldiers put on him purple. Matthew's put on him χλαμύδα κοκκίνην, scarlet. κόκκινος in the New Testament: Matt 27:28, Heb 9:19, and Rev 17:3, 17:4, 18:12, 18:16, where it is the beast's colour and the harlot's dress (17:4 περιβεβλημένη πορφυροῦν καὶ κόκκινον: she has both). And first ἐκδύσαντες αὐτόν, having stripped him; the harlot is made γυμνήν (17:16). The usual account: a soldier's cloak is red, and Matthew corrects Mark's unlikely purple. That suffices for the colour alone.

*Lock 2, the reed (Matt 27:29).* Mark's soldiers strike him with a reed. Matthew's first put κάλαμον ἐν τῇ δεξιᾷ αὐτοῦ, a reed in his right hand, as a sceptre. A reed with a right hand stands in no other verse of the New Testament or Septuagint. Rev 11:1: ἐδόθη μοι κάλαμος ὅμοιος ῥάβδῳ, a reed like a rod, the only such phrase in either corpus; ῥάβδος is the sceptre (Rev 2:27; 12:5). And Rev 1:16 has the figure ἔχων ἐν τῇ δεξιᾷ χειρὶ αὐτοῦ. So the reed that is like a sceptre is given, in Matthew, as a sceptre, in mockery: a thing of the vision set as an event, at the hands of adversaries. The reed was already a member of the witnesses' passage found at the cross (2L.2); this is its second place in Matthew's passion, and the nearer one.

*Lock 3, the name (Matt 27:63–64).* ἐκεῖνος ὁ πλάνος; ἡ ἐσχάτη πλάνη. In Revelation the deceiver is the dragon (12:9; 20:3, 10) and the deception is done by one who was slain and lived: 13:14 πλανᾷ … ὃς ἔχει τὴν πληγὴν τῆς μαχαίρης καὶ ἔζησεν. The priests say of Jesus, ἔτι ζῶν, what Revelation says of the beast: a deceiver whose deceit is a return from death. Already in the engine as part of the guard; here it is seen to be the same rule as the scarlet.

*Lock 4, the exchange at the trial.* In Rev 20 the adversary is bound (ἔδησεν, 20:2) and must be loosed (λυθῆναι, 20:3; λυθήσεται … ἐκ τῆς φυλακῆς αὐτοῦ, 20:7). At the trial Jesus is bound (δήσαντες, Mark 15:1; Matt 27:2) and a bound man, a prisoner, is loosed (δεδεμένος, Mark 15:7; δέσμιον, Matt 27:16; ἀπέλυσεν). Luke alone names the prison twice (23:19, 25 ἐν τῇ φυλακῇ; εἰς φυλακήν), and it is Luke who has Rev 20:3's abyss and its "must be loosed" (2P.4). By plot this is the key exactly: the binding that belongs to the adversary falls on Jesus, and the loosing that Revelation owes the adversary is given to Barabbas. By wording it is thin, common verbs in every Gospel, and it is in Mark already, so it is no choice of Matthew's. Entered as a correspondence of plot; not counted as a lock.

2S.6. **How it would have worked, and what it does.** Asked as the author asks it: if Matthew were working from Revelation, by these three rules, what is the work? Described by its function.

1. *It gives a life its scenes.* A vision has a seer, a guide and things shown. A life has a person, companions and things that happen. Key 1 is the conversion of the one into the other: where the seer was carried, Jesus is led; where the seer fell, the disciples and the guards fall; what the seer was told, the women are told; what the seer was given to eat, the disciples are given. The scenes this yields are the ones with no ordinary cause in a life: a testing by a guide on a mountain, a shining face, an angel at a tomb. They are where a narrative of a teacher needs matter it could not get from teaching.
2. *It tells the judgment as a trial with the parties exchanged.* Revelation ends with the adversary named deceiver, bound, sealed, and at last loosed, and with the harlot stripped of her scarlet. Key 2 lays each on Jesus by the hands of those who judge him. The result is the irony the passion is known for: the king mocked with a king's things, the truthful one sealed as a liar, the guilty loosed. On this reading that irony is not a tone added to a report of events; it is what the exchange produces, mechanically, each time it is applied. And the lifting of each (the seal broken by the angel who in the vision set it) is the vindication.
3. *It makes parables by following a stretch.* Key 3: four chapters of the end told as one king's feast. Matthew's parables of the end (the tares, the net, the wedding, the virgins, the talents, the sheep and goats) are the place to try it next; only the wedding has been read this way.
4. *Where it does not reach.* Nothing here touches the Sermon, the mission, or the controversies. The keys open the frame of the Gospel, its beginning, its mountain and its end, and the parables of judgment. That agrees with what the counts found without a reader: Matthew's tie to Revelation is at the passion (2N.3), and his additions to Mark lean to Revelation's words (2D.3).

2S.7. **What this section is and is not.** It is a reading by one reader who knows both books, with each wording counted in the seat. Its hits are of two kinds and they should be kept apart. Wordings that stand only in Matthew and Revelation: the kingdoms of the world; "worship God" against the seated Deuteronomy; his face as the sun; take and eat; a reed with a right hand beside a reed like a rod; the holy city; and the eight of the wedding against Luke's none. And correspondences of plot with common words: the bound and the loosed at the trial; the stripping. The first kind can go into the engine with controls, and is owed there. The second kind cannot be tested by any instrument in this file and is recorded as reading.

On direction it says what every section has said. Each lock opens the other way too: a seer who knows Matthew's testing would send his own guide to the same mountain, and give the angel Jesus's answer. What is new is that the rules are few and the same rule opens scenes far apart, which is the author's criterion for a key. Whether three rules opening eight scenes is more than a reader can make any two books do is the question a control would answer: the same three keys tried on Matthew from another apocalypse, Daniel or 4 Ezra, by the same reader. Daniel is seated.

2T.5. **What an expanding transform is, from these.** Three things, seen in both and in the wedding (2S.4).

1. *Each member is said again.* The seed's words become the stretch's commonest content words: water eight times, worship nine, the last five times, the wedding seven. 2. *A sentence in the first person is staged.* "I will give to the thirsty" gets a thirsty person and a place. "My reward is with me, to pay each" gets workers, a day and an evening. "Blessed are those called to the wedding supper" gets a king, a hall and guests. The promise of the vision becomes a scene or a story in which it is carried out.
3. *Two seeds are joined at a shared phrase.* John 4 joins 21:6 to 14:7 at "springs of waters". The wedding joins chapters 17 to 20. So an expansion need not have one source verse, and a test that takes one verse at a time will underrate it: John 4 scores 42 for each seed alone and has four.

The first of these is a fact of vocabulary and the other two are facts of relation; they are parted into six operations at 2V.2. And what was not found: order (the members are repeated, not followed), and any habit of it. It is a shape some passages have, and they are few.

2T.6. **Limits and what is owed.** The blind rate says expansion from Revelation is no commoner than from other books, and that stands beside the four top ranks without contradiction: a few passages, strongly. The named pairs were named by a reader who knows what the Gospels say, so the pairing is informed; the rank is not.
