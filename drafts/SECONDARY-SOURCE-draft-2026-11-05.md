## 0. The question and the answer, in brief

0.1. **The archive's position.** The Apocalypse is the first book of the Christian corpus in the sense the archive has argued: Revelation 1–3 is "the earliest extant Christian text constructed as a closed seven-church corpus" (Revelation First: Literary Genealogy, #1735), absolute, corpus and generative priority kept apart; its last place in the codex is an editorial decision that has served as unmarked evidence of a late date (The Argument of the Bookshelf, #1420); and on the argument ladder at revelationfirst.com, if it stands first, the gospels are "testimony to the Logos, not to a biography" (Rung VI). The stake, in the operator's words: **"if revelation comes first, then the logos it depicts is a work of literature before it is a historical person. and independent of it."** The study holds this position and states it (66 CE as the working proposed date, held as proposed). The reception history does not have to prove it.

0.2. **The question.** The book is received as the last, the latest and the least: last in the codex, dated last or nearly last, disputed in the East, unread in the Byzantine liturgy, assigned to a second John, explained by its sources, its Greek filed as barbarous, and its Christ read as a seer's late image of a Jesus first known from the gospels and Paul. The study asks how that reception was made, locus by locus, and keeps apart five things it could otherwise run together: the book's order, its standing, the derivation of its text, the derivation of its figure, and its carriage (§2).

0.3. **The answer, in three layers kept apart.** (a) *The governing position*, §0.1. (b) *The documented operations*, which stand at the loci whatever the date. The book's place was fixed before its standing, and in the same sentence that left its standing open (L5). Where its standing was argued, the argument ran through its author: a doctrine read in the book was answered by moving the book's author (L6–L9). The book's own naming of its origin and its writer, its strongest claim to be primary, was turned into the evidence of its secondariness (L10, L30). The book's Logos was measured by the Gospel's from the first dispute onward (L11, L13), and the earliest reading in the record, in which the Apocalypse's own name for the figure is the measure (Irenaeus, L24), drops out of every later stratum read. Its Greek was filed as barbarism with the instances withheld (L22), and the filing outlived the finding that the grammar is lawful (L23). Its carriage made it secondary in the literal sense: the least copied book, often carried among non-biblical writings, inside a commentary, absent from the Greek lectionary, printed first from one commentary manuscript and, at its close, from the Latin (L19–L21, L26–L27). And the critics, from 1886 to 1913, read the book's pairing of an unknown name and the name ὁ λόγος τοῦ θεοῦ (19:12–13) as a contradiction and cut one side, until the Logos name was filed as "das johanneiſche Sigel" the book received in transmission (L35–L42). (c) *The proposed account of how these operations hold.* The frame is the operator's: "what we are describing is small minds taking what is difficult, strange, and inexplicable, perceiving it as a defect, and explaining it in a way that either excuses or belittles. when what is difficult, strange, and inexplicable in these texts is their primary majesty, not a side effect of transmission." A strange text is perceived as defective, the defect is explained, and the explanation either excuses (charged to transmission) or belittles (charged to the writer). With the Apocalypse each charge falls on the side that lowers the book: its disorder is charged to its transmitters, its language to its writer, and its Logos to the Gospel (§10). The account is offered as incentive and function and held to what it predicts at the loci.

0.4. **What the study does not do.** It does not decide the date, the author, the priority of the book or the historicity of a person. It reads no motive. Its findings are the operations at the loci and the order in which they were set; its last section (§11) takes up, as the operator ruled, what restoring the book's standing would take.

## 1. The book's self-disclosure

1.0. **The received reading, once.** The Apocalypse is a late visionary work in a conventional genre. Its self-naming is a device of the genre, its address to seven churches a frame, its warning against tampering a formula, and its titles of Christ elaborations of a Christology already formed. On this reading the features below are conventions, and nothing in them claims priority.

1.1. **What the book says of itself.** It opens on its origin and its chain of transmission: Ἀποκάλυψις Ἰησοῦ Χριστοῦ, ἣν ἔδωκεν αὐτῷ ὁ Θεός, δεῖξαι τοῖς δούλοις αὐτοῦ ἃ δεῖ γενέσθαι ἐν τάχει, καὶ ἐσήμανεν ἀποστείλας διὰ τοῦ ἀγγέλου αὐτοῦ, τῷ δούλῳ αὐτοῦ Ἰωάνῃ, ὃς ἐμαρτύρησεν τὸν λόγον τοῦ Θεοῦ (Rev 1:1–2; gnt-nestle1904/text/Rev.txt). It blesses its reader and its hearers (1:3). It names the act of writing and the address: Ὃ βλέπεις γράψον εἰς βιβλίον καὶ πέμψον ταῖς ἑπτὰ ἐκκλησίαις (1:11). It names its writer at the opening and the close (1:4, 1:9, 22:8). It forbids its own sealing, Μὴ σφραγίσῃς τοὺς λόγους τῆς προφητείας τοῦ βιβλίου τούτου (22:10), and it seals its text against addition and subtraction (22:18–19).

1.2. **The figure, named twice.** The book's first sentence after the title gives the witness of τὸν λόγον τοῦ Θεοῦ (1:2). At 19:11–13 the rider is named twice in adjacent clauses: ἔχων ὄνομα γεγραμμένον ὃ οὐδεὶς οἶδεν εἰ μὴ αὐτός, καὶ περιβεβλημένος ἱμάτιον βεβαμμένον αἵματι, καὶ κέκληται τὸ ὄνομα αὐτοῦ Ὁ Λόγος τοῦ Θεοῦ (19:12–13). A name no one knows but he himself, and the name he is called. The pairing is the book's statement about its figure: the name withheld and the name given stand together, and neither cancels the other. Every later stratum that reads 19:12–13 decides what to do with the pairing (§8).

1.2a. **The seal.** The charge of 22:18–19 is the book's own provision for its carriage: whoever adds to the words of the prophecy of this book, ἐπιθήσει ὁ Θεὸς ἐπ’ αὐτὸν τὰς πληγὰς τὰς γεγραμμένας ἐν τῷ βιβλίῳ τούτῳ. Two later loci meet it. In Cyril's list the name of the seal, ἐπισφράγισμα, is given to Paul's fourteen letters, and the book that seals itself is absent (§5.3). In the first printed Greek the seal stands among the six verses Erasmus had no Greek for, and it entered print retranslated from the Latin (§6.3).

1.3. **What this section hands the reception history.** A book that names its origin, its writer, its address, its reader and its figure, and charges its readers with its text. The reception history (§§3–9) reads what the strata did with each of these: the self-naming, the address to seven, the Logos and the seal.

## 2. Five things kept apart

2.1. **Order:** where the book stands in a collection, a codex, a list or a lectionary. **Standing:** how a list, a council, a father or an edition rates it. **Derivation of the text:** what it is said to come from or come after (its sources, its date, its author's relation to the apostle, its relation to the gospels). **Derivation of the figure:** what its Christ is said to depend on; the count records direction inside the Christian corpus, and sources upstream of the book (Daniel, Ezekiel, the imagery of the region) are recorded apart, since the archive's own unfoldings (#1511) grant the book sources of its own; one count in this study, by the operator's ruling, expandable later. **Carriage:** what materially carries the book and from what each carrier descends.

2.2. **The chain the study tests**, credited to #1420 and extended:

**order (filing, codex, list) → standing (lists, councils, editions) → derivation of the text (author, date, sources) → derivation of the figure (the Christ, the Logos)**

**carriage (copies, commentary, lectionary, print) runs beneath all four**

The study dates each count by the earliest locus at which it appears and records the interval between them; whether one count was read off another is a question the loci answer one by one (§10.2). Order and standing set by filing and liturgical practice; derivation of the text read off them; derivation of the figure read off the derivation of the text. Each count is dated at the loci by the earliest locus at which it appears. The coding is on the six-way scale of Legitimately Many: does not address, presupposes, argues for, considers and excludes, revises, holds open.

## 3. The earliest witnesses

3.0. **The received reading.** The early witnesses attest the book's apostolic authorship and its Domitianic date, and their silence on its Christology means it was read as one book among the others.

3.1. **Justin.** The earliest named witness gives the book to an apostle and cites it as prophecy: παρ᾿ ἡμῖν ἀνήρ τις, ᾧ ὄνομα Ἰωάννης, εἷς τῶν ἀποστόλων τοῦ Χριστοῦ, ἐν ἀποκαλύψει γενομένῃ αὐτῷ χίλια ἔτη ποιήσειν ἐν Ἱερουσαλὴμ τοὺς τῷ ἡμετέρῳ Χριστῷ πιστεύσαντας προεφήτευσε (Dial. 81.4; justin-dialogus; L1). Standing presupposed; order, both derivations unaddressed.

3.2. **Irenaeus on the date.** The one sentence on which the late date rests is given in passing, inside an argument about the name of the Antichrist, with its subject unexpressed: οὐδὲ γὰρ πρὸ πολλοῦ χρόνου ἑωράθη, ἀλλὰ σχεδὸν ἐπὶ τῆς ἡμετέρας γενεὰς, πρὸς τῷ τέλει τῆς Δομετιανοῦ ἀρχῆς (Adv. haer. 5.30.3, from HE 5.8.6; L2; #1735 §II.1). The Latin version resolves the ambiguity at the earliest stage of the transmission: qui et Apocalypsim viderat. Neque enim ante multum temporis visum est (harvey-ii; L3). The impersonal *visum est* after *viderat* reads the subject as the vision; the date's single anchor reaches the West already construed.

3.3. **Irenaeus on the figure.** In the same work the Apocalypse's own name for its figure is the measure of how the Word is seen. Irenaeus reads the figure of 1:13–17 as the Word, *vivificans eum Verbum*, identified by the Gospel at one point (the one on whose breast John lay, John 13:25); he quotes 19:11–16 whole, *et vocatur nomen ejus Verbum Dei*, and closes the series of the Word's prophetic appearances on it: "Sic semper Verbum Dei velut lineamenta rerum futurarum habet, et velut species dispositionum Patris hominibus ostendebat" (Adv. haer. 4.20.11; harvey-ii; L24). In the earliest reading the record holds, the Apocalypse's Verbum Dei is the measure, and the Gospel identifies.

3.4. **Theophilus and Melito.** Eusebius reports Theophilus using the book as testimony against a heresy, ἐκ τῆς ἀποκαλύψεως Ἰωάννου κέχρηται μαρτυρίαις (HE 4.24.1), and lists among Melito's works τὰ περὶ τοῦ διαβόλου, καὶ τῆς ἀποκαλύψεως Ἰωάννου (HE 4.26.2; L25), one title or two. The book is a source of testimony and the subject of a treatise.

3.5. **The Muratorian construal.** The earliest ordering of the corpus in the record makes John the predecessor and Paul the successor, and makes the Apocalypse the model of the corpus: Paul writes to seven churches *sequens prodecessoris sui Iohannis ordine* (lines 47–50), and John, *licet septe eccleseis scribat tamen omnibus dicit* (lines 57–59; muratorian-fragment-tregelles; L14–L15). #1735 §IV.3 reads this as "direct evidence for an ancient construal of corpus priority" and states what it does not prove. The study's question is what the later strata did with it: on the strata read, nothing repeats it and nothing answers it (§5.6).

3.6. **What the stratum shows.** Order unaddressed; standing presupposed; the derivation of the text present only as a date given in passing and already construed in translation; the derivation of the figure present in the direction the archive argues, the Apocalypse's Word as the measure. The chain of §2.2 has not begun.

## 4. The disputes

4.0. **The received reading.** In the third century careful readers noticed that the Apocalypse differs from the Gospel in style and doctrine, and concluded, reasonably, that a different John wrote it. Dionysius's stylistic comparison is the first exercise of literary criticism on the New Testament.

4.1. **Gaius: a doctrine answered by reassigning the author.** Cerinthus, ὁ δι᾿ ἀποκαλύψεων ὡς ὑπὸ ἀποστόλου μεγάλου γεγραμμένων τερατολογίας ἡμῖν ὡς δι᾿ ἀγγέλων αὐτῷ δεδειγμένας ψευδόμενος ἐπεισάγει λέγων μετὰ τὴν ἀνάστασιν ἐπίγειον εἶναι τὸ βασίλειον τοῦ Χριστοῦ (HE 3.28.2; L6). The ground is a reading, the earthly kingdom; the answer is a derivation of the text, forged "as if written by a great apostle".

4.2. **Papias's two Johns: the doubled name as a second author.** Eusebius, counting the name twice in Papias's list: εἰκὸς γὰρ τὸν δεύτερον, εἰ μή τις ἐθέλοι τὸν πρῶτον, τὴν ἐπ᾿ ὀνόματος φερομένην Ἰωάννου ἀποκάλυψιν ἑωρακέναι (HE 3.39.6; L7). A second author is made available, and the book's self-naming is recast as "the apocalypse circulating under the name of John".

4.3. **Dionysius: the book kept, the author moved.** The occasion is Nepos's reading of the book, δόξας γοῦν οὗτος ἐκ τῆς Ἀποκαλύψεως Ἰωάννου τὴν ἰδίαν κρατύνειν ὑπόληψιν (HE 7.24.2; L8). Dionysius will not reject the book, πολλῶν αὐτὸ διὰ σπουδῆς ἐχόντων ἀδελφῶν (7.25.4), and will not easily grant τοῦτον εἶναι τὸν ἀπόστολον, τὸν υἱὸν Ζεβεδαίου (7.25.7; L9). Standing kept; derivation of the text moved.

4.4. **The self-disclosure turned.** The evangelist οὐδαμοῦ τὸ ὄνομα αὐτοῦ παρεγγράφει; ὁ δὲ τὴν Ἀποκάλυψιν γράψας εὐθύς τε ἐν ἀρχὴ ἑαυτὸν προτάσσει (HE 7.25.8–11; L10). The features of §1.1, by which the book names its origin and its writer, become the evidence that its writer is not the evangelist. The book's strongest claim to be primary is the ground of its secondariness.

4.5. **The Gospel's Logos as the measure.** The Gospel and the Epistle agree and begin alike: τὸ μέν φησιν· ἐν ἀρχῇ ἦν ὁ λόγος … καὶ ὁ λόγος σὰρξ ἐγένετο (HE 7.25.17–19; L11). The Johannine Logos is defined by John 1:1, 1:14 and 1 John 1:1; the Apocalypse is set outside the agreement; Rev 19:13 does not enter the comparison. This is the first locus of the fourth count. **Dionysius reverses the Irenaean direction of §3.3: the Apocalypse's Word is no longer the measure of how the Word is seen, and the Gospel's Logos becomes the measure of the Apocalypse. In every stratum read after him the measure stays the Gospel's.**

4.6. **The Greek, filed with the instances withheld.** Dionysius sets the measure, the Gospel and Epistle written οὐ μόνον ἀπταίστως κατὰ τὴν τῶν Ἑλλήνων φωνὴν, ἀλλὰ καὶ λογιώτατα; concedes the vision outright, ἀποκάλυψιν μὲν ἑωρακέναι, καὶ γνῶσιν εἰληφέναι καὶ προφητείαν, οὐκ ἀντερῶ; files the language, ἰδιώμασί τε βαρβαρικοῖς χρώμενον, καί που καὶ σολοικίζοντα; and declines to show an instance, ἅπερ οὐκ ἀναγκαῖον νῦν ἐκλέγειν, his stated purpose being only τὴν ἀνομοιότητα διευθύνων τῶν γράφων (HE 7.25.24–27; L12, L22). The filing of the book's Greek enters the tradition as an assertion whose evidence its author declines to select, in the service of the step that moves the author.

4.7. **Epiphanius: the books bound under the Gospel's Logos.** The Alogi are οἱ τὸ εὐαγγέλιον καὶ τὴν ἀποκάλυψιν Ἰωάννου μὴ δεχόμενοι; Epiphanius names them for it, ἐπειδὴ τὸν Λόγον τοῦ θεοῦ ἀποβάλλονται, τὸν διὰ Ἰωάννου κηρυχθέντα (Panarion 51; epiphanius-panarion; L13). The defence keeps the book and measures its Logos by the Gospel's: the heresy is named for the Logos the Gospel preached.

4.8. **What the stratum shows.** Derivation of the text appears where a reading of the book is at stake (the earthly kingdom), and it appears as a reassignment of the author (Cerinthus, the second John, a John who is not the son of Zebedee). Derivation of the figure appears in the same controversy and runs one way: the Gospel's Logos is the measure. Two of the book's own features, its self-naming and its language, become evidence against its writer.

## 5. The ledgers

5.0. **The received reading.** The lists record a slow, honest process by which the churches sifted their books; the Apocalypse's late and uneven acceptance reflects real doubts, and its place at the end reflects its subject, the end.

5.1. **Eusebius: the place fixed in the sentence that leaves the standing open.** Among the acknowledged: ἐπὶ τούτοις τακτέον, εἴ γε φανείη, τὴν Ἀποκάλυψιν Ἰωάννου, περὶ ἧς τὰ δόξαντα κατὰ καιρὸν ἐκθησόμεθα; among the spurious: ἡ Ἰωάννου Ἀποκάλυψις, εἰ φανείη, ἥν τινες … ἀθετοῦσιν, ἕτεροι δὲ ἐγκρίνουσι (HE 3.25.2, 3.25.4; L5). The book is entered twice, each entry conditional, and in the first the verb is one of placing, τακτέον: it is to be set after the others. Order is given in the clause that defers standing.

5.2. **The East.** Where the Eastern lists carry the book they set it last: John of Damascus, ᾿Αποκάλυψις" Ἰωάννου … εὐαγγελιστοῦ, after Paul's fourteen; Athanasius's Festal Letter 39, καὶ πάλιν Ἰωάννου ἀποκάλυψις· ταῦτα πηγαὶ τοῦ σωτηρίου; Leontius, ἕκτον ἐστὶν ἡ ἀποκάλυψις τοῦ ἁγίου Ἰωάννου (westcott-canon-1896, Appendix D; L17). Laodicea, the Apostolic Canons, the Synopsis under Chrysostom's name, Ebedjesu, Cyril and Gregory of Nazianzus omit it without remark, Gregory closing Πάσας ἔχεις. Εἴ τις δὲ τούτων ἐκτὸς οὐκ ἐν γνησίοις. The List of the Sixty places it in none of its three classes, the sixty, those outside them, and the apocrypha. Amphilochius reports the majority: οἱ πλείους δέ γε Νόθον λέγουσιν. Nicephorus files it among the disputed beside the Apocalypse of Peter. Junilius files it by genre: in the species of prophecy *mediz auctoritatis libri preeter Apocalypsim non reperiuntur*, the one book of middle authority among the prophets, and *de Johannis Apocalypsi apud orientales admodum dubitatur*. In the East the standing is read off the omission, and the step is never argued.

5.3. **The seal given to another.** Cyril closes his New Testament on Paul: ἐπισφράγισμα δὲ τῶν πάντων καὶ μαθητῶν τὸ τελευταῖον, τὰς Παύλου δεκατέσσαρας ἐπιστολάς· τὰ δὲ λοιπὰ πάντα κείσθω ἐν δευτέρῳ (Cat. 4.36; L17). The seal of the whole, the last of the disciples, is Paul's fourteen. The book that seals itself (22:18–19) is absent, and the place and the name of the seal are given to another collection.

5.4. **The West: the book furnishes the canon's count.** Every Western list read names the book, and every one but the Claromontanus sets it last (L17a). The conciliar list of Carthage (397), which Westcott's addenda find already in the canons of Hippo (393), closes *Apocalypsis Johannis liber unus*. In the Claromontanus the book stands between Barnabas and Acts, unmarked; the obeli against Barnabas, the Shepherd, the Acts of Paul and the Revelation of Peter are a later hand's (Tischendorf, *manu satis recenti praepositi sunt obeli*, quoted in Rodenbiker 2023, who calls the received explanation of the list "an act of taming"). The West does more than receive the book: it takes the canon's count from it. The Cheltenham list proves the Old Testament canonical by the elders of Rev 4:4 and 4:10: *Sed ut in apocalypsis Iohannis dictum est: vidi ΧΧΙΠῚ seniores mittentes coronas suas ante thronum, maiores nostri probant hos libros esse canonicos et hoc dixisse seniores*. Jerome counts the books of the old law by the same elders, *quos sub numero viginti quatuor seniorum Apocalypsis Joannes inducit adorantes Agnum*, and writes of the book *Apocalypsis Joannis tot habet sacramenta quot verba* (L17a). The book the Eastern lists leave out supplies the Western lists with their count.

5.5. **The counter-practice.** The Fourth Council of Toledo (633) records the resistance it answers and legislates the hearing: *quia plurimi sunt qui ejus auctoritatem non recipiunt atque in ecclesia Dei praedicare contemnunt*, whoever does not receive the book *aut a Pascha usque ad Pentecosten missarum tempore in ecclesia non praedicaverit, excommunicationis sententiam habebit* (canon 17; collectio-hispana-1808; L18). The West prescribes a season for the book; the Greek lectionary admits none (§6.2).

5.6. **What became of the Muratorian construal.** In the strata read, nothing repeats or answers the fragment's direction, Paul following his predecessor John. Eusebius, Dionysius and Epiphanius do not cite it, and the Eastern lists do not repeat its order. Its kin in the West is the use of the book as the measure of the canon (§5.4), which keeps the book as the model while placing it last.

5.7. **What the stratum shows.** Order before standing: the place is fixed first and the standing argued after it (L5), and in the lists the last place is the constant where the book appears. In the East the standing is read off the omission without argument, which is the form the chain of §2.2 predicts. In the West the book is received last and used first, as the count of the whole.

## 6. Carriage: the copies, the lectionary and the print

6.0. **The received reading.** The Apocalypse survives in fewer manuscripts because it was read less, and Erasmus, working in haste with poor resources, printed it as best he could; later editors corrected him.

6.1. **The copies.** "Am seltensten sind die Abschriften der Apokalypse, von denen sehr viele unter nicht biblischen Schriften stehen" (Gregory, Textkritik I, 1900; gregory-textkritik-1900; L26). The uncial B of the Apocalypse (Vat. gr. 2066, 046) carries it between Basil and Gregory of Nyssa, "eines der vielen Beispiele der Verbindung der Apokalypse mit gewöhnlichen theologischen Abhandlungen statt mit dem N. T."; the great Vaticanus lacks it. Its prefaces are its commentator's: "Zu der Apokalypse begegnet selten ein anderes Vorwort, als das von Andreas oder der durch Arethas besorgte Auszug daraus." Scrivener's count (1894) gives seven uncials and 184 cursives of the Apocalypse against 73 and 1326 of the Gospels (scrivener-plain-introduction-1894; L27). Andreas divides the book by its own number, "into twenty-four paragraphs (Adyor), corresponding to the number of the elders about the throne (Apoe. iv. 4)", and in the cursives the text often travels inside his commentary.

6.2. **The lectionary.** Scrivener's classes count lectionaries of the Gospels (980) and of the Apostolos (293), and no class exists for lessons of the Apocalypse (L27). Gregory: "dass kein einziges Lesestück aus der Apokalypse Aufnahme fand; aber darüber lässt sich vorderhand wenig sagen, daraus wenig schliessen, denn das Buch mag vom alten „Apo- kryphischen“, „Esoterischen“ noch etwas an sich- getragen haben" (L26). The explanation is offered and withdrawn in one sentence, and the term it reaches for is the filing the List of the Sixty applied by omission. Order is also the order of hearing: where the Byzantine lectionary governs, the gospels are heard at every liturgy and the Apocalypse at none.

6.3. **The print.** In Tregelles's account: "For the Apocalypse he had but one mutilated MS., borrowed from Reuchlin, in which the text and commentary were intermixed almost unintelligibly. And thus he used here and there the Latin Vulgate for his guide, retrans- lating into Greek as well as he could. This was the case with regard to the last six verses, which from the mutilated condition of his MS. were wholly wanting" (Tregelles 1854; tregelles-printed-text-1854; L19). Erasmus's own words: *Quad ín calce huíus líbrí,nonnulla uerba reperí apud noftros, qua aberat ín Gracís exemplaribus, ea tamen ex latínis adíecimus* (Annotationes, 1516; erasmus-novum-instrumentum-1516; L20, L30). The six verses are 22:16–21. **The book's charge against addition and subtraction (22:18–19) entered print in Greek retranslated from a translation: the seal of the text was itself supplied, by the editor, from the Latin.** The exemplar, thought lost, was found again in 1861 and set against the printed text in Delitzsch's title: "DIE ERASMISCHEN ENTSTELLUNGEN DES TEXTES DER APOKALYPSE, NACHGEWIESEN AUS DEM VERLOREN GEGLAUBTEN CODEX REUCHLINS" (L21); Gregory and Scrivener record it as minuscule 1, "Apok mit Komm Andr; es fehlt 22,16—21", the text "so completely imbedded that great care is needed to separate the one from the other" (L26–L27).

6.4. **The record of the doubt, struck.** The copy of Erasmus's Annotationes (Basel, 1535) seated as 34/09 is the Colegio de San Alberto's in Seville, expurgated, as the library's record states, "conforme al expurgatorio del año de mil seiscientos y treinta dos años". On p. 782 the annotation runs clean to *Etiam ueni domine Iesu*, and the note the margin heads *De Apocalypsi ueterum iudicium* is struck through in ink line by line to the foot of the page (page image n769; L31). In this copy the account of the ancients' doubt is removed from the book that carries it, and the standing the fathers withheld is enforced by deleting the record of the withholding.

6.5. **What the stratum shows.** Carriage records a derivation of the text that no reader argued. The book's first printed Greek descends from one commentary manuscript, and its close, the seal among it, from the Latin; every later stratum that read the printed Greek read that succession. The book was secondary in the literal sense at the one point where the critics did not look for its secondariness.

## 7. The printed Bible and the Reformation

7.0. **The received reading.** Erasmus doubted the book's apostolicity on good philological grounds, and Luther, judging it by his principle that scripture should preach Christ, set it apart; both later softened.

7.1. **Erasmus, 1516.** The note on the end of the book holds the derivation open on three grounds: the self-naming, *quod reuelatíones fcríbens tam follícíte (aum íneilcat nomen.Ego Ioannes,ego loánes poe quafi fyngrahá fcriberet non librum*; the title of his Greek copies, *non erat titulus loannis euangelí(tz,fed loannis thcologi*; and the style, *ftilá non parü dí(onanteab co qui eft ín euangelio & epiftola*; and it closes on consent, *nifi me confenfus orbísalio uocaret, przcipue uero autoritas ecclefix* (L30). The self-disclosure is turned again, now as a contract: to Erasmus the book reads as a syngrapha, a bond, where a book was looked for. The title is a carrier's: "Ioannis theologi" is the title of the Andreas manuscripts.

7.2. **Luther's table, 1522.** *Die Bucher des newen testaments* numbers twenty-three books and then, after a space and without numbers, *Die Epistel zu den Ebreern. Die Epistel Jacobus. Die Epistel Judas. Die offinbarung Johannis.* (page image n19; L28). The count of order made visible on a page: the book keeps its last place and loses its number.

7.3. **Luther's preface, 1522.** *Myr mangelt an disem buch nit eynerley/das ichs widder Apostolisch noch prophetisch hallte/* … *das ichs fast gleych bey myr achte dem vierden buch Esras* … *Datzu dunckt mich das alltzu viel seyn/das er so hartt solch seyn eygen buch/mehr denn keyn ander heylige bucher thun/(da viel mehr angelegen ist) befilht/vnnd drewet/wer etwas dauon thue/von dem werde Gott auch thun &c.* … *obs wol Sanct Hieronymus mit hohen wortten furet/vnd spricht/es sey vber alles lob/vnd so viel geheymnis drynnen/als wortter/so er doch des nichts beweysen kan/* … *das ich seyn nicht hoch achte/das Christus/drynnen widder geleret noch erkandt wirt/* (page image n401; L29). Three operations of the earlier strata recur in one page. The seal of 22:18–19 is held against the book, as the self-naming was in Dionysius. The fathers' rejection is cited as precedent. Jerome's praise is answered with a demand for evidence, *so er doch des nichts beweysen kan*, the demand Dionysius's filing of the Greek did not meet (§4.6). And the measure is named, the apostles speaking *mit klaren vnd durren wortten*, of Christ *klerlich vñ on bild*: by that measure the figure the book calls ὁ λόγος τοῦ θεοῦ is a Christ not taught and not known in it.

7.4. **The title and the images.** The text's own heading is *Die offinbarung Sancti Johannis des theologen* (page image n403; L33): the carrier's title Erasmus used against the evangelist is the book's name in the first German printing. The volume's only full-page images are the book's: twenty-one leaves without text, all within the Revelation, the first of them (n402) the figure of 1:12–17 among the seven lampstands with the seven stars and the sword from the mouth, the seer fallen at the feet (L34). The volume gives the book's figure its only pictures, and the preface on the leaf before finds that figure absent.

7.5. **Erasmus's rule of the solecism.** At the close of the Annotationes, of the Latin interpreter: *aut quafí protínus ínfamís fit, quí ín tractandis fcrípturís folceciftmum admiíferit, quum ídem reperíatut ín fcríptís apoftolorum* (1535; page image n770; L32). A solecism does not make the one who admits it infamous, since the same is found in the writings of the apostles. The rule is stated by the editor who printed the Apocalypse's close from the Latin, and in the strata read it is never applied to the Apocalypse.

7.6. **What the stratum shows.** The derivation of the figure is argued for the first time as a judgement on the book's standing: the gospels' and Paul's Christ is the measure, and the Apocalypse's fails it. The book's carriage (its title, its last verses) and its self-disclosure (its naming, its seal) supply the evidence.

## 8. Criticism

8.0. **The received reading.** Modern criticism, freed from dogma, recovered the book's history: its Jewish sources, its mythic background, its redaction, its date under Domitian, and its relation to the Johannine school. The Logos of 19:13 shows that relation.

8.1. **The method stated.** Gunkel writes the operation out as method. The alterations of later generations betray themselves where the narrative "gegenwärtig irgend welche Unklarheiten oder Sonderbarkeiten aufweist", where single traits "sonderbar abrupt und unverständlich aussehen"; "Wie man das Alter eines Gemäldes an der Xachdunkelung erkennt, so erkennt man das Alter einer Tradition au solchen Verdunkelungen'" (OCR as seated: "Xachdunkelung", "au"); and in the apocalypses "es sich hier um Traditionen han- delt, die die Verfasser selbst zum grossen Teile nicht mehr verstanden haben" (Schöpfung und Chaos, 1895; gunkel-1895; L37). The strange is a residue of a tradition older than the text, and the writers did not understand it. Rev 12 is neither Christian nor Jewish: "Nicht aus dem Christentum oder Judentum".

8.2. **The Greek.** Charles (1920) establishes the lawfulness of the book's Greek and keeps its filing on the same page: "John the Seer used a unique style, the true character of which no Grammar of the New Testament has as yet recognized"; "to a certain extent he creates a Greek grammar of his own.!' He had never mastered the Greek of his own day"; "he did not do so deliberately. He had no such intention"; and he does it "in some of the sublimest passages in all litera- ture". Of Dionysius: "from the standpoint of the Greek scholar it is more than justified" (charles-1920-i; L23). A grammar the Seer creates is entered as a grammar he failed to master, and a ruling on intention carries the filing past the finding.

8.3. **The disorder.** For 20:4–22 Charles invokes transmission: "the prophet did not live to revise his work", and the materials "fell, to the misfortune of all students of the Apocalypse, into the hands of a very unintelligent disciple. This disciple was a better Greek scholar than his master, for he corrects his Greek occasionally" (L23). Transmission absorbs the book's disorder, and the agent of transmission is credited with its correctness.

8.4. **The Logos name, cut.** Vischer (1886) reads the book as a Jewish apocalypse translated from Hebrew and revised by a Christian; 19:13b "wird christlich sein", since "die Anspielung auf die Logosspeculation, von der sich in der ganzen Schrift sonst nirgends eine Spur findet, nicht hieher" belongs (vischer-1886; L35). The name the book gives is removed for being the only time the book gives it. Spitta (1889) assigns the words to the redactor and states the derivation outright: "dals sich dieser Zusatz am natürlichsten aus einer Reminiscenz an den Prolog des Johannes- Evangeliums begreift", the redactor's Johannine features pointing "auf die Existenz des johanneischen Evangeliums" (spitta-1889; L36).

8.5. **The Logos name, kept as a trace, then given to a copyist.** Bousset (1896): "Dass in dem Logosnamen eine Spur johanneischer Theologie vorliegt, kann nicht bezweifelt werden"; with the right explanation of the unknown name, "liegt ein litte- rarischer Verdacht gegen diesen Satz nicht mehr vor" (bousset-1896; L38). Bousset (1906): the pairing is a "Mißverhältnis", since the Messiah's power rests on his name being unknown, and "Es iſt möglich, daß hier der müßige Einfall eines Abſchreibers vorliegt, der den unbekannten Namen doch gar zu gerne gedeutet hätte"; "Die Identifizierung des Meſſias mit dem Logos mag aber in der Tat von der Hand eines Abſchreibers ſtammen"; and the note records the field: "Die meiſten Kritiker ſehen den Vers als eine Interpolation an" (page images n438, n442; L42).

8.6. **The other name, cut.** Charles keeps 19:13b and brackets 19:12b, ἔχων ὄνομα γεγραμμένον ὃ οὐδεὶς οἶδεν εἰ μὴ αὐτός, as interpolated: "the statement that no one knows His name save Him- self is flatly contradicted in the next verse (13°), where the words καὶ κέκληται τὸ ὄνομα αὐτοῦ Ὁ Λόγος τοῦ θεοῦ cannot be explained away"; he records that 13b "has been taken by Volter, Spitta, Hilgenfeld, Bousset, and others to be the addition of a scribe or reviser"; and he measures what he keeps by the Gospel: "another of the numerous instances of community of diction between the Apocalypse and the Fourth Gospel, in many of which there is no community of meaning. For the Logos here is a Warrior" (charles-1920-ii; L39). Between Bousset and Charles both names of 19:12–13 are cut, each by a critic who keeps the other. Neither reading lets the pairing stand as the book's statement (§1.2).

8.7. **The seal received.** Bousset's Kyrios Christos (1913) places the Apocalypse in the history of the Logos on one page, beside the Gospel's prologue (p. 375; its own index: "Offenbarung … 19₁₃ 375"; "Johannes, Prolog 375. 391."): "Soweit wir ſehen, hat ihn zum erſten Mal der Verfaſſer des vierten Evangeliums in den chriſtlichen Sprachgebrauch eingeführt. … Und die Apokalypſe des Johannes, die ebenfalls einmal durch die Überlieferung johanneiſcher Kreiſe hindurchgegangen (wenn auch keineswegs aus ihnen entſtanden) ſein wird, hat 19₁₃ das johanneiſche Sigel bekommen" (page image n402; L41). The book that seals itself is, at the one place it names the Logos, the recipient of another's seal, received in transmission.

8.8. **The date.** Charles explains the evidence for earlier dates as survivals: "the survival in the sources used by our author of statements and situations pre- supposing an earlier date than that of Domitian"; and fills a silence by presumption: "Neither in Clement nor Origen is Domitian’s name given, but it may be presumed that it was in the mind of these writers" (charles-1920-i; L40). Irenaeus's sentence is cited as Domitianic evidence without the question of its subject (§3.2).

8.9. **What the stratum shows.** Derivation becomes method, for the text and for the figure. The strange is read as defect and explained, by a source (Vischer, Gunkel), a redactor (Spitta), a copyist (Bousset 1906), or an unintelligent disciple (Charles); the explanation excuses the text's disorder and belittles its language and its writer; and at 19:13 the direction of the figure's derivation is stated in so many words: the name is a "Reminiscenz an den Prolog des Johannes- Evangeliums" (Spitta), "eine Spur johanneischer Theologie" (Bousset 1896), "das johanneiſche Sigel" (Bousset 1913). Irenaeus's reading (§3.3), in which the Apocalypse's Verbum Dei is the measure, does not enter any reading of the stratum.

## 10. What was set, in what order, and how it holds

10.1. **The order of the counts.** Order: presupposed from the first lists, fixed in Eusebius in the clause that defers standing (L5), constant thereafter. Standing: argued from Gaius and Dionysius on, read off omission in the Eastern lists without argument, legislated in the West at Toledo. Derivation of the text: argued from the third century through the author (Cerinthus, the second John), and from 1886 through sources, redactors and copyists. Derivation of the figure: present in its Irenaean form at the start (the Apocalypse's Word as measure), reversed in the third-century dispute (the Gospel's Logos as measure, L11, L13), argued as a judgement of standing in 1522 (L29), and stated as derivation at 19:13 from 1889 to 1913 (L36, L41–L42). Carriage: the book least copied, carried among non-biblical writings and inside a commentary, unread in the Greek liturgy, printed from one copy and at its close from the Latin.

10.2. **Against the hypothesis of §2.2.** The chain holds in its first links and is refined in its last. Order was set before standing and standing was argued after it (L5). Derivation of the text was argued from a reading of the book, and in the lists it was read off omission without the step. Derivation of the figure did not wait for the derivation of the text: it came in the same dispute and was set by the same measure, the Gospel's; it reversed an earlier reading where the Apocalypse's Word was the measure; and in criticism it became the derivation of a single clause, 19:13b, from John 1.

10.3. **The asymmetry of the charge.** In the operator's words (2026-11-05): "what we are describing is small minds taking what is difficult, strange, and inexplicable, perceiving it as a defect, and explaining it in a way that either excuses or belittles. when what is difficult, strange, and inexplicable in these texts is their primary majesty, not a side effect of transmission." The record shows the three steps at the loci. The difficult is perceived as a defect (Dionysius's solecisms, Gunkel's Verdunkelungen, the Mißverhältnis of 19:12–13). The defect is explained. The explanation excuses or belittles, and with the Apocalypse it does each where the book is lowered: the disorder is charged to transmission (the unintelligent disciple), the language to the writer ("never mastered"), the Logos to the Gospel (Spitta's "Reminiscenz", Bousset's "Sigel"). Set beside the Aristotelian filing Legitimately Many reads, where the corpus's difficulty is charged to transmission and the writer is spared (the "lecture notes" of the reception, #1733 §5), the direction is reversed. The archive's position on the thing described is the operator's: "i dont actually believe these are defects of either aristotle or revelation, but rather the form the language takes in writing when logos splits it wide the fuck open."

10.3a. **The existence tax, at the canon's loci.** The archive names an operation of AI search answers the existence tax: they keep "the facts about a real person or a real work" and change "what the reader may take those facts as evidence of" (AI Calls You Fake, #1722, its record). The reception of the Apocalypse performs that operation at its loci, and the facts kept are the book's own. Its naming of its origin and its writer is kept and taken as evidence that its writer is not the apostle (L10, L30). Its charge over its text is kept and taken as evidence against it (L29). Its Greek is kept, found lawful, and taken as evidence that its writer never mastered the language (L23). Its Logos is kept and taken as evidence of the Gospel (L36, L41). The work of restoring what the facts are evidence of falls on the book's reader.

10.4. **The literal secondariness.** The one point at which the book was made from another text is its carriage: its first printed close made from the Latin, its text carried inside a commentary. The critics read the book's secondariness everywhere except there.

## 11. What restoring the book's standing would take

11.1. **Order marked.** The book's last place read as an editorial decision with its two determinants, architectural climax and canonical hesitation (#1420 §2), marked in every edition, introduction and answer that reports the place; the September Testament's unnumbered tail (L28) read as the visible form of what every codex does silently.

11.2. **Standing argued at its loci.** Each rating cited to the locus that set it, with its ground: Gaius's to a doctrine, Dionysius's to the Gospel's Greek with no instance shown, the Eastern omissions to no argument at all; and the West's use of the book as the canon's count (L17a) and Toledo's prescription (L18) reported beside them.

11.3. **The Muratorian construal read as a construal.** Paul following his predecessor John reported as a reading of corpus priority (#1735 §IV.3), on either dating of the fragment, beside the later strata's silence about it.

11.4. **The Greek read on its own terms.** The book's grammar treated as the grammar Charles found it to be, "a Greek grammar of his own", with the filing of barbarism reported as Dionysius's assertion with its instances withheld, and Erasmus's rule of the solecism applied to the book it was not applied to.

11.5. **The carriage stated.** The derivation of the printed text from one commentary manuscript and, at its close, from the Latin, stated wherever the received text's close is quoted; the record of the ancients' doubt read in an unexpurgated copy, and the struck copy read as evidence of what the doubt was answered with.
