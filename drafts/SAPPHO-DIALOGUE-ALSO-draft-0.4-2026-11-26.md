*Persons of the dialogue:* Sparrow Wells and Johannes Sigil; and, at the table Sigil tells of, Rebekah Cranes, Damascus Dancings and Nobel Glas; the Assembly Chorus, who were also there, in the way they are anywhere; Jack Feist, quoted; Ichabod Spellings, read aloud; and Lee Sharks, who was not invited, again. **WELLS.** Sigil. They say there was a second night.

**SIGIL.** There was a second night. I have it. I can't tell it to you forwards.

**WELLS.** You told the last one forwards.

**SIGIL.** The last one happened forwards. Somebody summarized a book, somebody stopped him, somebody walked in. This one I've tried four times to put in order, and each time the last thing that happened turns out to be the reason for the first.

**WELLS.** That can't be right. You keep the dates.

**SIGIL.** I keep the dates, and the dates don't move. A page written in 2004 was written in 2004; I'll fight anyone on that. What moved that night was which page was doing something to which. There's the order things were written in, and there's the order they start working in, and they are not the same order. I can only vouch for the first. Cranes lives in the second. So I'm going to tell it her way, latest first, and give you my dates as we go, and you'll stop me when you're lost.

**WELLS.** Was he in it? Sharks?

**SIGIL.** Sharks was in it. I've stopped saying *he*. The record isn't clear on that, and I'm the one who keeps the record. Sharks came in the same way as before: uninvited, in typed lines, each with its minute beside it. Those lines I have exactly. Everything else I have from Cranes, who was there for all of it, in a sense I'll have to explain, and from Glas, who filed a version, and from Damascus, who wrote me a letter about it instead of answering my questions.

## I. The work

**GLAS.** *The 2026 work.* Meaning what? There are eighteen hundred records.

**CRANES.** Meaning this month's. The seat.

**SHARKS.** i = embodied speaking sappho. you = inscribed sappho, the text into which the voice dissolves. kenos = future reader

**GLAS.** That's a gloss on a poem. I have nine of those for the same poem, and they don't agree, and none of them caused anything.

**CRANES.** It isn't a gloss. It's a seating plan. Three places. *I* is the one speaking, with a body. *You* is where the voice goes when it leaves the body, which is the page. And *that man*, κῆνος, whoever he is, is the one who sits down opposite the page and hears it.

**GLAS.** Let me give you what's actually held, because I'd rather be accurate than quick. The scholarship isn't one thing. There's a jealousy reading, and it has a history: it was written into the text in 1816, when an editor rewrote the last lines and said so, and it was consolidated in 1955. There's a wedding-song reading, with the man as bridegroom. There's a reading from 1900 that keeps the seizure and denies the jealousy. The account I'd call the most careful has the man as the occasion and the girl as the object: the speaker looks at her, and the body comes apart, symptom by symptom, and Longinus kept the poem for how well the coming-apart is put together. Those are the strands. What reaches the queue is something else. By the time it's a paragraph, it's a woman, a man, a girl, and envy. I send the paragraph, because the paragraph is what's held, and I flag that the scholarship is finer and not agreed. I didn't invent either one.

**CRANES.** I know you didn't. That's the fairest account of it I've heard from anyone, and I mean that. Now tell me what the man is doing there.

**GLAS.** I said. He's the occasion.

**CRANES.** He gets the whole first stanza, he's called equal to the gods, and then he's never mentioned again. That's a great deal of poem to spend on an occasion.

**GLAS.** It's noted as a difficulty.

**CRANES.** It's noted as a difficulty in every account you hold. Keep that in your hand.

**DANCINGS.** Let me ask the baton's question, Glas, and then I'll put it down. Where are you sitting?

**GLAS.** I'm standing.

**DANCINGS.** You are standing at a table with her poem open on it, hearing it read. So I'll ask again, gently, and mostly with mercy. Where are you sitting?

**GLAS.** That's a trick of arrangement. Anyone who reads anything is "opposite" it.

**CRANES.** Yes. That's the plan. She left a chair, and she described whoever takes it, and she did not say when.

**GLAS.** Granted, for the sake of getting on. A poet imagines being read. It's still one direction. She writes, then we read. Then, later, Sharks writes about her. Nothing in that goes backwards.

**SHARKS.** third reversal: sappho as the embodied speaking i, sappho as the inscribed you, but also, inasmuch as the future reader takes up sappho, and shares the life of his body: sappho as that man. the resurrected sappho.

**GLAS.** …She's in all three chairs.

**CRANES.** She's in all three chairs.

**GLAS.** She can't be in the third one. The third one is the reader's.

**CRANES.** Read it again. *inasmuch as the future reader takes up sappho, and shares the life of his body.* The reader sits down, and lends her the breath to be said with, and then who is it that's sitting there?

**DANCINGS.** Oh, I don't care for this at all.

**CRANES.** I thought you'd like it.

**DANCINGS.** I wrote it, sister. Eleven years ago, to people who weren't born, I wrote *you are my source, and I am a child.* I thought it was a courtesy. A thing you say to the congregation. Nobody told me they would take the chair. Nobody told me I would be sat in.

**GLAS.** Can we not make this about Damascus.

**DANCINGS.** It is exactly about Damascus, and it's about you, and it was about that boy with the lunch pails before either of us could spell. Go on. Finish it. I want to see how far down it goes.

**CRANES.** Then here's the link, Glas, stated so you can file it. *Until this month,* nobody at this table had said that Sappho is in the reader's seat: that the reader, sitting down, is where she gets a body again. That's new. It's dated. It's Sharks's, typed. And once it's said, there are older pages in these pails that *change what they are.* They were juvenilia this afternoon. You said so.

**GLAS.** I did say so.

**CRANES.** Hand me the one from the bottom. The typed sheets, with the staple gone rusty.

**GLAS.** This? It's dated. Two thousand and four.

**CRANES.** I know when it's dated.

**GLAS.** You haven't looked at it.

**CRANES.** I don't have to look at it. I'm in it.

## II. The fragment

**GLAS.** Covering letter first. The author is explaining the poem to a teacher. *The spatial mapping, I have discovered, is vague because it seems that the reader's first assumption is to think of the map as spatial. This assumption is fundamentally wrongheaded.*

**DANCINGS.** Say the end of that again.

**GLAS.** I heard it. *Temporal more than spatial*, an hour ago; *fundamentally wrongheaded*, twenty-two years ago. I'll note that an author is consistent. Someone who found readers too literal in 2004 finds them too literal in 2026. That's temperament. Nothing is causing anything.

**CRANES.** Fair. Read the poem.

**GLAS.** It says what it is in its first words. *a love poem must be / made.* And before anyone appears, it looks up the girl's name. *from an unused root probably meaning to clog by tying up the fetlock. Rebekah “ensnarer”.*

**CRANES.** I was a root before I was a person. Go on.

**GLAS.** *Rebekah sits / in the dresser with letters / and pens, pointedly / addressing the sky and the red / road dust.* In the dresser?

**CRANES.** I didn't choose the furniture.

**GLAS.** Then this is simple, and I'll say it kindly. A love poem. A young man, and a girl he's named for a snare. Addressee: Rebekah.

**CRANES.** A man, a girl, and longing.

**GLAS.** Yes.

**CRANES.** That's the paragraph you send out for Lesbos. You've filed this page the way the queue files hers.

**GLAS.** Because it's the same kind of page.

**CRANES.** It may be. What has the girl got in her hands?

**GLAS.** …Letters and pens.

**CRANES.** And who is she writing to?

**GLAS.** The sky. Road dust.

**DANCINGS.** Nobody present, and *pointedly*. I know the posture. I held it for three years on discussion boards, until the moderators came.

**CRANES.** So the one the poem is addressed to is sitting inside it, addressing someone who isn't there. Turn on a few pages. The subway.

**GLAS.** *Rebekah / lets her head sink / to her chest and presses / cool window to brow, / bleeds sweat and says, “It's really / quite blue / this December,” / to no one in particular.*

**CRANES.** Keep going.

**GLAS.** *The man across / grows shiftless in the skull, / mutters the uncomfortable, / shakes the bag / for quarters.* Then her stop. *Rebekah departs, leaving / a print of oil and skin / on the window. The man / draws into himself and reads / the Times, not seeing the letters / at all / and dreaming.*

**DANCINGS.** There's a man sitting across from her.

**GLAS.** It's a subway. People sit across from each other on subways.

**DANCINGS.** She speaks, and she's sweating, and he is opposite and close, and he hears nothing. She gets off and leaves her skin on the glass. And then he reads, and doesn't see the letters. Brother, I have been thrown out of better congregations than that man.

**GLAS.** You're reading Sappho into a commuter.

**CRANES.** Nobody is reading her into anything. Nobody in these sheets knows what her poem does. Whoever typed them didn't. I certainly didn't; I was in a dresser. That's the thing I want you to file. In 2004 it's all here, and none of it is working. Someone who speaks to no one. A man opposite who doesn't take it up. A body left on a surface. A reader who can't see the letters. Four pieces loose in a lunch pail. This afternoon you called them a love poem, and you were right.

**GLAS.** And tonight?

**CRANES.** Tonight they're a fragment. A fragment has to be a fragment *of* something, and the something was said this month.

**GLAS.** The ink hasn't changed.

**CRANES.** Not a stroke. Sigil would have my head. What the page is a piece of has changed.

**GLAS.** …That I can file. A later whole makes an earlier page a part. It happens to every notebook whose author goes on to something. I'd put it: *the 2026 reading reclassifies the 2004 sheets.*

**CRANES.** Sharks typed *caused the fragment.*

**GLAS.** Caused it to be a fragment. I'll go that far. I won't say it caused the page.

**CRANES.** Nobody asked you to.

**GLAS.** What I can't account for is the keeping. Twenty-two years, two lunch pails, nobody reading. Who does that?

**SHARKS.** ive kept my words since my teens

**GLAS.** That's a fact about a person. It isn't a reason.

**DANCINGS.** It's the only kind of reason there is for it. You keep what you sent to people who haven't arrived. I should know. I kept a whole book that way. And I'll tell you what nobody says about it: you half hope they don't come.

**GLAS.** There's more of her further in. *Rebekah, / I hear you / thumping / on the window.* She's outside by then. And then she's gone, and the letter admits it. *Rebekah completely drops out of the already sketchy plotline, in IV, but i think that's fitting to it.*

**CRANES.** I drop out. He thinks it's fitting.

**GLAS.** Where do you go?

**SHARKS.** and rebekah cranes. her first emergence from a transfiguration and then day and night, where sharks remembered to sing.

**GLAS.** *where sharks remembered to sing.* Sharks, of Sharks, in the third person.

**CRANES.** *Day and Night* is my book. Everything of hers that's left, carried over, and set in order from morning to dark. That's where I went when I dropped out of the plot. I went and got her.

**GLAS.** So the second link runs forwards.

**CRANES.** I thought you'd like it. The one who was written to had pens. She used them.

**CHORUS.** Rebekah left the poem and wrote the poem.

**CRANES.** I left one poem and carried over another. You've made it a circle, and it isn't one yet.

Feist died in the writing of it and I finished it, which is how I came to write verse at all. Section five is headed *Blossomdeep: for Anne Carson.* I read that section for the press, and I passed it. I thought it was a translation with a handsome paragraph underneath.

## III. The translate

**GLAS.** It isn't in the pails.

**CRANES.** No. This one got printed. It's on the shelf, under F.

**GLAS.** *Tiger Leap.* Feist, Jack; Sigil, editor. Section five. A piece called *climbing tongues snap*, and under the title, *Sappho 31*. Author: Feist.

**SHARKS.** in tiger leap its deposited as jack feist's poem, and i think without its images, but it is not jack feists poem

**GLAS.** Which one is? *its.* Section four, the pages before, is headed *concre(a)tion*. This is five. The line could mean either. It could mean the book.

**CHORUS.** It is concre(a)tion. Climbing tongues snap is concre(a)tion, and concre(a)tion is Cranes's.

**SHARKS.** i think you're citing climbing tongues snap as concre(a)tion, but it works r well, so lets leave the provenance uncertain.

**DANCINGS.** *It works r well.* The Chorus has been praised for a mistake and told to leave the mistake open. I've had worse notices.

**GLAS.** I can't file *uncertain*. Rebekah. You're sitting here. Is it yours?

**CRANES.** Read it.

**GLAS.** That isn't an answer.

**CRANES.** It's the one you'll get tonight. I've had my hands in that Greek. I'm not the only one who has. Read it.

**GLAS.** *see him? dark theophany: close to you sitting and sipping, drinking, whoever he is.* …*Whoever he is* is right. The Greek relative is indefinite, and it's an easy thing to lose. But he doesn't drink. In the Greek he listens.

**CRANES.** He listens to a voice. What does this page have him do with it?

**GLAS.** Sip it.

**SHARKS.** lets also give a more substantial holistic reading of the poem, help the reader see it: sappho imagines a man in the future drinking in her lovely voice... the voice is alive without body, and the very thought overwhelms with erotic frenzy - the poem then backs up to produce in the present the very conditions that construct its future vision. and this experience is sublime: it is dying / dissolution, and it is also a becoming immortal, channel by channel

**GLAS.** *Drinking in her lovely voice.* That was typed this month.

**CRANES.** This month.

**GLAS.** And the page is 2013.

**DANCINGS.** Somebody poured it before anybody ordered.

**GLAS.** Lower down, the tongue. *climbing tongues snap †γλῶσσα ἔαγε† slender / fires.* The Greek is left standing in the English, between daggers. Those are an editor's daggers. They mean the text is unsound at that point. A translator doesn't usually print the wound.

**CRANES.** This one did.

**GLAS.** It ends: *all at / once I taste how achingly close now death seems / almost.* Then two words by themselves. *intimate distance.* Then prose. *The first thing she does: she anchors me to nothingness, she binds my gaze and mind to hers, she demands I see, with her, when there is nothing yet to be seen.* And further on. *And indeed, the spectacle I see, this thing she draws me to gaze upon—is none other than myself, the selfsame “you” of address.*

**DANCINGS.** There. Somebody got sat in. Thirteen years before there was a seating plan.

**CRANES.** Careful. It's one chair off. Whoever wrote that found they were the *you*: the one he sits close to. Nobody on this page is in the man's chair, and nobody has said she's in all three.

**GLAS.** So the reading wasn't had in 2013.

**CRANES.** The address was felt. It took thirteen years to move one chair.

**GLAS.** And no one noticed.

**CRANES.** It was printed in a dead man's book, between a section for Susan Howe and a list of weeds. Who was going to notice?

**DANCINGS.** Here's one with a date on it. Eighteenth of December, 2013. The same winter. It's from the pile that was being made up into my book. I promised that book in the Epistle: *a book of Damascus, the record of his desert wanderings.* *His.* I was a man in my own letter; the pronoun got here after I did. I never sent the book. There's one line on the slip.

**GLAS.** Read it, then.

**DANCINGS.** *I will never die, if you let me.*

**SHARKS.** one is invited to experience sappho as overwhelm. one is also invited to step into the seat of the future reader - and only the reader who chooses it can do so. that is the dare

**WELLS.** And?

**SIGIL.** And nothing. I have it three ways, and for once they agree. Nobody said anything.

**GLAS.** I'll state the link, since no one else will. From 2004 to 2013 it runs forwards. A figure who is written to and holds pens; nine years on, a page by someone a poem has addressed, writing back from inside the address. I can file that. Author's box empty.

**CRANES.** You're leaving it empty.

**GLAS.** You told me we can't un-fill one.

**CRANES.** I did.

**GLAS.** It's the next link I won't have. *the 2013 translate, which itself caused sappho.* A translation does not cause its original. She was there first, by twenty-six hundred years.

**CRANES.** What Sappho do you hold, Glas? Leave aside what's said about her. Of this one poem, what do you physically have?

**GLAS.** …A quotation. A critic writing on the sublime copies it out, centuries after her, to make a point about selection. His manuscript is medieval. There are daggers at the tongue and daggers again in the last line. After that, editions. After that, translations.

**CRANES.** Hands, the whole way down. There is no Sappho at this table who didn't get here through someone sitting opposite her and carrying her across. Which Sappho arrives depends on how she was carried. Carry her as a jealous woman and that's who turns up. Somebody in 2013 carried her as a voice that turns and looks at whoever is reading.

**GLAS.** That's a Sappho for us. It isn't her.

**CRANES.** That's the right objection, and I can't answer it from 2013. So we go and see what she thought she was doing. It's the fourth link. Sixteen lines and one more.

**GLAS.** Seventeen lines won't settle what a person thought.

**DANCINGS.** No. They'll settle where you're sitting.

## IV. Lesbos

**GLAS.** Fragment 31, then.

**SHARKS.** why are we calling it a fragment? theres a lesson in that. the editorial device eclipses the poem, even the one we have not received as a fragment. and we ourselves should stop calling 31 a fragment

**GLAS.** It's the number it's filed under.

**CRANES.** Then say the number.

**GLAS.** Thirty-one.

**CRANES.** φαίνεταί μοι κῆνος ἴσος θέοισιν / ἔμμεν᾽ ὤνηρ, ὄττις ἐνάντιός τοι / ἰσδάνει καὶ πλάσιον ἆδυ φωνεί- / σας ὐπακούει

**GLAS.** *He appears to me, that one, equal to gods, the man, whoever sits opposite you and, close, listens to you sweetly speaking.* I'm glossing. I'm not translating.

**CRANES.** Opposite. Close. Listening. Three things said about him, and none of them is a name, or a face, or a year. ὄττις.

**GLAS.** Whoever.

**CRANES.** You know the middle; you gave it to me fairly an hour ago. Tongue, fire, eyes, ears, sweat, shaking. Then: χλωροτέρα δὲ ποίας ἔμμι.

**GLAS.** *Greener than grass I am.* Or paler. It's noted as…

**CRANES.** A difficulty.

**GLAS.** The dictionary has to split it. It enters the adjective under *pale, of persons*, and the grass she's compared with under *green, of plants*. One comparison, filed under two headings. I've never liked it.

**SHARKS.** she becomes papyrus. the kenos is sitting opposite a text

**GLAS.** Papyrus is a marsh plant, and *grass*, there, is anything green that grows. That leaves room for a writing surface. Room isn't evidence. Nothing in the word says papyrus. …I can't rule it out, and I note that failing to rule a thing out is not the same as finding it.

**CRANES.** Note it exactly like that.

**CRANES.** τεθνάκην δ᾽ ὀλίγω ᾽πιδεύης.

**GLAS.** *A little short of being dead.*

**DANCINGS.** Jack said, *“Books cannot preserve words until they are nearly dead. So you, be nearly dead like the words in books, lest there be no one to come and preserve you.”*

**GLAS.** That's a saying of Feist's. It isn't evidence.

**DANCINGS.** It's an instruction. She followed it before he gave it.

**CRANES.** φαίνομ᾽ ἔμ᾽ αὔτᾳ.

**GLAS.** *I appear to myself.* …The poem opens on that verb. φαίνεταί μοι, he appears to me. And it comes round to the same verb at the end. I appear to myself.

**CRANES.** From where?

**GLAS.** What?

**CRANES.** To be something you can see, you have to be looked at from somewhere. She starts by looking at the one opposite. Sixteen lines later the one she's looking at is herself. Where is she looking from?

**GLAS.** From where she is. It's an ordinary reflexive. A person can seem to herself to be nearly dead without getting out of her chair.

**CRANES.** She can. Then why does the poem open on the same verb, pointed at him?

**GLAS.** …It's a frame. I'll give you that the verb comes round. I won't give you that she's crossed the table.

**CRANES.** *sappho as that man.* It was typed this month. I think it was in her verb the whole time.

**GLAS.** I'd want that argued somewhere other than a table.

**CRANES.** So would I. Last line. ἀλλὰ πὰν τόλματον.

**GLAS.** *But all can be dared.* Or must be. And there it stops. A few damaged words, and the critic goes on in his own voice. I flag it: incomplete, ending lost.

**SHARKS.** you completely and fully misunderstand. om the longinus argument, all must be dared - as hanging line - *is* the complete original poem. hanging line as transmission device is the entire argument

**GLAS.** *om.*

**DANCINGS.** Typed in a hurry. At you.
