Reproduced here are translations of “Poem 31” by Mary Barnard, Aaron Poochigian, Philip Freeman, and Stanley Lombardo, to be compared with the Anne Carson translation that appears first. Another version of this poem, more of a close imitation than a translation, written by Catullus in Latin and translated into English by Charles Martin, appears on page 863.
Catullus
The poem describes the response of the speaker, a woman, to watching another woman talking to a man. (The grammar of the original Greek makes the gender of each figure clear.) The speaker seems at first to be interested in the godlike man, but then turns her attention to the delightfully laughing woman, and then, in an extraordinary switch, focuses in detail on herself and her own intense contradictory bodily emotions of jealousy and desire.
This poem is one of the most nearly complete of Sappho’s extant lyrics, although as preserved (through a quotation in On the Sublime, a Roman Imperial–era Greek treatise), it seems to be missing the ending. The lyric is composed in Sappho’s most characteristic meter, sapphics, which are four-line stanzas using a regular pattern of long and short syllables, designed originally to be sung to music. In theory, an English translator of Sappho could try to replicate this pattern using stressed and unstressed syllables (which are the building blocks of anglophone metrical poetry): for instance, a possible rendering of the first line, using this pattern, would be, “SEEMS to ME THAT GUY gets to DO what GODS do” (the naturally stressed syllables are here capitalized). The artifice, poetry, and music of the original, and its original context in oral musical performance, is arguably made inaudible in the nonmetrical translations, although each of these has a different kind of artifice. Poochigian suggests the artfulness of the original by using both meter and rhyme (although no ancient Greek poetry rhymed). Barnard, Lombardo, Freeman, and Carson do not use meter, although Carson’s artful arrangement of words on the page could be seen as providing a visual equivalent of the aural qualities of the original. The fragmentary final stanza is treated differently by the translators: Barnard skips it altogether; Carson treats it as a call to action, and Poochigian presents it as a moment of self-hating submission. Catullus, who had access to the whole original, provides a very different final stanza.

CRITICAL THINKING QUESTIONS
LISTEN AS YOU READ
The Greek original makes it clear that “you” and “I” are both female (because Greek uses participles that specify gender): this is unambiguously a poem about same-sex desire. Do any of the translations make this clear? Would that be possible to do in English?
All the translators except Anne Carson use possessive pronouns like “my” eyes; Carson echoes the original in omitting these. What difference does it make if the speaker does or does not identify her body as “hers”?
In the original, Sappho uses a series of different syntactical subjects for the sentence that straddles the third and fourth stanzas: “tongue,” “fire,” “seeing/eyes,” “hearing/ears,” “sweat,” and “trembling” are all grammatical subjects of the verbs—rather than “I.” Carson comes closest to replicating this syntax. What difference does it make, when translators use “I” as the subject instead, as in “I convulse,” “I tremble,” “I see,” or “I drip”? Is “Sappho” more or less in control of herself, depending on the translation?
How is one translation of the poem different from another depending on the ending? Does the ending refer to metaphorical or literal poverty? Is it legitimate for a translator to skip the ending of a fragment?
