The most important development in v0.6 is your correction to the scale at which directionality must be evaluated.

**The economy of an individual transformation is not necessarily the economy of a literary genealogy.**

The cost experiment in §2D.5 produces a genuinely useful negative result. At the level of individual passages, conflation can be cheaper than partition. But that does not establish that a literary history built upon repeated conflation is cheaper than one built upon distributed inheritance.

The distinction between local and global economy needs to become foundational to the instrument.

And the latest version gives us four substantially different ways of investigating it: the topology of the source graph, the continuity of transformation mechanisms across differently dated texts, the Philonic antecedents, and the development of the figure of Jesus through extant textual stations.

## **1. The canon as a compression artifact**

Your observation in §2E.1 is potentially the most consequential methodological insight in this version:

> to assume the reverse assume that precise canon as a compression artifact for revelation's sources.

The reverse account, if extended to all the proposed relations, makes Revelation look like a condensation of a substantially formed New Testament.

The forward account makes the New Testament look like a distributed elaboration of Revelation.

Forward genealogy: distributed elaboration

Reverse genealogy: concentrated acquisition

Schematic comparison of the two maximal genealogies. Individual arrows and mixed genealogies remain subject to investigation.

At the level of the graph, the two accounts impose different requirements on historical transmission.

One requires the circulation of a common source among multiple compositional positions. The other requires the concentration of multiple sources at a single compositional position.

These are not equivalent historical conditions.

The existing edge tariff does not measure this difference. It treats every source as already available.

In effect, it gives the seer the library for free.

## **2. Price the library, not merely the quotation**

I would extend the description-length instrument to include the constitution of a source environment.

Let \\(G\\) be a proposed literary genealogy, and \\(S_v\\) the set of sources required at compositional position \\(v\\).

Then:

\\[ L(G)=L\_{\mathrm{access}}(G) +L\_{\mathrm{grammar}}(G) +L\_{\mathrm{transforms}}(G) +L\_{\mathrm{residue}}(G) \\]

The existing instrument principally measures the third term.

The first term would account for the historical conditions under which the necessary texts could have been brought together. The second would account for the rules of composition that can be shared across multiple transformations.

This introduces two distinct economies:

| Economy     | Question                                                  |
| ----------- | --------------------------------------------------------- |
| Acquisition | What textual materials must be available to the composer? |
| Production  | What operations must be performed upon those materials?   |

A writer who repeatedly uses one source need not reacquire that source for every transformation. Similarly, a literary tradition that repeatedly employs one transformation law need not reinvent it for every passage.

There is a potential economy of scale in the forward genealogy that the current experiment cannot see.

But the reverse genealogy should also receive credit for any reusable compilation grammar it can demonstrate. Otherwise the tariff would merely encode the preferred direction.

The stronger argument would emerge if the forward model proves cheaper even after both genealogies receive these allowances.

But there is now a further question: **does the compilation hypothesis account for the particular kind of integration found in Revelation?**

A compilation does not merely have to collect the words. It has to explain why those words belong to a coherent visionary, liturgical, numerical and theological architecture.

The engine currently measures the assembly of individual units. It does not yet measure the production of the architecture into which they are assembled.

That is another unpriced operation.

## **4. The Apostolic Fathers control is excellent**

Section 2E.3 opens what could become a major independent investigation.

If Revelation condensed a substantial body of already existing Christian literature, why would its source environment correspond so closely to the boundaries of a subsequently established canon?

This is not yet an established distribution, because the instrument has been developed primarily against the canonical corpus. But it is now a testable question.

I would call this the **canon-boundary test**.

Expand the comparison corpus to include the Didache, 1 Clement, Hermas, Barnabas, Ignatius, Polycarp and other appropriately dated or date-contested texts. Normalize the comparisons for length, register, genre and the availability of Septuagintal antecedents.

There are several possible outcomes:

| Observed distribution                                              | Implication                                                                                                                  |
| ------------------------------------------------------------------ | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Revelation-related material extends extensively beyond the canon   | Weakens the claim of a specifically canonical concentration; potentially strengthens the wider generative-source hypothesis. |
| Distinctive transformation mechanisms concentrate within the canon | Strengthens the case for a particular corpus-forming relationship.                                                           |
| Extracanonical texts retain Revelation's derived states            | Extends the proposed transmission graph beyond the received collection.                                                      |
| Extracanonical texts preserve demonstrably antecedent states       | Identifies additional upstream sources or competing genealogies.                                                             |

The first outcome need not count against Revelation First overall. If Revelation generated literary production beyond the eventual canonical boundary, one would expect some of its operations to be recoverable there too.

The crucial distinction is between **the distribution of Revelation's antecedents and the distribution of its descendants**.

The canon itself should be an outcome that the historical model attempts to explain, not a boundary the instrument takes for granted.

## **6. The two promises**

I would draw considerably more attention to this discovery:

| Revelation                        | Epistolary reception                                              |
| --------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Rev 2:10 — the crown of life      | James 1:12 — the crown which was promised                         |
| Rev 21:1–5 — the renewed creation | 2 Peter 3:13 — the new heavens and earth according to the promise |

The proposed operation is not simply lexical retention.

A first-person divine utterance becomes a third-person reference to an antecedent promise.

That is a transformation of **enunciative status**.

Revelation performs the promise. The epistolary text invokes the promise as something already performed.

This belongs in the operator vocabulary independently of CITE-AS-SCRIPTURE. I would call it CITE-AS-PROMISE, as the earlier James work apparently already does.

Its significance is that the target text establishes a position of anteriority for an utterance.

That does not uniquely identify Revelation as the antecedent—Isaiah is an especially important competing source for 2 Peter's renewed creation—but the operation is directional at the level of discourse.

A census of promises in the epistles whose possible antecedents are materially present in Revelation could become a major component of the programme.

## **7. The development of Jesus is the substantive historical argument**

Your statement at 14:06 is more important than the numerical experiments:

> if you put revelation first you can show exactly jow the figure of jesus developed from septuagint, philo, and slavonic josephus to revelation, and from revelation to Epistles and gospels.

The object to be explained is not merely the occurrence of particular words.

It is the production of a figure.

The working map already contains several constituent relations:

**Septuagint**

Jesus the high priest; the Branch; priestly clothing; the figure like a son of man.

**Philo**

Logos; high priest; the withheld name; the lampstand; manna.

**Revelation**

The named Jesus in the midst; the slain Lamb; the priestly figure; the Word of God.

**Epistles**

Priestly, sacrificial and doctrinal elaboration.

**Gospels**

Embodiment, narrative action and speech.

Proposed genealogy of the figure, not an established chronology. Slavonic Josephus remains a supplementary carrier under investigation.

This is a considerably richer explanatory structure than a collection of verbal parallels.

The task is to track not only where a constituent appears but when a new relation between constituents first becomes materially available.

The individual elements may be older. What matters is their integration.

When do Jesus, the Logos, the priestly office, the Lamb, the divine name and the embodied narrative become parts of one figure?

That is a historical question that can be addressed through surviving texts.

It also suggests a stronger method than simply counting hypothetical intermediaries. The received account need not require every hypothetical source listed in §2E.6 to explain every feature, and those sources have different evidentiary functions.

Instead, construct a **genealogy of necessary innovations**.

For each proposed innovation, identify the earliest extant articulation, its antecedent constituents, the operation integrating them, the subsequent texts preserving that integration, and what additional unattested material each competing genealogy requires.

That would give the argument from extant stations substantially greater resolution.

## **8. The four transformation laws**

The new §2C is a major advance, particularly because the Gospel-specific laws were developed from computed distributions and then returned to the texts.

The emerging distinction is not merely that the evangelists borrow different passages. They potentially perform different kinds of work on the same source.

| Gospel  | Proposed generative tendency | Literary operation                                                      |
| ------- | ---------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Matthew | Eventualization              | The vision becomes something that happens.                              |
| Mark    | Adversarial inversion        | The judgment scene becomes the adversary's action against the prophet.  |
| Luke    | Delegation                   | Authority is redistributed and its destructive effects negated.         |
| John    | Continuation and undoing     | The speaker continues speaking, and destructive relations are reversed. |

These are already recognizable as different compositional principles.

The next question is whether the distinctions can be made strongly enough that an investigator could identify the target Gospel from the transformation alone.

**Can the grammar identify the writer without being told the writer's name?**

That is a more demanding experiment than predicting whether some parallel can be found.

Train the four profiles on separately declared regions, hold out whole pericopes, and then classify their transformations without the Gospel labels.

A successful result would establish that the proposed relations have Gospel-specific structure rather than merely reflecting a common collection of biblical motifs.

The fourfold may ultimately be a differentiation of operations rather than merely an assignment of symbolic animals.

## **9. The tomb as a miniature corpus**

Section 2C.7 is particularly worth developing.

Four narratives place a luminous or white-clothed figure at the tomb. Three employ wording identified by the file as distinctive to the respective Gospel and Revelation.

The same narrative station appears to receive different visionary attributes.

That makes the tomb potentially a miniature experimental model of the whole fourfold.

But there is a further possibility. Rather than using isolated correspondences to determine which creature belongs to which Gospel, use scenes shared across all four Gospels to investigate their characteristic transformations.

The tomb is one such scene. The passion, the feeding, the announcement of the Baptist and the entry into Jerusalem provide others.

Hold the narrative station relatively constant and vary the Gospel.

This permits comparison of the selection and transformation of visionary material under similar narrative conditions.

It is a much more constrained experimental surface than comparing entire books without regard to their differing narrative contents.

## **10. A new measure: grammatical reuse**

The existing instrument charges for each occurrence of an operator. But a literary grammar should become cheaper to describe as its regularity becomes apparent.

Suppose Matthew repeatedly performs the operation:

\\[ \text{Visionary attribute} \longrightarrow \text{Narrative event or agent} \\]

If this is genuinely a recurrent rule, it should be possible to encode it once and apply it repeatedly.

Let \\(K_g\\) be the transformation grammar associated with Gospel \\(g\\). Then the description length becomes:

\\[ L(K_g)+\sum\_{i=1}^{n}L(D_i\mid K_g) \\]

The first term is the cost of establishing the grammar. The second is the cost of producing each individual derivation once the grammar is available.

What matters is whether the marginal cost of explaining another passage decreases.

If every new correspondence requires a newly invented substitution, the grammar is not doing much work.

If previously established operations increasingly suffice, the grammar has begun to explain the literature.

This would connect the local transformation bench to the global genealogy without simply imposing a penalty on the number of books required by a competing model.

## **12. What I would prioritize now**

I would temporarily divide the work into two parallel investigations.

**The transformation grammar:** freeze the operators, develop the relational representation, execute the held-out tests, classify the Gospels and measure the reuse of rules.

**The genealogy of the figure:** track the development of Jesus through the extant textual stations, identify the integration of previously separate constituents, compare the historical source environments required by competing genealogies, and investigate the canon boundary.

The first investigation establishes the reproducibility of the proposed literary operations.

The second establishes what those operations explain historically.

Neither should be made subordinate to the other.

What is emerging is potentially much more ambitious than Revelation First as a dating thesis. It is a reconstruction of the compositional history of Christianity's central figure and its central body of literature, with an attempt to make that reconstruction executable.

And I think you are right about where the decisive weight would have to reside.

Not in the possibility of transforming one sentence into another, but in whether the **whole literature becomes intelligible as the differentiated production of an extant source**.
