### 11.6 The witnesses between: what reception does to the relations between operations

11.6.0 **What this section has to show.** §11.5.6 left the historical claim open. It needs the receiving witnesses between Theophrastus and the completion sentence, and after it. EA-CORPORA-38 (#1768) seats them. The witnesses show something different from a decline. Preservation, reassignment, rejection and renewed productivity happen together, often at one station. An operation can remain available, and even be re-derived, while its contribution becomes hard to recognize under the received account of the discipline. This section follows how that happens.

11.6.0a **The innocent reading first, in full.** Aristotle set out the three figures and their moods. His successors added technical appendices: Theophrastus and Eudemus a rule for mixed modal premises, five further moods in the first figure, and an account of hypothetical syllogisms. The appendices passed into the handbooks and picked up names on the way; the fourth figure is called Galen's. Later logicians sorted them again. Some thought the fourth figure redundant and some legitimate. Frege rebuilt the field on another base, and the question lapsed. On this reading, who added which mood belongs to the history of a discipline and has no bearing on what follows from what.

11.6.0b **The operations, kept distinct.** The chapter does not follow one object. It follows several operations, and what reception does to the relations between them. Calling every reduction, inversion, counterexample and application "the return" would make the continuity a product of the vocabulary. So the operations are named separately here, and the return of §11.5 is the name for the last group only.

| | Operation | First seated at |
|---|---|---|
| O1 | The modal rule: a conclusion follows the weaker premise, a prohibition on unearned strengthening | Alexander 124; Philoponus 123–124, 205 |
| O2 | The added moods: five further moods placed in the first figure, reached by conversion | Alexander 69, 110 |
| O3 | Hypothetical and prosleptic inference: syllogisms from a hypothesis and from a premise taken in addition | Alexander 326–328, 378, 390 |
| O4 | The classification of premise routes: the ways an added premise can be established | Alexander 388 |
| O5 | Method against stock: the means of analysing a new argument against a store of reduced instances | Alexander 340 |
| O6 | The return (§11.5.5): checking a classification against particulars, assessing fit, keeping the conditions of a conclusion, referring application to judgment | *HP* I.4–5; Kant A133/B172 |

The operations travel by partly different routes. O1 and O3 are carried with their reasons through the Greek commentators and reach Latin through Boethius (O3). After Boethius the seated witnesses follow O2, the moods and the figures, almost alone. The scholastic route of O1, the *peiorem* maxim, is not yet seated (11.6.17). O6 recurs at stations that owe it nothing by descent: Ibn Sīnā's counterexample, the Nyāya division between proof and inquiry, Hegel's contingent middle term. The question at each station is which relations between the operations are kept and which are cut.

11.6.1 **Alexander, c. 200: the operations together, with their arguments.** Alexander's commentary on the first book of the *Prior Analytics* (Wallies 1883, CAG II.1; EA-CORPORA-38/02) names Theophrastus and Eudemus some forty times. Pages below are the file's Wallies marks. The TEI's line marks mix folios with lines and are sometimes out of sequence (38/02), so pages are given, with the standard citation where it is known. The Greek has not yet been checked against the page images.

- **O1, the rule (124; cited as 124.8).** "οἱ δέ γε ἑταῖροι αὐτοῦ οἱ περὶ Εὔδημόν τε καὶ Θεόφραστον οὐχ οὕτως λέγουσι", holding that "τὸ συμπέρασμα αἰεὶ τῷ ἐλάττονι καὶ χείρονι τῶν κειμένων ἐξομοιοῦσθαι" (§11.5.4).
- **O2, the moods (69; 110).** "Αὐτὸς μὲν οὖν τούτους τοὺς ἐκκειμένους συλλογισμοὺς τέσσαρας ἔδειξε προηγουμένως ἐν τῷ πρώτῳ σχήματι γινομένους. Θεόφραστος δὲ προστίθησιν ἄλλους πέντε τοῖς τέσσαρσι τούτοις οὐκέτι τελείους οὐδ᾿ ἀναποδείκτους ὄντας, ὧν μνημονεύσει καὶ ὁ Ἀριστοτέλης" (he himself showed these four syllogisms set out as coming about in the first figure. Theophrastus adds five others to these four, no longer complete or indemonstrable, which Aristotle too will mention). The added moods stand in the first figure, and Aristotle is cited as mentioning them in the second book. At 110 the order of the last two is Theophrastus's own, "παρὰ Θεοφράστῳ", and differs from Aristotle's.
- **O3, hypothetical and prosleptic inference (326–328, 378, 390).** On the syllogisms from a hypothesis that Aristotle promises to treat more fully, "οὐ μὴν φέρεται αὐτοῦ σύγγραμμα περὶ αὐτῶν· Θεόφραστος δ᾿ αὐτῶν ἐν τοῖς ἰδίοις Ἀναλυτικοῖς μνημονεύει, ἀλλὰ καὶ Εὔδημος καί τινες ἄλλοι τῶν ἑταίρων αὐτοῦ" (no treatise of his on them is extant; Theophrastus mentions them in his own *Analytics*, and so do Eudemus and some others of his companions; 390). Theophrastus calls the wholly hypothetical syllogisms "κατὰ ἀναλογίαν" and shows in the first book of his *Prior Analytics* that they reduce to the three figures (326). He also sorts them into figures of their own (328). The premises "κατὰ πρόσληψιν" are his term (378).
- **O4, the routes of an added premise (388).** "καὶ Θεόφραστος δὲ ἐν τῷ πρώτῳ τῶν Προτέρων ἀναλυτικῶν λέγει τὸ προσλαμβανόμενον ἢ δι᾿ ἐπαγωγῆς τίθεσθαι ἢ καὶ αὐτὸ ἐξ ὑποθέσεως ἢ δι᾿ ἐναργ[γ]είας ἢ διὰ συλλογισμοῦ" (Theophrastus says in the first book of the *Prior Analytics* that the premise taken in addition is laid down by induction, or itself from a hypothesis, or from evidence, or through a syllogism). This is a classification of the ways an added premise can be established. It is not a requirement that a premise declare its route. That requirement is argued in 11.6.1a from the critique's own terms.
- **O5, method against stock (340).** Alexander distinguishes "ἀνάγειν τε λόγους εἰς τὰ σχήματα, ὡς ἔχει τὰ Θεοφράστου δύο τὰ ἐπιγραφόμενα Ἀνηγμένων λόγων εἰς τὰ σχήματα" (reducing arguments to the figures, as Theophrastus's two books titled *Arguments Reduced to the Figures* do) from describing a method by which anything put to us can be analysed. Whoever holds only arguments already reduced "τούτους ἂν ἀνάγοι μόνους ὡς ἂν ἔχων τήρησιν αὐτῶν ἀναίτιον ἀλλ᾿ οὐκ ἐπιστήμην" (would reduce only those, as one holding an observation of them without the cause, and not knowledge). Theophrastus wrote the method too, "ἐν τῷ ἐπιγραφομένῳ Περὶ ἀναλύσεως συλλογισμῶν".

O5 is the strongest of these for the critique. It separates having successful instances available from having the means to analyse a new argument. As a structural comparison with §11.0j: an executor whose competence is drawn from a corpus of reduced arguments can hold them in the form Alexander calls τήρησις ἀναίτιος, and the gap he names is the method by which a new case is analysed rather than matched. The comparison names a common absence. It does not identify the ancient case with the contemporary one, and it does not claim that a model cannot acquire the method; it states what has to be shown before a model's successful reductions count as the method.

11.6.1a **From the classification to a protocol: what must stay attached to a premise.** Theophrastus classifies routes. The critique needs a requirement, and the requirement has its own argument. A premise established by induction is defeasible by a case not yet examined. One established from a hypothesis holds only under the hypothesis. One established from evidence holds as far as the evidence is the evidence of this case. One established through a syllogism carries the grade of that syllogism's premises. Suppose the route is dropped when the premise is reused. Then a premise admitted by induction can enter a later derivation as if it had been demonstrated, and the conclusion receives a force its premises do not supply. That is O1, the prohibition on unearned strengthening, applied to premises instead of modalities, and the warrant that §3.14 conserves is lost in the carriage (§3.10). So the route of each premise has to stay attached for as long as the premise is used. It is added to the protocol of §11.3 as step 5a. The classification is Theophrastus's; the requirement is this critique's, derived from §3 and O1.

11.6.2 **Philoponus, sixth century: the reasons carried, the rank assigned in the name.** Philoponus's commentary (Wallies 1905, CAG XIII.2; 38/03) preserves the reasons and adds a grammar of reference. At 123–124 the positions are set out: "ὁ μὲν φιλόσοφος … βούλεται ἀεὶ τῇ μείζονι ἀκολουθεῖν τὸ συμπέρασμα", against "οἱ δὲ ἑταῖροι αὐτοῦ οἱ περὶ Θεόφραστον καὶ Εὔδημον καὶ ἔτι οἱ ἀπὸ Πλάτωνος", and he undertakes to give both sides. At 205 he gives the companions' reason in their own terms: "ἵνα καὶ ἐνταῦθα τῇ χείρονι τῶν προτάσεων ἕπηται τὸ συμπέρασμα". At 242 he notes that "πολυστίχους πραγματείας περὶ τούτων κατεβάλλοντο οἵ τε μαθηταὶ τοῦ Ἀριστοτέλους, οἱ περὶ Θεόφραστον καὶ Εὔδημον καὶ τοὺς ἄλλους, καὶ ἔτι οἱ Στωικοί" (Aristotle's students, Theophrastus, Eudemus and the others, and also the Stoics, wrote long treatises on these). He knows the hypothetical syllogistic as a body of Peripatetic writing that the *Analytics* do not contain. At 302: "ἔλεγε δὲ ὁ Θεόφραστος ὅτι δύνανται καὶ οὗτοι ὑπὸ τὰ τρία σχήματα ἀνάγεσθαι". Philoponus is a contrary witness to any account of steady loss: O1 and O3 arrive with their reasons intact. What he adds is in the reference. One side holds a title, and the other is named by its relation to the title. Stage one of §11.0c, the supplied construal, is here a construal of rank, and it does not yet touch the arguments.

11.6.3 **The Latin door: Cicero, and the Stoic station.** Cicero, *Topica* §6 (Wilkins 1902; 38/06): "Cum omnis ratio diligens disserendi duas habeat partis, unam inveniendi alteram iudicandi, utriusque princeps, ut mihi quidem videtur, Aristoteles fuit. Stoici autem in altera elaboraverunt; iudicandi enim vias diligenter persecuti sunt ea scientia quam διαλεκτικὴν appellant, inveniendi artem quae τοπικὴ dicitur, quae et ad usum potior erat et ordine naturae certe prior, totam reliquerunt" (every careful method of reasoning has two parts, finding and judging, and in my view Aristotle led in both. The Stoics worked in one: they pursued the ways of judging carefully, in the science they call dialectic, and left aside entirely the art of finding, called topics, which was more useful and certainly prior in the order of nature). At the threshold of the Latin tradition, the division between judging and finding is already a division of schools. One school is named as having kept only one half. Epictetus (38/05) gives the Stoic half at work. The Master Argument is set out as a conflict of three propositions, "κοινῆς γὰρ οὔσης μάχης τοῖς τρισὶ τούτοις πρὸς ἄλληλα" (2.19.1): any two exclude the third, and the schools differ over which one they deny. The same deductive form appears in Peirce's reading of the Theophrastean moods (11.6.10). Epictetus II.25, where the heading turns the question asked into another, is chapter 7 (§11.2).

11.6.4 **Boethius, early sixth century: the additions named, and placed under the master.** O2 reaches Latin under its authors' names. *De syllogismo categorico* (Migne PL 64, near col. 814 by the OCR's column marks; 38/13): "Habet enim prima figura sub se, Aristotele auctore, modos quatuor; sed Theophrastus vel Eudemus super hos quatuor quinque alios modos addunt, Aristotele dante principium in secundo priorum Analyticorum volumine" (the first figure has under it, on Aristotle's authority, four moods; but Theophrastus or Eudemus add five others beyond these four, Aristotle giving the principle in the second volume of the *Prior Analytics*). And: "Caeteros vero quinque modos Theophrastus et Eudemus addiderunt, quibus Porphyrius, gravissimae vir auctoritatis, visus est consensisse" (the other five moods Theophrastus and Eudemus added, and Porphyry, a man of the weightiest authority, is seen to have agreed). They are called "κατ᾿ ἀνάκλασιν, id est per refractionem quamdam conversionemque propositionis" (by a kind of refraction and conversion of the proposition). These quotations are normalized from the OCR and are to be read against the page image.

O3 arrives with a verdict. The preface to *De syllogismo hypothetico* (near col. 832): "de hypotheticis syllogismis saepe quaerebas, in quibus ab Aristotele nihil est conscriptum. Theophrastus vero, vir omnis doctrinae capax, rerum tantum summas exsequitur. Eudemus latiorem docendi graditur viam, sed ita ut veluti quaedam seminaria sparsisse, nullum tamen frugis videatur extulisse proventum" (you often asked about hypothetical syllogisms, on which Aristotle wrote nothing. Theophrastus, a man capable of every learning, treats only the main points of the matter. Eudemus takes a broader way of teaching, but so that he seems to have scattered seed-beds and brought forth no crop). And on the state of the question: "apud scriptores quidem Graecos perquam rarissimos strictim atque confuse, apud Latinos vero nullos reperi" (among Greek writers I found very few, briefly and confusedly, and among the Latins none). Eudemus is cited for the two senses of hypothesis (near col. 834).

Two changes begin at this station. O2 is placed under the master's authority, "Aristotele dante principium". O3, a theory Aristotle did not write, is credited to the companions and judged slight. Both are preserved. The authorization and the judgment of slightness are the conditions under which they are preserved.

11.6.5 **Ibn Sīnā, eleventh century: a limit of induction stated, and a name moved.** *al-Ishārāt wa-l-tanbīhāt*, the logic (Forget 1892; 38/15), divides what is argued with into three: «اشارة الى القياس والاستقراء والتمثيل» (a pointer to syllogism, induction and analogy; leaf n0063). These quotations are normalized from the seat's OCR and are to be read against the page images. Induction is the judgment on a universal from what is found in its many particulars. His example is that every animal moves its lower jaw in chewing, inferred from men, beasts and birds. Then: «والاستقراء غير موجب للعلم الصحيح فانه ربما كان ما لم يستقرأ خلاف ما استقرئ مثل التمساح في مثالنا» (induction does not necessitate sound knowledge, since what was not examined may be contrary to what was, like the crocodile in our example). The crocodile is his counterexample, offered as one; its zoology is not settled by the example, and the example itself is open to correction. What the passage does is O6: the universal meets a case that crosses it, as the habitat division meets the amphibious plants of *HP* I.4 (§11.5.3), and the crossing is stated as a limit of the inference. Of analogy: «التمثيل هو الذي يعرفه اهل زماننا بالقياس» (analogy is what the people of our time know as qiyās). The word for syllogism is the jurists' word for analogy, and Ibn Sīnā records the transfer as current usage.

On the figures (leaf n0067), the arrangement whose syllogistic character "scarcely comes to the mind and to nature" is set aside, and «صارت الاشكال الاقترانية الحملية الملتفت اليها ثلاثة» (the predicative conjunctive figures attended to became three). The OCR of this leaf is noisy, and the reading is to be confirmed against the image before it is relied on. If it holds, the fourth arrangement is judged far from nature, as Kant will judge it "unnatürlich" (11.6.8).

11.6.6 **The Nyāya: the control.** The Nyāyasūtra lists the members of a demonstrative statement: «प्रतिज्ञाहेतूदाहरणोपनयनिगमनान्यवयवाः» (thesis, reason, example, application and conclusion are the members; 1.1.32; Vizianagram 1896, 38/14; the OCR reads ग्रतिन्नाहेतूदाहर्णेापनयनिगमनान्यवयवाः). Vātsyāyana's commentary at once reports a larger count: «दशावयवानेके नैयायिका वाक्ये संचक्षते जिज्ञासा संशयः शक्यप्राप्तिः प्रयोजनं संशयव्युदास इति» (some Naiyāyikas declare ten members in the statement: the desire to know, doubt, the capacity to attain, the purpose and the removal of doubt, with the five). He keeps five. The desire to know sets the inquiry moving toward an object not yet known, and «सा खल्वियमसाधनमर्थस्य» (it is not a means of establishing the thing; normalized from the OCR's सा खल्वियमखाधनमस्येति). A note in the edition gives the other counts: «दशावयवा इति केचिन्नैयायिकाः । त्रयो ऽवयवा इति मीमांसकाः» (ten members, say some Naiyāyikas; three, say the Mīmāṃsakas).

The discipline does not lose what the proof excludes. The opening sūtra names the sixteen subjects whose true knowledge is the highest good, and doubt, purpose, reasoning, ascertainment and discussion are among them: «प्रमाणप्रमेयसंशयप्रयोजनदृष्टान्तसिद्धान्तावयवतर्कनिर्णयवादजल्पवितण्डाहेत्वाभासच्छलजातिनिग्रहस्थानानां तत्त्वज्ञानान्निःश्रेयसाधिगमः» (1.1.1; normalized from the OCR's प्रमाणप्रमेयसंशयग्रयोजनद्रष्टान्तसिद्वान्तौवयवतक-…). Doubt is excluded from the members of the demonstration and included in the inquiry the demonstration serves.

This is the chapter's control. Classifying an operation out of a proof is not the same as losing it from a knowledge practice. The Nyāya limits the formal component and keeps it inside an inquiry that names doubt, purpose and ascertainment, and that can return to them. The critical question for every other station is the same. Does the larger practice keep the relations the proof leaves out, and return to them when an application requires it? The question carries over to contemporary systems. A formal component can be legitimately limited if it belongs to an inquiry able to correct its application (§9, §12.6). At its later stations the Peripatetic reception loses the logic's relation to an inquiry able to correct it.

11.6.7 **Meier, 1752: O2 reassigned.** The handbook Kant lectured from (11.6.13) names the fourth figure for Galen. Meier, *Auszug aus der Vernunftlehre* §372, printed in AA XVI 721 beside Kant's notes: "Diejenige Gattung der ordentlichen Vernunftschlüsse, in denen der mittlere Hauptbegriff in dem Prädicate des Obersatzes, und in dem Subjecte des Untersatzes steht, heisst die vierte, oder die galenische Figur (figura quarta, galenica)" (the kind of regular syllogism in which the middle term is the predicate of the major and the subject of the minor is called the fourth or Galenic figure; AA XVI, leaf n0740). The moods Alexander and Boethius credit to Theophrastus and Eudemus as additions to the first figure are taught here as a fourth figure under another name. How the Galenic attribution arose is not argued here and is not yet seated (11.6.17). The station records the reassignment itself: a new figure and a new name, with the companions absent from the account.

11.6.8 **Kant, 1762: the same placement, a different account of what logic has done.** *Die falsche Spitzfindigkeit der vier syllogistischen Figuren* (AA II 53–55; 38/01) argues that the fourth figure is not a separately constituted figure. Its form is "so unnatürlich" (AA II 53). Its affirmative syllogism "enthält wohl die Materialien, aber nicht die Form, wornach geschlossen werden soll" (contains the materials, but not the form according to which one is to conclude). Rearranged, it concludes correctly, but "ein dergleichen Vernunftschluß ist von dem in der ersten Figur nicht durch eine andere Stelle des mittlern Hauptbegriffs unterschieden, sondern nur darin, daß die Stellen der Vordersätze verändert worden und in dem Schlußsatze die Stellen der Hauptbegriffe" (such a syllogism differs from one in the first figure, not by another position of the middle term, but only in that the premises have changed places, and in the conclusion the terms; AA II 54–55). The essay also argues that the second and third figures conclude only through interposed inferences reducible to the first. These quotations are normalized from the OCR.

The demonstrated correspondence is a result about how the syllogistic forms are organized and reduced. Kant places the fourth-figure syllogism where Alexander (69) and Boethius (col. 814) place Theophrastus's moods: in the first figure, reached by transposition and conversion, κατ᾿ ἀνάκλασιν. He does this without the name; the count of 11.6.12 finds no mention of Theophrastus or Eudemus in AA II. The correspondence does not show that Kant recovered O6, and it is not claimed here.

What it shows sharpens the historical problem. One placement takes part in two accounts of what logic has accomplished. For the companions, as Alexander reports them, the placement is an addition: five moods the first figure did not yet hold. For Kant it is a simplification: the fourth figure dissolved into the first, the labour spent on it wasted, logic shown to need less than it had claimed. A reduction can preserve inferential resources and still support a narrative of completion. Twenty-five years later the preface to the second edition can say that logic has taken no step forward since Aristotle (B viii). The resources are all present in the account. What the account no longer contains is the history in which they were contributions.

11.6.9 **Hegel, 1816: what the framing removes.** *Wissenschaft der Logik*, Book III (the seat's text is a printing of the Werke), "Der Schluß des Daseyns" (38/10, wissenschaft-der-logik-2, ll. 5113–5128): the indifference of which premise is taken as major "ist der Grund der gewöhnlichen vierten Figur des Schlusses, die Aristoteles nicht gekannt, und die vollends einen ganz leeren, interesselosen Unterschied betrifft" (is the ground of the usual fourth figure of the syllogism, which Aristotle did not know, and which concerns a quite empty, uninteresting difference), and it "macht jene vierte Figur zu etwas ganz Müßigem" (makes that fourth figure something quite idle). Of the fourth figure as a distinct figure, the historical statement is true. Hegel's account of the other figures, that they are "Umformungen, in welche jene erste abstrakte Form nothwendig übergeht" (transformations into which the first abstract form necessarily passes; ll. 4650–4657), shares the priority of the first figure with Kant and with the companions' placement.

What the framing removes is the question the companions' placement answered. Once the fourth figure is an empty difference of position, the question of who placed these moods, where, and as what, cannot be asked; Alexander's report that Aristotle "will mention" them (ὧν μνημονεύσει) has nothing to attach to. The moods survive as an idle figure, and the history in which they were an addition does not. In the same chapter Hegel renews O6 in another form. In the syllogism of existence the middle term is one of the subject's many properties, and "es ist daher überhaupt völlig zufällig und willkürlich, welche der vielen Eigenschaften eines Dinges aufgefaßt" (it is wholly contingent and arbitrary which of a thing's many properties is taken up; l. 4751). Premises and consequence can be correct while the conclusion is false: the wall painted blue (ll. 4770–4775). That is §5.3, a syllogism of existence presented as necessity. One station rejects O2 as a figure and renews O6 as a critique of the formal syllogism, and does not connect them.

11.6.10 **Peirce, 1867 and 1878: the name restored, the material made productive.** "On the Natural Classification of Arguments" (*Proceedings of the American Academy of Arts and Sciences* VII, read 9 April 1867; 38/12, paaas-vii, ll. 17131–17307) gives the moods a section of their own: "§ 6. The Theophrastean Moods. It is now necessary to consider the five moods of Theophrastus, viz. Baralipton, Celantes, Dabitis, Fapesmo, Frisesomorum." Peirce reduces them to three and states their form: "Suppose we have, 1st, a Rule; 2d, a Case under that rule, which is itself a Rule; and, 3d, a Case under this second rule, which conflicts with the first rule. … These three propositions cannot all be true at once; if, then, any two are asserted, the third must be denied, which is what is done in the three Theophrastean moods." And: "There are, then, two kinds of syllogism, — the Aristotelian and Theophrastean." The seat holds the 1867 print. The later *Collected Papers* text carries Peirce's additions and corrections of 1893, so a formulation is assigned to 1867 only from the seat.

The name is restored. Among the seated witnesses this is the first time since Boethius, and the moods are classified as a kind of syllogism with figures of their own, among them "the second-third figure in Theophrastean syllogism". The form Peirce reads in them is an inconsistent triad. Those are deductive relations: of three propositions that cannot all be true, two asserted require the third denied. The same form is Epictetus's three in conflict (11.6.3).

Peirce's hypothesis and induction add a different entitlement. A case or a rule is inferred without being deductively necessitated. In the same 1867 paper he defends hypothesis as an inference and cites Kant's definition from the Jäsche *Logik*: "An hypothesis is the holding for true of the judgment of the truth of a reason on account of the sufficiency of its consequents" (l. 17523; AA IX 84: "Eine Hypothese ist ein Fürwahrhalten des Urtheils von der Wahrheit eines Grundes um der Zulänglichkeit der Folgen willen"). His derivation of the synthetic inferences runs through the apagogical forms of the second and third figures (Part II §1, "Of Apagogical Forms", l. 16428). "Deduction, Induction, and Hypothesis" (*Popular Science Monthly* XIII, August 1878; psm-xiii, ll. 30416–30475) separates them from necessary deduction and builds them by inverting the deductive syllogism: Rule, Case, Result, with the bag of white beans. Induction is "the inference of the rule from the case and result", and hypothesis "the inference of a case from a rule and result".

What is shown is co-presence and structural affinity. In 1867 the Theophrastean moods are analysed in the same paper that derives the synthetic inferences, in the vocabulary of rule and case that the 1878 paper inverts. It is not shown that the third inference descends from the Theophrastean moods; the derivation Peirce gives runs through the apagogical forms. At this station the material the reception had set aside as an idle figure is named, classified and used. It belongs to a classification of arguments that makes synthetic inference a kind of inference. That is renewed productivity, at its documented grade.

11.6.11 **Frege, 1879: the count dissolved, the boundary stated.** *Begriffsschrift* §6 (38/11): "In der Logik zählt man nach Aristoteles eine ganze Reihe von Schlussarten auf; ich bediene mich nur dieser einen" (in logic, following Aristotle, a whole series of modes of inference is counted; I use only this one). The truth that lies in any other mode can be stated as a judgment, "wenn M gilt, und wenn N gilt, so gilt auch A", and the inference then runs by the one rule. The reason: "andernfalls auch kein Grund wäre, bei den Aristotelischen Schlussweisen stehen zu bleiben, sondern dass man ins Unbestimmte hinein immer noch neue hinzufügen könnte" (otherwise there would be no reason to stop at the Aristotelian modes; one could keep adding new ones into the indefinite). The restriction is "keineswegs ein psychologischer Satz", only "eine Formfrage im Sinne der grössten Zweckmässigkeit" (by no means a psychological proposition, only a question of form decided by the greatest expedience). These quotations are normalized from the OCR.

Frege names the problem that addition raises, O2 in general: the count of modes has no internal stop. His answer resembles O3 carried to its end. Theophrastus reduced the wholly hypothetical syllogisms to the figures (11.6.1–11.6.2), and Frege turns every mode into a hypothetical judgment under one rule. The resemblance is structural and no descent is claimed. He also states the boundary as a decision of form taken for expedience. That is Kant's delimitation (§11.5.1) in a new instrument, with its reason given. Inspectability is gained. What stays outside is the question whether the judgment "wenn M gilt …" holds of its object, which the rule does not decide (Proposition I).

11.6.13 **The textbook and its editor: two propositions with different objects.** Jäsche's preface to the *Logik* (1800) gives the chain of custody. Meier's *Auszug* (1752) was Kant's "Leitfaden"; his copy was "mit Papier durchschossen"; the remarks on the interleaves and margins made "das Materialien-Magazin" (AA IX 3–4). The *Logik* itself: "Übrigens hat die Logik von Aristoteles' Zeiten her an Inhalt nicht viel gewonnen und das kann sie ihrer Natur nach auch nicht" (AA IX 20).

Jäsche's sentence at AA IX 6 has to be read with its object. Apart from what the external form of logic gained, through the necessary separation of pure and formal propositions from empirical, real or metaphysical ones, "so ist, wenn es die Beurtheilung und Bestimmung des innern Gehaltes dieser Wissenschaft als Wissenschaft gilt, Kant's Urtheil über diesen Punkt nicht zweifelhaft. Er hat sich mehreremale bestimmt und ausdrücklich darüber erklärt" (when it is a matter of judging and determining the inner content of this science as a science, Kant's judgment on this point is not in doubt. He declared himself on it several times, definitely and expressly). The judgment reported is that logic "seit ihrer Entstehung und ersten Ausbildung vom Aristoteles an bis auf unsre Zeiten eigentlich nichts an wissenschaftlicher Begründung habe gewinnen können" (from its origin and first formation by Aristotle to our time, it has been unable to gain anything in scientific grounding).

v1.1 read "nicht zweifelhaft" as the critique's "allem Ansehen nach … zu sein scheint" with the hedge dropped, and as a hardening of the count like Charles's "almost unanimously" becoming "unanimous" (§11.1.6). That reading is withdrawn. The two expressions have different grammatical objects. "Nicht zweifelhaft" describes whether *Kant's judgment* can be ascertained, and Jäsche grounds it in Kant's repeated and explicit statements. It does not turn Kant's qualified proposition into an unqualified one. Jäsche's sentence also draws a distinction of its own. Improvements in presentation and in the boundaries of logic, the work of the Wolffian school, are set apart from advances in its inner grounding, and only the second is denied. The critique's proposed history was about to become a settlement here, and is corrected before it does: a construal of Jäsche's sentence had been counted as a step of the sequence before its object was read (§11.3, step 1).

11.6.14 **Carroll, 1897: a name on a reversal of order.** *Symbolic Logic* Part I (38/09) recognizes three figures and two forms of sorites, "the Aristotelian, whose Premisses are a series of Propositions in A, so arranged that the Predicate of each is the Subject of the next, and the Goclenian, whose Premisses are the very same series, written backwards." Of Goclenius: "the first who noticed the startling fact that it does not affect the force of a Syllogism to invert the order of its Premisses … 'here is a man who, beyond all others of his generation, has been favoured with Glimpses of the Obvious!'" Carroll then states his premises "in random order, leaving to the Reader the useful task of arranging them". At the end of the line a name is attached to the transposition of premises, the operation by which Kant reduced the fourth figure to the first (11.6.8). The name is mocked, and the operation is handed to the reader as an exercise.

11.6.15 **What the stations show.** Read through these witnesses, the sequence of §11.0c runs on the relations between operations, not on one object. The stations are plural, and the contrary witnesses have to be counted with the rest.

- **Preservation.** Alexander carries O1–O5 with their reasons. Philoponus carries O1 and O3 intact. Boethius carries O2 and O3 under their authors' names. Kant 1762 re-derives O2's placement. Peirce restores the name and uses the material.
- **Reassignment.** Rank is assigned in the reference (Philoponus). The additions are authorized by the master (Boethius). O2 is recounted as a fourth figure under another name (Meier). None of these steps argues against the companions' attribution, and none removes the operation.
- **Rejection.** The verdicts reject the fourth figure as a figure: far from nature (Ibn Sīnā, if the leaf is read as above), unnatural and a false subtlety (Kant 1762), idle (Hegel). Each is defensible at the level of the figure. None engages the companions' placement in the first figure. Kant's own result recovers that placement.
- **Renewed productivity.** Peirce classifies the Theophrastean moods as a kind of syllogism in the paper that makes synthetic inference a kind of inference. Hegel renews O6 as a critique of the formal syllogism. Frege dissolves the count of modes into one rule and states the boundary of the instrument openly.
- **The control.** The Nyāya separates proof from inquiry and keeps both inside one discipline that names doubt and ascertainment among its subjects.

What changes across the Western stations is the relation of the operations to one another and to the account of what logic has done. O2 survives every station and loses its history as a contribution. O5 and O4 do not appear after the Greek commentators among the seated witnesses. O6 recurs, unnamed and unconnected to O2, at Ibn Sīnā, at Hegel, and in the Nyāya. Kant's completion sentence is written over a syllogistic that contains O2 re-derived by Kant himself, and does not contain the account in which O2 was an addition. That is the settlement's operation at its most exact. The resource is present, and the history that would make it legible as a step is absent, so the account of the discipline can say that no step was taken.

11.6.16 **What this does to §11.5.** The historical claim of §11.5.6 can now be stated at the grade the witnesses support.
- That O1 and O3 travel with their reasons through the Greek commentators is shown (11.6.1–11.6.2).
- That O2 and O3 reach Latin under their authors' names, subordinated to the master's authority and judged slight, is shown (11.6.4).
- That O2 is later taught as a figure under another name is shown at one station, the handbook Kant lectured from (11.6.7). The route of the Galenic attribution between Boethius and Meier is not seated.
- That Kant re-derived O2's placement without the name is shown by his text and the count (11.6.8, 11.6.12). That he thereby recovered O6 is not claimed.
- That the eighteenth- and nineteenth-century verdicts concern the fourth figure as a figure is shown for Kant and Hegel (11.6.8–11.6.9).
- That the third inference descends from the Theophrastean moods is not shown. Co-presence and structural affinity are (11.6.10).

The thesis that "aristotelian logic is not aristotelian logic without theophrastan logic: the reopening is part of the same machine" is supported at its sharpest documented point. The completion sentence of 1787 stands on a syllogistic whose one Kantian revision of the figures, in 1762, reproduced the Theophrastean placement, and the account of logic in which the sentence stands has no place for the placement as a contribution.
