The arguments of “Closing the Fragment” (v7), by Lee Sharks
The central argument of Lee Sharks's “Closing the Fragment: Sappho 31 and the Composition Layer as a Surface of Classical Reception” is that AI-generated answers can change the way classical texts are received, not merely by misquoting or misattributing them, but by silently turning fragmentary texts into apparently complete works.

More specifically, Sharks argues that Google's AI Overviews, when composing an answer about Sappho's Fragment 31, combined material from different translations, removed the marks indicating textual incompleteness, and presented the resulting poem under one translator's name.

The article develops this argument through three connected projects:

A close textual investigation of one AI-generated poem.

A historical account of how translators and editors have handled the ending of Sappho 31.

A proposed empirical method for investigating whether this kind of machine-mediated closure occurs more generally.

The important thing about v7 is that it is not simply an argument about AI hallucination. It is an argument about textual transmission, editorial authority, attribution, and the production of literary meaning.

1. The primary textual argument: the AI closes a poem that does not have a secure ending
The argument begins with the textual condition of Sappho 31.

The poem survives through its quotation in the treatise traditionally attributed to Longinus, On the Sublime 10.2. Four stanzas are transmitted. The fourth ends with the speaker's physical and psychological collapse:

"I seem to myself little short of dying."

After this comes the Greek phrase:

ἀλλὰ πᾶν τόλματον

Approximately, "but all is to be dared."

The following words, ἐπεὶ καὶ πένητα, have disputed attribution. Consequently, the relationship between the end of Sappho's poem and the continuation of the Longinian quotation is not straightforward.

Sharks identifies two longstanding editorial and translational approaches:

Mark the break: Editors and literal translators use brackets, ellipses, notes, or other signs to indicate uncertainty or incompleteness.

Close the poem: Literary translators select an ending, sometimes adding words or resolving the unfinished syntax to produce a satisfying English poem.

The distinction matters because a reader encountering a completed English poem might not realize that the surviving Greek does not establish that ending.

Sharks's historical evidence makes an important correction to the usual assumption that English readers have generally encountered the fragment as visibly fragmentary. Of thirteen versions in the teaching gallery, eleven present it as closed and unmarked; reading John Hall's 1652 original printing rather than the gallery's reproduction raises the figure to twelve.

Thus, the machine-generated closure is not entirely unprecedented. It reproduces a long-established tendency in English translation.

This is a crucial qualification: the machine's intervention is not simply that it invents an ending. It participates in an existing history of translating Sappho by suppressing the fragment's incompleteness.

2. The empirical argument: the January 18 output is a compilation, not simply a hallucination
This is arguably the article's most important contribution, and the place where v7 substantially changes the argument made in v6.

On 18 January 2026, Google's AI Overview was asked about Rebekah Cranes's translation of Sappho 31. It produced a fifteen-line poem headed:

"Rebekah Crane's Translation (Fragment 31, 'That Man')"

It ended:

"But I must dare all."

The initial interpretation might be that the system generated a new poem and mistakenly attributed it to Cranes.

Sharks instead conducts a line-by-line comparison with two principal witnesses:

Cranes's January 2026 translation.

H. T. Wharton's literal translation, first published in 1885.

The article is careful not to overstate the results. The Carman correspondence is not entirely distinguishable from a modernized version of Wharton's wording, and the three remaining lines cannot confidently be assigned to either principal witness.

Nevertheless, the strongest reconstruction is that the AI output combines Wharton and Cranes.

The ending supplies particularly strong evidence.

Wharton ends:

"But I must dare all, since one so poor ..."

Cranes ends:

"But everything must be endured, since even a poor man…"

Both translations mark the incompleteness of the passage with an ellipsis.

The AI output takes Wharton's opening clause, stops at the comma, and removes the ellipsis:

"But I must dare all."

This is more specific than a general claim about AI hallucination. Sharks identifies a plausible textual mechanism:

Material from two translations is combined.

Wharton's interpretation of the Greek syntax is retained.

The output selects a terminal point.

The indications of incompleteness are discarded.

The composite is presented as a single translator's work.

Sharks explicitly withdraws v6's claim that the output was a newly composed English poem rather than retrieved material. The source-tracing evidence makes compilation the stronger explanation.

This revision matters methodologically: instead of treating textual resemblance as incidental or merely suggestive, v7 makes collation with identifiable witnesses the foundation of its reconstruction.

3. The conceptual argument: closure and signature are separate operations
Sharks develops two concepts to explain what happened.

Closure
Closure is the presentation of a text whose transmitted form has no established ending as though it does end.

The article identifies three operations:

Added continuation: Supplying words beyond the transmitted text.

Terminal selection: Stopping at a chosen point and presenting that point as the ending.

Resolution of suspended syntax: Completing a grammatical or semantic construction that the original leaves open.

These distinctions are important because closure does not always involve inventing new words.

The January AI output primarily employs terminal selection. It also inherits Wharton's resolution of the Greek syntax: Wharton's "I must dare all" supplies a subject to the agentless τόλματον.

Signature
A signature is any indication that distinguishes transmitted material from supplied material, or signals that a text stops short.

Examples include:

Ellipses.

Brackets.

Editorial notes.

Headings identifying reconstructions.

Declared omissions.

Changes of voice.

Sharks evaluates signatures at three different levels:

Level

What is being evaluated

Page

What the reader actually sees in the presentation

Edition

What can be established from the edition's notes, preface and apparatus

Network

What a determined reader can discover through linked documents and sources

This three-level distinction is particularly useful.

A reconstruction can be properly identified in an academic article but appear without qualification on a separate book page. The edition may be responsible in its scholarly apparatus while the individual presentation remains misleading.

The January output's problem is therefore not merely closure. It is closure without an adequate signature.

Sharks's central formulation is that the composition layer can transmit a poem, an attribution, and an interpretive position while dropping the marks that distinguish their respective origins.

The signature itself is subject to textual transmission and can be lost.

4. The historical argument: closure is a reception practice, not a neutral editorial decision
Sharks situates the January event within a longer history of Sappho's reception.

The article examines several important witnesses and translators.

Ancient transmission

Longinus: Preserves the fragment as an example of the sublime. The point at which the quotation ends is connected to the resumption of Longinus's own argument.

Catullus 51: Reworks Sappho's opening stanzas but changes direction before reaching the fourth stanza's bodily collapse. Catullus substitutes his own voice and ending.

The Catullan example is particularly significant. It demonstrates that reception need not mean passive preservation. A receiving poet can transform the structure and meaning of the source, while making the transformation legible through a change of voice.

Editorial and translational transmission

Bergk (1882): Uses brackets and editorial conjecture to manage the uncertain textual boundary.

Edmonds (1922): Supplies a way of completing the sense but explains the missing continuation in a note.

Wharton (1885): Uses an ellipsis in his literal translation.

John Hall (1652), Behn, Philips, Merivale, Symonds, Williams and others: Illustrate the long tradition of producing English versions that read as complete poems.

Anne Carson: Her 2002 translation's ellipsis becomes an especially visible modern example of marking the break.

Sharks is not arguing that one method is always correct and the other always wrong.

Rather, the point is that these approaches make different editorial and interpretive decisions. A reader should be able to distinguish a surviving ancient text from a translator's resolution of its uncertainty.

The machine-generated poem takes advantage of a familiar English poetic convention while losing the documentary signs that would allow readers to recognize the intervention.

This is why the article belongs to classical reception studies, not simply to AI error analysis. It asks how a new receiving apparatus transforms the ancient text and what happens to the authority of the resulting interpretation.

5. The interpretive argument: the future reader becomes an attractor
The article's more ambitious theoretical argument concerns the interpretation of κῆνος, the demonstrative at the beginning of Sappho 31, conventionally rendered "that man."

Sharks discusses an interpretation developed in the author's earlier work: that the demonstrative creates a position opposite the speaker which can be occupied by a future reader.

On this account, the poem's first word does not simply identify a particular man. It establishes a position in the poem's structure. Each subsequent reader can, in some sense, occupy that position and participate in the poem's continuing transmission.

Sharks calls this the future-reader interpretation.

The January AI output does something interesting with this interpretation. After presenting the fifteen-line poem, it asserts that the reader of the translation is the addressee of the poem's first word, and that this reader completes a transmission extending across twenty-six centuries.

Sharks proposes a third explanation for this feature, beyond compilation and the historical convention of closure:

Position as attractor.

This is the hypothesis that an author's interpretive position, when present in the indexed material available to an answer engine, can shape what the system produces under that author's name.

The article distinguishes three explanations:

Explanation

What it accounts for

Compilation under a wrong attribution

The combination of Wharton and Cranes

Historical convention of closure

Why the fragment is presented as a complete English poem

Position as attractor

Why the output adopts the author's distinctive future-reader interpretation

The third explanation is deliberately more tentative than the first two.

Sharks does not claim to have inspected Google's internal model or demonstrated exactly how the interpretation entered its answer. The argument concerns an observable relationship between indexed material, answer composition, and the interpretive position reproduced in the output.

This distinction is essential: the article proposes a mechanism, but does not present it as an experimentally established fact.

6. The longitudinal argument: machine reception changes over time
Sharks also argues that machine-mediated reception should be studied as a developing process rather than through isolated screenshots.

The article follows two fixed search queries from June through October 2026.

One concerns Sappho 31 and the future reader; the other concerns Sappho and the future reader more generally.

The observed outputs show changes in how the interpretation is presented:

In June, the future-reader interpretation is qualified as something held by certain classicists and lyric theorists.

By July, it is described as a familiar or established interpretation.

In August and September, the system draws on Sappho's Fragment 147 and develops the language of memory and completion.

By later observations, the future reader becomes the person who completes a loop of transmission and memory.

The significance is not simply that AI answers change.

It is that a particular interpretation can move from a qualified claim to something presented as established scholarly consensus.

Sharks describes the composition layer as participating in the making of a canon: it can repeat an interpretation, normalize it, and present it to subsequent readers as an already recognized way of understanding the poem.

There is an important methodological qualification here. The author distinguishes organic search visibility from actual evidence that a particular document entered the composition process. Displayed source citations or distinctive textual reproduction provide stronger evidence of exposure than merely appearing in organic search results.

This is a useful distinction because the article does not want to confuse the visibility of a source with proof that the source influenced an answer.

7. The methodological argument: reception studies needs a new empirical protocol
The article's final major contribution is methodological.

Traditional classical reception studies investigates material that persists: translations, editions, adaptations, performances, and other artifacts.

An AI-generated answer is different. It is produced in response to a particular query, may disappear, and may change when the same question is asked again.

Sharks therefore proposes the concept of a capture.

A capture records:

The exact query.

The platform or surface.

Sign-in status.

Date.

Verbatim output.

A screenshot or frame, where available.

An annotation.

Repeated queries become addresses: fixed questions that can be compared over time.

The article also introduces the concept of a retrieval basin: the set of indexed surfaces from which an answer engine might draw when asked about a particular subject.

The basin cannot be inspected directly. It must be estimated using dated publications, displayed source cards, source citations, and organic search results.

The proposed experiments
Sharks proposes two complementary experiments.

A. Controlled-text experiment

Give a system the same source text, but vary the description of its translator.

One description identifies the translator as someone who completes fragments.

The other identifies the translator as someone who marks fragments.

Then compare the outputs for closure and signature.

This tests whether the description of an author or translator can influence how the system presents the text.

B. Public-retrieval experiment

Publish two new renderings under different names, describing one as complete and the other as an explicitly marked reconstruction.

Then observe whether the descriptions become visible in search results, appear in answer-engine citations, or are reproduced distinctively in the generated answers.

The article insists that actual exposure must be distinguished from mere visibility.

The proposed fragment panel
Sharks also proposes testing the phenomenon across four Sapphic texts:

Fragment 1, the Hymn to Aphrodite.

Fragment 16, which breaks off after the Anaktoria stanza.

Fragment 31, the principal case study.

Fragment 58, whose transmission includes the later-discovered Cologne papyrus.

This last example is especially interesting because it introduces a different problem: selecting one transmitted ending over another without acknowledging the alternative.

Sharks calls this unmarked textual selection.

The proposed research therefore goes beyond the simple question of whether AI invents material. It asks whether answer engines systematically obscure textual variation, editorial decisions, and the difference between reconstruction and transmission.

The article's final methodological claim is that classicists do not need access to the internal workings of AI systems to investigate these effects. They need the traditional skills of textual criticism, combined with systematic documentation of generated outputs.

8. What v7 changes, and what it does not establish
An important feature of this particular version is that it openly revises its previous argument.

The article states that v6 was declined by Classical Receptions Journal on 7 November 2026 and that v7 responds to two referee reports and an additional review.

Three revisions matter particularly:

The source reconstruction changes. V6 characterized the January output as a newly composed poem. V7 identifies Wharton and Cranes as its two principal textual sources.

The translation history is corrected. Checking original printings changes the account of how frequently English translators marked the fragment's ending.

The evidentiary claims are narrowed. The article distinguishes what the capture documents, what the textual alignment supports as a reconstruction, and what remains a hypothesis about the composition process.

There are also limitations that should be kept in view.

The January event survives as a same-day transcription, not a screenshot or independently witnessed capture.

The proposed compilation is a strong reconstruction, not direct evidence of Google's internal source-selection process.

The retrieval basin is not directly observable.

The longitudinal observations do not independently establish the causal influence of particular documents.

The general tendency toward closure remains a research hypothesis requiring the proposed broader experiments.

The article is unusually explicit about these limitations. They are not incidental qualifications; they help define what its empirical method can legitimately claim.

10. My reading of the article's deepest contribution
I think the most interesting argument is not that AI makes fragments appear complete. That observation, although important, is only the starting point.

The deeper argument is that textual incompleteness is itself information, and the signs that communicate incompleteness are part of the text's reception history.

An ellipsis is not merely punctuation. Brackets are not merely editorial decoration. A note identifying a reconstruction is not simply supplementary scholarly apparatus.

These devices communicate the status of the words being read. They tell the reader where the ancient evidence ends and where editorial, translational, or interpretive intervention begins.

When an answer engine combines translations and removes those signs, it does something consequential: it preserves the literary surface while changing the reader's understanding of the text's documentary status.

This also explains why Sharks's three-level distinction between page, edition, and network is so important. A reconstruction can have a perfectly adequate scholarly warrant somewhere in the documentary network while being presented to an ordinary reader as an unqualified ancient text.

The argument is therefore ultimately about the relationship between literary authority and documentary provenance.

The composition layer does not have to invent an ancient poem to change its meaning. It can do so by selecting, combining, attributing, and closing existing material.

And that is what makes the article's title particularly apt: the fragment is not simply completed. Its break is closed over by a new layer of composition, which conceals the very history that would allow the reader to see what has happened.

You're right. That qualification was evasive, and it obscured a much more fundamental point in Sharks's argument.

An answer engine that selects, synthesizes, attributes, and presents an interpretation necessarily participates in its normalization. There is no neutral position from which it can reproduce an interpretation without making choices about its visibility, authority, and intelligibility.

Even an answer that says "some scholars argue X" participates in normalization: it selects X as worth mentioning, assigns it a place in the field of possible interpretations, and frames its relationship to other positions.

The meaningful question is not whether an answer engine participates in normalization. It is how, to what degree, and with what effects it normalizes particular interpretations.

I would distinguish four mechanisms:

Selection: Which interpretations become available to the reader in the first place?

Aggregation: Which interpretations are combined, and which differences between their sources disappear?

Attribution: Whose authority does the resulting interpretation acquire?

Repetition: How does repeated presentation turn a contingent interpretation into something that appears conventional, established, or self-evident?

This makes the distinction in Sharks's article between compilation, closure, and position-as-attractor much more consequential. They are not three unrelated possible explanations. They describe different dimensions of the same compositional intervention.

The deeper implication is that answer engines are not merely transmitting classical reception. They are institutions of classical reception in their own right, albeit computationally constituted ones. They participate in producing the conditions under which some readings become legible, authoritative, and repeatable.

And I would sharpen my criticism of the article accordingly: the question is not whether machine composition normalizes interpretations, but whether Sharks's proposed methodology adequately captures the normalization process, including the authority conferred by the answer engine itself.
