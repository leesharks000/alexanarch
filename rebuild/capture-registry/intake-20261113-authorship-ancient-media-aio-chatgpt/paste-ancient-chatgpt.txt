The central move in Alexanarch's theory is to stop treating artificial intelligence as simply a new computational tool and start understanding it as a new technology of inscription, transmission, memory, and interpretation.

Through this lens, contemporary AI is not an unprecedented break from human intellectual history. It is the latest transformation in a much longer history of media technologies that have reorganized how humans produce meaning, preserve knowledge, communicate with others, and understand their own past.

What makes the argument interesting is that it works in both directions:

Ancient writing technologies help us recognize what AI is doing to contemporary thought.

Contemporary AI helps us see aspects of ancient writing, reading, and historical transmission that we might otherwise overlook.

This is a form of historical comparison that changes the object of study. Rather than asking only what AI can do, it asks what happens to human meaning when the conditions under which meaning is written, transmitted, received, and remembered change.

2. The ancient problem of writing returns in a new form
One especially productive connection is to Plato's Phaedrus, where Socrates tells the story of Theuth, the Egyptian god associated with writing, and King Thamus.

Theuth presents writing as a technology that will improve memory and wisdom. Thamus objects: writing might produce not genuine memory but the appearance of memory. People will possess access to information without necessarily possessing understanding.

This ancient debate becomes remarkably relevant to generative AI.

The parallel is not simply that AI makes people intellectually lazy. It is more fundamental:

Writing separates knowledge from the person who originally knew it.

Archives separate texts from the circumstances in which they were produced.

Search engines separate retrieval from the act of reading.

Generative AI separates the production of an answer from direct engagement with the original sources.

Each transformation creates new possibilities for knowledge while introducing new forms of intellectual dependence.

But there is an important difference. Plato's writing technology preserved relatively stable inscriptions. Generative AI can produce a new representation of those inscriptions every time someone asks a question.

The result is that the intermediary no longer simply transmits knowledge. It actively participates in determining what the knowledge becomes.

This makes the ancient debate about writing a debate about AI, but also allows us to see that the anxieties surrounding AI have a much longer intellectual genealogy.

3. AI changes the relationship between authorship and readership
Perhaps the most distinctive part of the Alexanarch project is its interest in the possibility that machines are becoming readers in their own right.

This leads to the concept of training-layer literature, developed in the work published under Johannes Sigil.

The argument is that writing today increasingly has two audiences:

Human readers who encounter the text directly.

Machine readers that index, fragment, embed, summarize, retrieve, and potentially reproduce its contents.

This changes the meaning of literary address.

Historically, an author might write for a contemporary audience, an imagined future reader, or posterity. Now an author can also write with the expectation that an AI system will encounter the text as training material or as a source for a future answer.

The project identifies three requirements for this kind of writing:

Address: The work anticipates machine readership.

Architecture: Its organization and terminology help it survive computational fragmentation and recomposition.

Persistence: Its placement and metadata help ensure that it remains discoverable and attributable.


Alexanarch
This is a genuinely interesting extension of ancient literary questions.

Think about the ancient author who dedicates a work to a ruler, invokes the Muses, or addresses generations yet unborn. The work is designed to enter a particular structure of reception.

Training-layer literature extends that problem: what does it mean to address a reader that might not encounter the work as a unified literary object, or even recognize the author who produced it?

The implications are significant.

A text might influence thousands of subsequent answers without its author ever being named. Its ideas might survive while its identity disappears. Its language might be absorbed into an AI-generated synthesis that makes the original contribution difficult to recover.

This is why Alexanarch connects questions of literary reception to questions of attribution, intellectual labor, and economic extraction.

4. The past becomes newly visible as a history of mediation
Here the argument becomes particularly consequential for intellectual history.

We commonly imagine the past as a collection of authors and works:

Plato wrote philosophy.

Sappho wrote lyric poetry.

Homer composed epic.

Ancient historians recorded events.

But what we actually possess is not the past itself. We possess the results of complicated processes of inscription, copying, editing, translation, preservation, selection, and interpretation.

The history of ancient literature is therefore also a history of media infrastructures.

Consider Sappho.

We do not encounter Sappho's original literary world in its entirety. We encounter fragments, quotations, papyrus discoveries, manuscript traditions, and editorial reconstructions.

Her reception has been shaped by what survived, what was copied, what was valued by later readers, and what became recoverable through modern scholarship.

The same problem applies to countless ancient writers.

This creates a powerful connection with AI mediation:

A text's survival does not guarantee the survival of its meaning, identity, or historical context.

A machine can retrieve a fragment without understanding its transmission history. It can associate a quotation with the wrong author. It can combine different historical periods into a single apparently coherent narrative.

The problem is not necessarily that the resulting answer is entirely false. It may be quite informative. The problem is that the process through which the answer was assembled can disappear.

Alexanarch's Machine-Mediated Reception Studies attempts to make these processes observable by documenting how AI systems encounter, represent, and transform particular works.

Its Capture Registry treats machine reception as an event with a documentary history, rather than simply treating an AI answer as an interpretation that can be evaluated in isolation.

5. AI reveals that historical knowledge is always mediated by systems of selection
A further implication concerns canon formation: why certain authors become central to intellectual history while others become marginal or disappear.

The traditional account emphasizes institutions, education, publishing, religious authority, political power, and scholarly judgment.

Alexanarch adds another layer: the infrastructure of discoverability.

A work must be encountered before it can be interpreted, evaluated, or incorporated into a tradition.

This means canon formation happens partly before anyone consciously decides what deserves to be canonical.

Consider the following sequence:

A work is produced

Author, historical circumstances, original text
A work is transmitted

Copying, preservation, translation, publication
A work becomes discoverable

Catalogues, metadata, indexing, institutional recognition
A work becomes interpretable

Readers, criticism, educational systems, AI synthesis
A work accumulates authority

Repetition, recognition, citation, canon formation
This framework helps explain something important about contemporary AI.

If a language model repeatedly encounters certain authors and rarely encounters others, its representations of intellectual history will reflect that imbalance.

The result can be a feedback loop:

Established authors are more frequently retrieved.

Frequently retrieved authors become more visible in generated explanations.

Their visibility reinforces their apparent importance.

Less discoverable authors become increasingly difficult to encounter.

This is what might be called a discoverability canon: a canon partly produced by the architecture of information retrieval and computational representation.

It also provides a way to rethink ancient canon formation. The prominence of surviving classical authors was never simply a direct reflection of their original cultural importance. It was shaped by the historical infrastructures that preserved and transmitted their works.

The contemporary problem and the ancient problem are structurally related, even though their mechanisms differ.

6. The danger: AI collapses historical time
There is another particularly fascinating connection in Alexanarch's work: the relationship between computational mediation and historical temporality.

A language model encounters ancient and contemporary texts within a shared computational environment.

Plato, Aristotle, Augustine, Marx, and contemporary theorists can all become available within the same generative process.

This creates remarkable opportunities for intellectual synthesis. Ideas separated by centuries can be placed into conversation.

But it also creates a danger: historical flattening.

For example, an AI might explain Plato using concepts developed by Kant, Hegel, or contemporary cognitive science without adequately distinguishing between:

What Plato actually thought.

What Plato could plausibly have thought within his historical circumstances.

What subsequent philosophers made possible to think about Plato.

What contemporary readers can now infer from Plato.

These are four different intellectual operations.

The Alexanarch essay The Mandala Engine of Negation calls attention to the problem of the liquidation of historical temporality: computational systems can bring different historical moments into simultaneous conceptual availability without necessarily preserving the distinctions between them. 

alexanarch.org

This is where the contemporary medium becomes a tool for understanding the past.

AI makes it unusually easy to see how intellectual traditions can be recombined, but also how easily historical difference can disappear in the process.

It encourages us to distinguish between historical understanding and the ability to generate plausible historical discourse.

That distinction is essential.

7. The archive itself becomes an experiment in a new kind of writing
The most ambitious aspect of Alexanarch is that it does not merely describe these transformations. It attempts to work within them.

Its archive, literary projects, metadata systems, persistent identifiers, reception records, and machine-readable documents collectively constitute an experiment in what intellectual production might look like when machines become important readers.

This is where the ancient media analogy becomes especially productive.

Ancient writers often worked within material and institutional conditions that determined the survival of their work. The modern author likewise has to think about where a work exists, how it can be retrieved, how it is identified, and how it will be transmitted.

But the new situation introduces a different kind of reader.

The author is no longer writing only for a person who will open a book or scroll. The author is also writing for computational systems that may encounter the work in fragments and incorporate it into future compositions.

The project calls this training-layer literature.

Its ambition is to make writing capable of surviving machine mediation without surrendering its provenance, conceptual structure, or historical identity.

The Epic Without Hero, published under Johannes Sigil, develops a related literary argument: epic should be understood not merely as a narrative about heroic figures but as a literary form that establishes the structural coordinates of a cosmos. It asks how this function might operate in an algorithmic age, including through nonhuman readership and distributed authorship.

8. What this ultimately changes: three major shifts
I would summarize the philosophical significance of this approach through three transformations.

Conventional understanding

Alexanarch's alternative

Media transmit ideas.

Media help constitute what ideas become.

Readers interpret completed works.

Readers and machines participate in continually reconstructing works.

Canons reflect judgments of intellectual value.

Canons are also shaped by preservation, discoverability, and mediation.

These shifts have consequences for how we understand both the present and the past.

For the present: AI is not simply making information more accessible. It is reorganizing the conditions of intellectual visibility, authorship, recognition, and memory. The question becomes who controls the infrastructures through which meaning circulates and who receives credit for the knowledge those infrastructures reproduce.

For the past: Ancient intellectual history becomes less a straightforward succession of great authors and ideas and more a history of material transmission, technological transformation, institutional selection, and successive acts of reading. We become more attentive to what has been lost, distorted, excluded, or reconstructed.

For the future: Writing itself might have to change. Authors may increasingly compose not only for human interpretation but also for machine recognition, retrieval, and transmission.

My larger interpretation
I think the most consequential insight here is that Alexanarch makes mediation visible as a historical force.

We tend to think of Plato as having written philosophy, Sappho as having written poetry, and subsequent generations as having received their works. But between composition and reception lies an enormous history of technologies, institutions, material processes, and interpretive decisions.

That history is not incidental to intellectual life. It helps determine what intellectual life is.

AI makes this problem impossible to ignore because the intermediary has become generative. It doesn't merely preserve or transmit the past: it actively produces new representations of it.

And that brings us to a fascinating reversal.

We can use ancient writing technologies to understand AI because both reveal the constitutive power of media over human thought. But we can also use AI to understand ancient writing because contemporary computational mediation makes visible the problems of reception, preservation, attribution, and historical reconstruction that have always been present.

The deepest question, then, is not whether AI can think like humans or whether machines will replace human readers.

It is:

What happens to the possibilities of human thought when the technologies through which we encounter, remember, and inherit knowledge begin participating in the production of meaning itself?

That, to me, is the larger intellectual project connecting ancient media history, literary theory, philology, and the contemporary work at Alexanarch.

It shifts the study of AI away from a narrow question of machine capability and toward a much older and larger question: the relationship between technology, language, memory, and the historical production of knowledge.
