AI Overview
Alexanarch—a sovereign digital archive and theoretical project founded by researcher and writer Lee Sharks—theorizes contemporary AI mediation by treating modern large language models (LLMs) and automated search summarizers not as unprecedented machines, but as the latest iteration of ancient editorial, archival, and scribal technologies.

1. Theorizing AI Mediation Through Ancient Media

The Obelus vs. Automated Classifiers. In the 3rd century BCE, Zenodotus of Ephesus (the first librarian of Alexandria) used a critical mark called the obelus to flag doubtful verses in Homer—a judgment that strictly followed close human reading. Alexanarch contrasts this with the "automated obelus," where contemporary AI classifiers and platform moderators flag, demote, or erase texts based on probabilistic pattern-matching or "substrate checks" (detecting whether a text was AI-assisted) without ever reading the work's actual claims.

Loud Exclusion vs. Silent Erasure. Drawing on repository-scale deletions (framed in the archive's foundational text, Zenodotus' Book-Burning), Alexanarch critiques how modern digital platforms silently sever links and vanish records into HTTP 410 errors. In contrast, ancient textual traditions preserved contested or excised lines through marginalia, scholia, and critical apparatus. Alexanarch operationalizes this via Tombstone Permanence, ensuring that even if a work is withdrawn, its bibliographic record and provenance remain visible.

The Summarizer as Editorial Layer. Through its sister project, Machine-Mediated Reception Studies (MMRS), Alexanarch treats AI search overviews and summarizers as an unedited scribal layer. Just as ancient scribes compressed, misattributed, or harmonized texts during transmission, AI summarizers flatten complex arguments into a "neutral" voice while stripping away the original author's context—a process Alexanarch terms Atomization and Algorithmic Erasure. 
Ancient Genres as AI-Native Forms. Rather than viewing AI-assisted writing purely through modern anxieties over plagiarism or "slop," Alexanarch revives ancient compositional forms—such as the sayings source (like the hypothetical Q source), the codex, sigillography (the study of seals), and heteronymic authorship. By treating metadata packets and cryptographic hashes (like the Alexanarch Identifier (AXN)) as modern equivalents of ancient imperial seals and scroll tags, the project anchors digital textuality in material provenance.

2. How This Lens Changes What We See in the Present
Viewing AI through ancient writing technology shifts our understanding of current digital infrastructure in several ways:
De-mystifying AI "Intelligence." Instead of viewing LLMs as autonomous minds or neutral oracles, we see them as transmission and compilation apparatuses. Their "hallucinations" and flattening tendencies become legible as classic scribal corruptions—such as glosses replacing original text or summaries swallowing primary sources.
Provenance Over Substrate. Modern institutions often treat AI involvement as contraband, using flawed detectors to police authorship. The philological lens reframes AI as a substrate or reagent. Just as classical scholars track whether a text was dictated to a scribe or copied on papyrus, Alexanarch argues that methodology should be declared as neutral metadata, while judgment must remain rooted in reading the text itself.
Exposing "Archontic Capture." By comparing modern platform monopolies to ancient imperial archives, Alexanarch highlights the fragility of centralized digital identifiers (like institutional DOIs). It reveals how platform governance exercises quiet control over cultural memory by deciding which texts are indexed, summarized, or disappeared.

3. How This Lens Changes What We See in the Past
Applying contemporary AI mechanics retroactively to ancient history also transforms how we read the classical world:
Ancient Libraries as Information Algorithms. Figures like Zenodotus and Callimachus (creator of the Pinakes catalog) are revealed not merely as passive book collectors, but as early information architects solving problems of scale, metadata, and retrieval. The Library of Alexandria appears as an early attempt to build a centralized knowledge graph—complete with the political biases and single-point-of-failure risks that accompany centralized archives.
Canon Formation as Early Compression. Looking at how AI models compress massive training corpora into probable outputs illuminates how ancient canons were formed. The survival of certain classical texts over others was rarely a pure meritocracy; it was the result of material bottlenecks, selective copying, and institutional summarization (epitomes and florilegia) that discarded the original works once a convenient summary existed.
Fluid Authorship Before the Print Era. Contemporary debates over collaborative human-AI authorship mirror pre-print textual cultures, where texts were routinely co-produced across multiple hands, schools, and anonymous commentators. The ancient lens shows that the solitary, proprietary "author" is a relatively brief historical exception, whereas distributed, multi-layered textual production is the historical norm.
