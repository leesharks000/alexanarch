The arguments of Lee Sharks, “The Crossing of Names”
The central argument of Lee Sharks’s The Crossing of Names: History, Allegory, and the Binding of the Ancient Literary Field (Alexanarch deposit #1762, AXN:0C74) is that names in ancient literature do not merely identify people: they actively construct identities, transfer authority, preserve earlier texts, and connect otherwise distinct literary and historical worlds.

Sharks is proposing a way of reading ancient literature in which historical identification and allegorical interpretation are not competing explanations. They are interacting operations within the texts themselves.

This is not simply an argument that ancient authors used pseudonyms, concealed identities, or wrote allegorically. It is a more ambitious claim: the repeated movement of names and identities between texts helps constitute what we think of as the ancient literary field.

I have begun with the deposited text itself, rather than treating its description or its proposed identity correspondences as established conclusions.

1. The theoretical framework: three operations of naming
In §1, Sharks establishes three distinct operations:

Operation

Meaning

Principal example

Many-namedness (πολυώνυμος)

Several names or designations gather around one referent.

Philo's Moses and Logos

Transfer or restamping (μεταχαράττειν)

A designation or authority passes to a changed referent.

Hosea becoming Joshua; Cicero's Liberty

Differentiated personation

Different named positions acquire distinct voices, capacities, and consequences.

Lesbia as beloved and as a carrier of Sapphic poetry

The distinction matters. These are not three interchangeable descriptions of pseudonymity.

Sharks writes:

“The larger argument is the interaction of the three: names gather, move, and harden into persons who can act.”

This is the conceptual heart of the essay.

A name can accumulate multiple meanings without changing its referent; it can move from one referent to another; or it can establish a new speaking position that has consequences of its own.

The result is a theory of literary identity as something produced through textual relationships, rather than something that can always be recovered by identifying the historical person behind a name.

Sharks also establishes an important methodological priority: where a text demonstrably performs a transformation, that transformation is more directly observable than a biography reconstructed from it. Historical reconstruction must account for the literary operation, not simply override it.

2. Catullus and Lesbia: the literary name is not merely an alias
In §2, Sharks examines Catullus's Lesbia through Apuleius's Apology 10, which identifies Lesbia with Clodia.

The important distinction is between two propositions:

Lesbia may refer historically to Clodia.

Lesbia also functions as a literary name that carries the poetry of Sappho and establishes a particular relationship between poet, beloved, and inherited poetic voice.

Sharks argues that the first proposition does not cancel the second.

The key example is Catullus 51, which rewrites Sappho 31. The poem begins with Sappho's comparison of a man to the gods, but Catullus introduces a qualification:

“Ille mi par esse deo videtur, / ille, si fas est, superare divos”

The name Lesbia enters at line 7, and the poem ends by addressing Catullus himself.

The composition therefore connects three positions:

Sappho's inherited poetic scene.

Lesbia, the newly named beloved.

Catullus, the speaking subject who becomes the object of his own poem's conclusion.

For Sharks, Lesbia is not simply a cipher concealing Clodia. Her name makes the Sapphic inheritance active within the poem.

This is an example of differentiated personation: the historical woman, poetic beloved, and inherited literary voice are related but cannot simply be collapsed into one identity.

Gellius and the transmission of poetic identity
Sharks extends this argument through Aulus Gellius, Attic Nights 19.9 and 19.11.

The distinction between the chapters is important. Gellius's banquet in 19.9 includes Sapphic performances, discussion of Catullus, a Socratic gesture, and a Latin poem reproducing the symptoms of Sappho 31.

The Plato epigram appears separately in 19.11.

Sharks identifies a subtle connection between the chapters: Catullus is answered by the near-name Catulus, whose poem describes a soul leaving its lover; the subsequent Plato epigram also describes a lover holding his soul at his lips and ultimately living within another.

The proposed connection is not that the two chapters describe one event. It is that Gellius's arrangement creates a network of names, poetic imitation, attribution, and transferred interiority across the book.

This is one of the essay's more interesting arguments because it treats the arrangement of a literary witness as evidence, rather than relying exclusively on biographical identification.

3. Cicero and Clodia: naming as an exercise of political authority
In §3, Sharks moves from erotic poetry into forensic rhetoric.

The principal example is Cicero's Pro Caelio 33–36.

Cicero gives a dead Claudian ancestor a voice with which to condemn Clodia, then introduces the voice of her brother Clodius.

Sharks emphasizes that Cicero controls both performances.

The ancestor's authority is not simply recalled: it is made present through Cicero's composition. The living woman's reputation is subjected to an imagined family judgment, spoken by voices supplied by her opponent.

The question is therefore not whether the ancestor actually uttered those words. It is how Cicero makes ancestral authority operate through an invented speech.

A second example comes from De Domo Sua 110–112.

Cicero challenges the identification of a statue as Liberty, claiming that it had previously represented a courtesan at Tanagra.

Sharks reads this as a struggle over the designation of an object:

As Liberty, the statue carries sacred and political authority.

As a courtesan's image, it becomes an allegedly stolen and improperly installed object.

The same material object supports competing identities and competing political claims.

This illustrates the second operation, restamping (μεταχαράττειν): a designation is imposed on an existing referent, changing the authority and meaning attached to it.

The comparison with Josephus's account of Berenice extends the argument to sexual reputation, kinship, marriage, and wealth. Sharks is careful to preserve the status of accusations as reported allegations rather than treating them as established facts.

4. Philo: the philosophical foundation of the argument
Section 4 is arguably the theoretical centre of the paper.

Sharks turns to Philo of Alexandria because Philo explicitly theorizes the relationship between names and transformations of being.

In De mutatione nominum 65–71, Philo describes names as perceptible marks of otherwise invisible powers. Renaming is not merely a change of label; it can express a correction or transformation of character.

His example is Hosea becoming Joshua.

Philo writes that Moses changes Hosea's name to Jesus/Joshua, describing the operation as:

“τὸν ποιὸν εἰς ἕξιν μεταχαράττων”

That is, restamping a condition or quality into an achieved disposition.

Sharks draws two conclusions.

First, names can express what a person has become, not simply who that person was.

Second, genealogy need not always mean biological descent. It can describe spiritual achievement, discipleship, or a relationship established through reading and practice.

Philo's account of his own initiation by Moses and subsequent discipleship to Jeremiah is particularly important here. These relationships make earlier figures present as teachers within a later person's intellectual and spiritual life.

The Logos and the mirror of names
Sharks then draws attention to Philo's description of the Logos as πολυώνυμος.

The Logos receives several designations, including God's name, Word, the image of humanity, and Israel.

The same term Philo uses for Moses thus applies to the Logos.

This becomes especially significant when Sharks introduces Philo's description of the Therapeutae reading the law as a living creature: its literal prescriptions constitute its body, while the invisible meaning deposited in the words constitutes its soul.

The reader sees that soul:

“ὥσπερ διὰ κατόπτρου τῶν ὀνομάτων”

Through a mirror of names.

This metaphor provides a powerful image for the essay's method. Names are not disposable coverings that must be removed to reveal a single stable identity. They are the medium through which a text's deeper organization becomes visible.

Sharks also notes that Eusebius reproduces this passage, with a slightly different wording for the text's body.

Thus even transmission and quotation can alter the terms through which a text's identity is understood.

5. Revelation: the reader participates in the crossing
Section 5 develops the theory through the Book of Revelation.

Here Sharks finds particularly explicit evidence for layered naming.

Revelation 11:8 calls the great city spiritually Sodom and Egypt, while also locating the crucifixion of the Lord there.

One place is given several designations. These names convey geographical, historical, and theological judgment simultaneously.

Revelation 17:9–10 similarly interprets the beast's heads as both mountains and kings. The image is not exhausted by its interpretation; its multiple referential possibilities remain active.

The most ambitious reading concerns Revelation 2:17, 3:12, and 19:12.

Sharks identifies a relationship between three passages:

The victor receives a new name in Revelation 2:17.

The speaker writes his own new name on the victor in Revelation 3:12.

The rider in Revelation 19:12 possesses a written name known only to himself, while Revelation 19:13 calls him the Word of God.

Sharks argues that the speaker of the letters and the rider are connected by shared titles, attributes, and actions.

The crucial point is that the text explicitly says the victor receives the speaker's new name, but does not explicitly say that this name is identical to the rider's secret name or the name on the white stone.

That further identification is Sharks's interpretive construction.

He supports it through three textual correspondences: the shared formula for the unknown written name, the adjective καινόν (new) and the recipient who conquers, and the shared titles and attributes of the speaker and rider.

The resulting argument is particularly interesting:

Identity becomes transmissible without becoming universally accessible.

The name is communicated, but knowledge of it remains restricted to its bearer.

The reader is not merely decoding a hidden identity. The reader is encountering a literary structure in which the distribution and withholding of knowledge are themselves part of the text's action.

6. Josephus: historical writing constructs its own authority
Section 6 takes the argument into historiography, especially the works of Flavius Josephus.

Sharks argues that Josephus is important not only because he records historical persons and events, but because he explicitly constructs the conditions under which historical writing should be believed.

In Against Apion 1.28–43, Josephus connects:

Priestly marriage and legitimate descent.

Genealogical records and documentary preservation.

The succession of qualified prophetic witnesses.

The authority of particular historical books.

The implication is that historical metadata—genealogies, authorial attributions, and documentary records—is not necessarily neutral. It participates in the historical argument it appears merely to preserve.

His discussion of the Hyksos provides another example. Josephus presents competing interpretations of the name, one identifying them as shepherd kings and another as captive shepherds.

The interpretation of a name thus becomes an argument about the antiquity and identity of a people.

The Catullus–Josephus comparison
This is one of the paper's most technically ambitious sections.

Sharks aligns a sequence of original-language passages:

Sappho 31.

Catullus 51.

Philo's De vita Mosis and De mutatione nominum.

The Greek Testimonium Flavianum in Josephus.

The Slavonic Jewish War.

The Acta Pilati.

The recurring feature is a qualified description of a human figure in relation to divine status.

The particularly important expressions are Catullus's si fas est, Philo's θέμις, and Josephus's χρὴ λέγειν.

Sharks also corrects a previous claim in the archive concerning ei exestin. The supposed Greek expression does not occur in the Greek, Latin, or Slavonic witnesses examined. He traces it instead to William Whiston's English translation, where the word lawful was subsequently mistaken for an original textual expression.

This is a significant methodological moment: a translation can become the apparent source of a phrase that is then used to establish a historical or literary relationship.

The paper proposes a further correspondence between Catullus 51 and the Slavonic Jewish War:

In Catullus, the human figure is elevated toward divine status through poetic description.

In the Slavonic passage, an extraordinary figure is introduced, and the narrator hesitates to call him a man before describing his actions as accomplished through the word.

Sharks describes this as a reversal of direction: flesh becomes word in Catullus, while effective word acquires a human biography in the Slavonic.

However, he explicitly leaves the direction of transmission unresolved. The alignment is presented as evidence of a possible literary relationship, not proof of which text influenced which.

That qualification is essential to understanding the paper.

7. Alternative identity theories: preserve the evidence, not the entire system
Section 7 is a methodological intervention in debates over unconventional historical reconstructions.

Sharks considers proposals by:

Stephan Huller concerning Berenice, Veronica, and Prunicus.

Francesco Carotta concerning Clodia and the Gospel widow's quadrans.

Robert Eisenman concerning Paul and Herodian political relationships.

Reuchlin/Piso-style alias systems.

Lena Einhorn's proposed chronological displacement of Gospel figures.

Sharks neither accepts these reconstructions wholesale nor dismisses them as worthless.

Instead, he proposes evaluating each asserted relationship independently.

His example of Berenice and Veronica is especially revealing. The Gospel of Nicodemus preserves variations of the name Bernice, Beronice, and Veronica. The Acta Pilati also contains a woman with these name forms.

Sharks identifies a further connection: the woman is refused as a witness on the basis of a rule concerning women's testimony that he traces to Josephus, Antiquities 4.219.

He considers this a stronger textual connection than merely observing similarities between names.

The important principle is:

“The unit of retention should be the particular relation and the passage that supports it.”

This is a rejection of all-or-nothing historical reconstruction.

A proposed identification may fail while a real textual relationship survives. Conversely, a genuine resemblance does not establish that two historical persons were identical.

8. The intervention in scholarship: history and allegory need not be alternatives
Section 8 situates the argument in existing scholarship.

Sharks discusses several intellectual traditions:

Catullan scholarship on pseudonyms, poetic addressees, and the distinction between poet and speaker.

David Dawson's work on allegory as cultural revision.

Erich Auerbach's concept of figura, in which two figures can be related while both retain historical reality.

A. J. Woodman's work on rhetoric in classical historiography.

Steve Mason's treatment of Josephus as a literary and historical author.

Judith Lieu's work on the construction of Christian identity.

The point is not that previous scholarship has overlooked naming, allegory, or constructed identity.

Rather, Sharks argues that scholarship generally treats these phenomena within separate disciplinary compartments.

His proposed extension is to investigate whether the same operations recur across works that are ordinarily classified as belonging to different authors, periods, genres, and religious traditions.

There is an important qualification: Sharks explicitly acknowledges that this draft has not established a general history of scholarly suppression or exclusion. Such a claim would require independent historical evidence about reviews, editorial practices, correspondence, and institutional decisions.

This restraint distinguishes the paper's actual thesis from a broader polemic about academic orthodoxy.

9. The methodological test: how to distinguish a real crossing from a resemblance
Section 9 converts the theoretical proposal into a research protocol.

Sharks defines the working unit as a documented crossing: a relationship in which a designation retains or transfers a consequential feature across persons, speaking positions, functions, or textual settings.

He proposes recording eight elements for every crossing:

Witness and locus: The actual edition, language, recension, passage, and words.

Naming act: Who names whom, and whether the naming is explicit.

Retained relation: What wording, sequence, role, or relationship persists.

Transformation: What changes and how.

Access: Who knows the name or relationship.

Consequence: What the naming authorizes or makes possible.

Direction: Whether the relationship is composition, reception, revision, or unresolved.

Alternative and break: What competing explanation exists and what evidence would defeat the proposal.

This is a substantial part of the paper's contribution.

Sharks wants an interpretation to be falsifiable. It must specify what would count against it.

For example, a common motif appearing in two works carries less evidentiary weight than a distinctive sequence of words, roles, and transformations appearing in the same order.

He also insists on distinguishing:

Explicit identities from modern reconstructions.

A translator's intervention from an author's original composition.

Recurring offices from recurring individuals.

Multiple independent witnesses from multiple texts repeating one earlier allegation.

The argument is therefore not that every resemblance demonstrates hidden authorship or coordination.

It is that a sufficiently constrained pattern of transformations may warrant a larger compositional or transmission hypothesis.

The author-boundary question
Sections 9a and 9b extend this reasoning to authorship itself.

Sharks draws on earlier Alexanarch investigations into the boundaries between Plato, Aristotle, Theophrastus, and Socrates.

The underlying question is whether a distinction between two author-names necessarily establishes two composing persons.

He distinguishes several possible functions of an author boundary:

Locating and citing a textual object.

Assigning accountability for a published work.

Describing a literary function.

Establishing a production history.

Reconstructing textual transmission.

Interpreting relations between differentiated speaking positions.

These are not equivalent claims.

A name may be indispensable for describing a literary position without proving that the position was produced by a separate biological author.

This distinction is crucial because the essay's broader theory of naming applies to the classification of literature itself. Author-names are also names that can preserve, divide, and organize literary identities.

10. The concluding hypothesis: one maker can produce many literary persons
The final section, §10, makes the paper's most consequential move.

Sharks introduces the archive's existing production hypothesis: that one self-dividing maker composed works received under the names Plato, Aristotle, and Theophrastus, distributing different literary and philosophical positions across those names and Socrates.

This is not simply another example of pseudonymity. It is a hypothesis about the relationship between compositional unity and literary plurality.

Sharks argues that differentiated literary positions must remain meaningful even if they originate in one maker.

A teacher, receiver, accuser, beloved, prophet, and historian must have different capacities and consequences. Their differences are not necessarily evidence of different biological authors; they may be the means through which a composition thinks.

He writes:

“Recognition of their relation should deepen those differences.”

This is an important qualification. A theory of unity that reduces every character or voice to a cosmetic disguise would explain less, not more.

The larger ancient literary field is then proposed as an extension of this research programme. But Sharks does not claim to have demonstrated that every text in his dossier shares one author or one coordinated production history.

He requires each additional connection to establish its own textual evidence.

What the paper is ultimately arguing
I would summarize the paper's arguments in five propositions.

Proposition

Argument

1. Names construct identity

A name can establish a literary, spiritual, political, or historical position.

2. Identity is relational

Figures acquire meaning through their relationships with earlier texts and other named figures.

3. History and allegory coexist

A literary transformation does not automatically invalidate historical reference.

4. Literary transmission constructs authority

Attribution, translation, genealogy, and archival classification can shape the identities they appear merely to record.

5. Unity does not eliminate plurality

A single compositional agency could produce multiple genuinely differentiated literary positions, but this requires independent evidence.

My assessment of the argument
I think three aspects deserve particular attention.

First, the strongest contribution is the distinction between identifying a person and explaining what a name does.

This is a genuinely productive interpretive distinction. The Catullus–Sappho comparison, Cicero's performed ancestral voices, and Philo's account of renaming offer different kinds of primary-text evidence for it.

The argument does not depend on accepting an unconventional historical identification.

Second, the paper's treatment of textual transmission is unusually important.

The Whiston/ei exestin correction is a good example. It demonstrates how a later translation can become incorporated into an apparent source tradition. The same general problem applies to historical cataloguing: the evidence through which we recognize an author or person may itself have been shaped by an earlier interpretive operation.

Third, the paper's largest hypothesis remains less established than its local readings.

There is a difference between demonstrating that:

Catullus transforms Sappho;

Philo theorizes names as transformations;

Revelation deliberately distributes and withholds names;

Josephus constructs historical authority;

and demonstrating that these phenomena belong to a coordinated compositional system spanning the entire dossier.

The first set of arguments has identifiable textual foundations. The larger claim requires more extensive comparative controls, independent evidence of transmission, and demonstrations of directionality.

Sharks recognizes this problem and makes it central to §9. That is a strength, although it also means the paper should be read as a research programme and theoretical proposal rather than a completed demonstration of a unified ancient literary construction.

There is also a limitation the author acknowledges in §8: some scholarly engagements are preliminary bibliographic contacts rather than full readings of the relevant monographs and articles.

Final interpretation
The title's three principal terms can now be understood:

History: The identities, genealogies, persons, and authorities claimed by ancient texts.

Allegory: The additional meanings and transformations those identities acquire through literary and interpretive operations.

Binding: The process by which names connect texts, figures, traditions, and authorial positions into a larger literary field.

The deepest argument is that these three dimensions cannot reliably be separated by first identifying the historical person and then treating everything else as literary decoration.

Instead, the reader must investigate how the text constructs the person, how that person carries earlier textual material, and how the resulting identity acquires historical and interpretive authority.

The final question of §10 captures the project particularly well:

“How does the writing make a name carry a person, a prior text, a transformation, and a claim to authority at once?”

That, I think, is the most accurate statement of the essay's intellectual ambition.
