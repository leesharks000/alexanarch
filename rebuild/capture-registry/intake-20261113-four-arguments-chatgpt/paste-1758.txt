The argument of In the Midst of the Seven
Johannes Sigil's In the Midst of the Seven: Philo, the Logos Named and Lived, and the Seven Stations of Revelation (EA-MIDST-01 v1.1, Alexanarch deposit #1758) advances a substantial argument about the literary relationship between Philo of Alexandria and the Book of Revelation.

Its central thesis is that Revelation's opening vision and seven letters are built upon a Philonic theological and symbolic architecture: the Logos, the high priest, the seven-branched lampstand, the seven planetary gods, and the sun occupying the middle position.

The paper goes further than identifying parallels. It argues for literary dependence, or transmission of a Philonic construction through an intermediary tradition. It also proposes that the Haran Gawaita, a Mandaean text, provides an interpretive key to Revelation's seven stations.

There are three important distinctions in reading the argument:

What Philo actually says in the surviving Greek texts.

What the author argues Revelation does with that material.

What remains conjectural, particularly the historical route by which the material reached Revelation.

I have located the actual deposit and its full-text presentation, rather than relying on the title or an external description. The following reconstructs its arguments from the text, including its qualifications and its version 1.1 revisions.

1. Philo's Logos is a named, stamped and embodied principle
Part I develops a sequence of operations rather than claiming that Philo explicitly equates Jesus with the Logos.

The sequence is:

The Logos receives names.

The Logos impresses or stamps a divine pattern.

That pattern is embodied in human lives.

A human bearer can receive and enact the pattern.

The pattern is transmitted through speech that originates elsewhere.

The author assembles this argument principally from De confusione linguarum, De mutatione nominum, De vita Mosis, and Quis rerum divinarum heres sit.

1.1. The significance of Ἀνατολή (Anatolē, Rising)
The crucial passage is Philo's De confusione linguarum §62.

Philo quotes Zechariah 6:12, where the Septuagint speaks of Ἀνατολή, the Rising or Branch, in an oracle addressed to the crowned high priest Ἰησοῦς (Jesus/Joshua), son of Iosedek.

The paper emphasizes a particular textual operation:

Philo takes Ἀνατολή as a name of the Logos.

He quotes the oracle without identifying its addressee.

He refers to the speaker as one of Moses' companions rather than naming Zechariah.

The name of the high priest to whom the oracle is addressed is consequently absent from Philo's quotation.

The author calls this omission a socket.

The argument is that Philo's quotation leaves a place that a reader consulting the Septuagint can fill: the missing name is Jesus, specifically Jesus the high priest, son of Iosedek.

This is an important distinction. The paper does not establish that Philo consciously intended the reader to make this identification. It establishes the omission and interprets its literary potential.

1.2. The prophetic voice and displacement
The paper then connects this passage with Philo's theory of prophecy in Quis rerum divinarum heres sit §§259–265.

Philo writes:

προφήτης γὰρ ἴδιον μὲν οὐδὲν ἀποφθέγγεται, ἀλλότρια δὲ πάντα ὑπηχοῦντος ἑτέρου.

The prophet speaks nothing of his own; everything is another's, with another sounding through him.

This is a particularly important connection because the same vocabulary of utterance occurs in De confusione §62.

The paper argues that the naming of the Logos and prophetic displacement belong to the same conceptual structure:

A speaker is the vehicle of another's voice.

The Logos can be approached through names that are not its proper name.

The divine pattern manifests itself through a human or prophetic bearer.

This is not simply a comparison of two isolated passages. The author finds the same conceptual and verbal machinery operating across Philo's works.

1.3. The renaming of Hosea as Jesus
The second major component comes from De mutatione nominum §§121–123.

Philo discusses the renaming of Hosea as Ἰησοῦς.

The paper's interpretation depends on the language of stamping and re-striking, particularly the vocabulary of coins and impressions.

A change of name is not merely a change of designation. It signifies a change in the condition of the person.

The paper reads the transformation as the striking of a mortal quality into an enduring or perfected state.

It is careful to correct an earlier, stronger formulation: Philo does not explicitly write that Jesus is the name of the Logos. The Greek passage glosses the name as σωτηρία κυρίου, and describes it as the name of the best disposition or state.

The author instead constructs the connection from several passages:

The Logos is the divine renamer.

Renaming is a re-striking of character.

Hosea receives the name Jesus.

Jesus is associated with the imitation of Moses.

The divine pattern can consequently be reproduced in a human life.

1.4. Moses as the model and Jesus as imitator
The paper brings in Life of Moses and Philo's discussion of Moses as a model of the perfected life.

Jesus, the son of Nun, is presented as the imitator who receives this pattern.

The important qualification is that Jesus is one receiver among several. The author does not reduce every instance of divine embodiment to one person or claim that Philo makes a simple identity between Jesus and the Logos.

The argument is about a transmissible pattern: a divine quality can be impressed upon a human life.

1.5. The sealed Name, stones and manna
The author extends the stamping argument through Philo's accounts of the high priest's vestments and the sacred objects.

Three elements are especially important:

The sealed Name: the high priest's crown carries the divine Name in sealed form.

The stones: names are engraved or impressed upon precious stones, using the vocabulary of stamping.

The manna: Philo treats heavenly food as the Logos, which impresses and strengthens the soul.

The paper then makes a particularly interesting connection with Revelation 2:17.

The conqueror receives a white stone bearing a new name, which nobody knows except the person receiving it.

The proposed transformation is:

The author interprets the white stone as a juror's pebble: white signifies acquittal, black condemnation. Philo's discussion of divine judgment supplies the negative side, while Revelation turns the verdict into a gift of acquittal.

The most ambitious part of this argument is the connection between the missing name in Philo's Zechariah quotation and the name known only to its recipient in Revelation.

In the author's reading, Revelation fills the socket that Philo's quotation leaves open.

The discussion of Justin Martyr is an additional step. Justin gathers the names that Philo distributes across different contexts—Jesus, Israel, Anatolē and the Logos—onto one figure. The author calls this a consolidation, but explicitly does not establish that Justin directly read Philo.

2. The central structural argument: the seventh that divides
This is arguably the most important part of the entire paper.

The author identifies a remarkable concentration of material in one Philonic treatise: Quis rerum divinarum heres sit, particularly §§130 and 215–225.

Philo brings together three related sevenfold structures.

2.1. The lampstand
In §§215–219, Philo interprets the seven-branched lampstand.

There are six lateral branches, three on either side, and a seventh branch in the middle.

Philo identifies the seventh with the dividing Logos.

His formulation is:

ἑξὰς εἰς δύο τριάδας ὑπὸ μέσου τοῦ ἑβδόμου λόγου διῄρηται.

The six is divided into two triads by the seventh Logos in the middle.

The seventh is therefore not merely the last member of a numerical series. It performs an operation: it divides and orders the six.

The Logos is a mediating and organizing principle.

2.2. The seven planets
Philo then interprets the lampstand as a representation of the seven planets (Her. 221–224).

The sun occupies the middle position.

The paper is precise about this: Philo does not call the Logos a planet.

Rather, the author argues that the correspondence between the sun's central position and the Logos's central position is established through the shared lampstand symbolism.

This distinction matters because it avoids attributing a statement to Philo that he does not make.

2.3. The sevenfold soul
In §225, Philo applies the same architecture to the soul.

The soul is divided into six parts, and the Logos is the seventh, the divine principle that divides them.

3. Revelation transforms the single lampstand into seven independent stations
This is the central argument of Part II.

The author distinguishes the symbolism of Philo and Zechariah from that of Revelation.

Philo has one lampstand with seven lamps.

Zechariah has one lampstand with seven lamps.

Revelation has seven separate lampstands.

This is not treated as an incidental variation.

The paper argues that Revelation transforms a unified cosmic instrument into seven individually accountable stations.

The figure in Revelation 1:12–20 stands in the middle of the seven lampstands. The letters of chapters 2–3 address the seven messengers, each associated with a church.

The transformation is therefore:

One organized sevenfold structure becomes seven distinct stations, each individually judged and addressed by the central figure.

3.1. The messengers as stations of transmission
The author pays close attention to the Greek grammar of the letters.

Each letter is addressed to the angel or messenger of a church, not directly to the church.

There are three voices or positions:

The speaker in the midst of the lampstands.

The messenger who is addressed.

The Spirit who speaks to the churches.

This is compared with Philo's account of angels as intermediaries who transmit the ruler's commands to subjects and the subjects' needs back to the ruler.

The paper also uses Philo's distinction between true and counterfeit angels. Some messengers faithfully transmit; others distort.

This gives the letters a more elaborate structure than seven independent moral exhortations.

They are seven communications operating through seven stations.

3.2. Each station is judged, corrected or confirmed
The author reads every letter as a complete unit, considering:

The attribute of the speaker.

The condition of the station.

The demand made upon it.

The promise given to the conqueror.

Its planetary assignment in the author's existing mapping, deposit #1101.

Pergamum, Thyatira and Laodicea have the strongest correspondences on the wording of the letters themselves.

Ephesus has a textual connection through the testing of false messengers.

Smyrna, Sardis and Philadelphia depend more heavily on the planetary mapping.

The author explicitly acknowledges that alternative planetary associations are possible for some of the weaker cases.

3.3. Retention is conditional
One of the paper's more interesting theological arguments concerns what happens to the seven.

The stations are not dissolved.

Instead, the occupants are corrected, confirmed or threatened with removal.

Ephesus can lose its lampstand. Laodicea risks being expelled. Smyrna and Philadelphia, by contrast, receive no reproach.

The sevenfold structure is preserved, but continued occupation depends on the response of each station.

This becomes especially important in the comparison with the Mandaean material.

4. The Haran Gawaita provides an interpretive key to the seven
This is one of the most distinctive arguments introduced and strengthened in version 1.1.

The paper reads two Mandaean works:

The Ginza, which presents the seven planetary powers as seducers and corrupting influences.

The Haran Gawaita, which describes the rejection of Ruha's seven sons and their replacement by seven guards of the Life.

The distinction between these texts is important.

4.1. The Ginza: the seven as corrupting powers
The paper identifies the seven planetary figures in the Ginza as a configuration of demonic or deceptive powers.

They include:

Šamiš, the Sun.

Libat, Venus.

Nbu, Mercury.

Sin, the Moon.

Kewan, Saturn.

Bel, Jupiter.

Nerig, Mars.

The seven are associated with seduction, deception, false prophecy, sexual disorder and distorted religious authority.

Mercury is particularly important because Nbu is described as a lying messiah.

The author sees a correspondence with Revelation's concern about false apostles and false messengers.

4.2. The Haran Gawaita: replacement rather than abolition
The Haran Gawaita supplies a more elaborate structure.

It describes Ruha and her seven sons, associated with the House of the Seven. These powers occupy a sevenfold arrangement.

But the text also describes a counter-operation.

Hibil-Ziwa sends Anuš-ʿUthra with seven darts and seven guards from Mount Parwan.

These seven guards are installed at seven locations and ultimately placed:

At the seven corners of the House.

Upon the seven horns of the worlds.

To crush the power of Darkness.

To establish the Call of the Life.

The author interprets this as a reseating of the seven.

The sevenfold structure survives, but its occupants change.

This is the conceptual distinction that organizes the comparison:

Haran Gawaita

Revelation

Seven stations remain

Seven stations remain

Ruha's seven are rejected

Corrupt occupants are challenged

Seven guards of the Life replace them

Messengers are addressed and corrected

Replacement of occupants

Purification and conditional retention

The two texts are not claimed to have the same historical origin or to be dependent upon one another.

Rather, the Haran Gawaita offers a model for understanding how a sevenfold cosmic configuration can be preserved while its occupants are judged and transformed.

4.3. The seven communities and the seven churches
Version 1.1 takes this argument further.

The Haran Gawaita identifies planetary powers with communities and locations.

For example:

Mercury establishes a community at Qum.

Saturn establishes a community at Sinai.

Ruha establishes a community associated with Jerusalem.

It also names the seven guards and their respective stations.

The author then applies this arrangement to Revelation 1:20, where the seven stars are identified as the angels of the seven churches and the seven lampstands as the seven churches.

The proposal is that the Mandaean configuration supplies an interpretive model: seven powers, seven stations, seven communities, and specific occupants associated with each.

4.4. Applying the Gawaita station by station
The paper identifies several especially strong correspondences.

Ephesus and Mercury

The Haran Gawaita identifies Mercury with a false messiah who operates through fraud and sorcery.

Revelation 2:2 praises Ephesus for testing those who claim to be apostles but are not.

The connection is the testing of false messengers.

Pergamum and Mars

The Haran Gawaita associates Mars with the Son of Slaughter, a false prophet who converts people through the sword.

Pergamum is the station where Satan's throne is located, where Antipas is killed, and where the speaker threatens to fight with the sword of his mouth.

The sword is the central correspondence.

Thyatira and Venus

The Ginza identifies Ruha with Venus.

The Haran Gawaita describes Ruha's deceptive activity, including her use of a disguised identity and dictated writings that cause division.

Revelation 2:20 describes a false prophetess who teaches and leads the servants astray into sexual immorality.

The correspondence is between a false female prophetic authority and the corruption of a community.

Laodicea and the Sun

The Haran Gawaita associates deliverance with the day of the Sun and the saved person's placement at the right hand.

Laodicea ends with the promise of sitting with the speaker on his throne.

Both configurations close on enthronement.

The author also considers Sardis and Saturn, but treats the relationship more cautiously. The Haran Gawaita supplies a community founded by Saturn at Sinai, while Revelation's Sardis is characterized by the paradox of having a name for life while being dead.

The text does not supply equivalent planetary communities for the Moon and Jupiter, so the author does not pretend to establish all seven assignments through this source.

That qualification is important: the Haran Gawaita supports four particularly developed station correspondences, one weaker one, and leaves two without a planetary identification.

4.5. Why the text's own claims matter
The author insists that the Haran Gawaita be read on its own terms.

It calls itself the Great Revelation and presents itself as an unveiling of hidden history.

It claims to disclose the history of the Nasoraeans, the House of the Seven, and the prophets of the Lie.

The author's methodological position is that this self-description should be taken seriously as evidence of how the text wants to function.

It is not simply a later document being mined for isolated parallels.

It presents itself as a key to a hidden sevenfold history, and the author applies that key to Revelation.

There is, however, an important chronological limitation. The Haran Gawaita is transmitted in a much later form, including references to Muhammad and the Arab-era setting of its surviving transmission. It cannot straightforwardly establish that Revelation borrowed from it.

Its value in the paper is interpretive, not proof of historical priority.

The author also identifies further correspondences: the sealed book, the eighth arising from the seven, the number forty-two, the crowned envoy, and counterfeit prophetic writing. These are presented as additional interpretive yields, not as demonstrated lines of literary dependence.

5. Plato's two Aphrodites: the precedent for judging and purifying a power
Part II also introduces a philosophical precedent from Plato's Symposium 180d–181a.

Pausanias distinguishes two Aphrodites:

Heavenly Aphrodite (Ourania).

Common Aphrodite (Pandemos).

The author is interested not merely in the existence of two goddesses, but in the principle behind the distinction.

A power or activity is not necessarily good or bad in itself. Its moral character depends on how it is exercised.

This is connected to Philo's treatment of renaming as the correction of character.

The author then applies this to Revelation's Thyatira letter.

Jezebel's sexual immorality is condemned, but the morning star is promised to the conqueror.

The power is not simply abolished. Its distortion is judged, and its proper gift is retained.

The paper distinguishes three related precedents:

Plato's Pausanias divides Aphrodite into heavenly and common forms and supplies a principle for judging conduct.

Xenophon preserves the possibility of two Aphrodites and two corresponding forms of worship.

Philo preserves the symposium but changes its company, culminating in the rising sun.

This is a supporting argument for the paper's broader theme: purification need not mean the destruction of the underlying structure or power.

The same principle helps explain the contrast between the Mandaean replacement of the occupants and Revelation's correction of them.

6. Revelation is itself a commission to produce further writing
Part III advances a different but related thesis.

The author argues that Revelation does not merely contain seven letters. It establishes a commission involving writing, transmission and testimony.

This follows from Philo's theory of prophetic displacement.

If the prophet speaks another's words, then writing under divine command is the written form of that same displacement.

6.1. The grammar of the commission
The author emphasizes that the seven letters are addressed to the angels of the churches, not directly to the churches themselves.

The seer, John, is commanded:

To write what he sees.

To put it in a book.

To send it to the seven churches.

The command γράψον, write, appears twelve times in the book.

There is also a messenger sent to testify to the churches.

The paper separates four agents:

The speaker who commands the writing.

John, who writes and sends.

The seven messengers who are addressed.

The Spirit who speaks to the churches.

The commission is therefore a chain of transmission, not simply a set of messages delivered directly from Christ to seven congregations.

6.2. The letters as the manifestation of the commission
Here the author advances a more speculative thesis.

The seven letters are read as the literal manifestation of the commission contained in Revelation.

In this interpretation, the book establishes an ongoing practice of authorized writing and transmission.

The author connects this with the early Christian letters:

Galatians 4:14: the messenger received as an angel of God.

2 Corinthians 11:13–14: false apostles and false angels.

Galatians 1:8: the testing of an angel's message.

1 Thessalonians 5:27: the command that a letter be read publicly.

Colossians 4:16: the circulation of letters between churches.

These passages are assembled into a proposed downstream sequence:

Authority is received through a messenger.

The messenger is tested.

The message is publicly read.

Letters circulate among churches.

Individual churches receive letters under their own names.

The question is whether this sequence reflects an epistolary practice that existed before Revelation, which Revelation organizes, or whether Revelation establishes the commission that subsequent letters carry out.

The author does not claim to have settled the direction.

This is an important restraint: the paper provides the evidence and the test, but leaves the historical arrow open.

7. The history of scholarship: how the connection was filed away
Part IV examines the reception history of the Philonic parallels.

The central figures are Wilhelm Bousset and R. H. Charles.

The author's argument is that modern scholarship recognized many of the relevant connections but repeatedly assigned them to separate explanatory categories.

7.1. Bousset
Bousset discusses the relationship between the seven-branched lampstand and the planetary gods.

He cites Philo's Quis rerum divinarum heres sit and recognizes the planetary background of the imagery.

But he suggests that the Apocalyptist no longer understood the original symbolism.

In other words, the correspondence survived, but the author of Revelation was supposedly unaware of its significance.

Bousset also discusses the Logos name, the unknown name, and Mandaean parallels concerning the power of hidden names.

7.2. Charles
Charles is especially important because he cites Philo's planetary lampstand discussion.

He recognizes the connection between the seven-branched lampstand and the seven planets, but suggests that the material is merely an afterthought in Philo and Josephus.

He also describes the Seer as probably unconscious of the original correspondence.

Yet elsewhere Charles attributes deliberate design to Revelation.

In his discussion of the twelve foundation stones of the New Jerusalem, he considers their ordering and the reversal of the zodiacal sequence.

He concludes that the arrangement cannot be accidental.

7.3. The author's criticism: unequal standards of evidence
This is where the paper makes a methodological argument about scholarship itself.

Why is an internal sequence treated as evidence of deliberate design in one case, while a closely related numerical and symbolic arrangement is treated as unconscious inheritance in another?

The author argues that the difference is the warrant being applied.

When Charles sees a sequence with a discernible direction, he credits the Seer with design.

When he sees the seven lampstands and their planetary significance, he files the connection as inherited material that the Seer no longer understands.

The paper proposes that its own planetary mapping reveals a directional structure in the seven letters.

8. Hebrews supplies a particularly important comparison
The paper makes a final comparison with Hebrews 4:8–14.

This passage brings together three things that the paper has assembled from Philo:

Joshua (Ἰησοῦς), son of Nun.

The dividing Logos, described as sharper than a two-edged sword and capable of dividing soul and spirit.

Jesus as the great high priest.

The author sees a striking correspondence with Philo's construction:

The Logos as the universal divider (Her. 130).

The Logos as the seventh that divides the soul (Her. 225).

The high priest as a figure of the Logos (Fug. 108).

Joshua as the human bearer of the Mosaic pattern.

The same elements appear in Revelation's figure with the sharp two-edged sword, the priestly robe and the central position among the seven lampstands.

The author notes that dependence of Hebrews on Philo has been extensively discussed in scholarship, although it remains contested.

The implication is methodological: if scholars are prepared to consider a Philonic genealogy for Hebrews when these elements converge, they should take seriously the possibility of a comparable, and potentially stronger, relationship with Revelation.

The author is not saying that Hebrews proves Revelation's dependence. Rather, Hebrews demonstrates that the proposed combination of Joshua, Logos, sword and high priest is intelligible within an established Philonic reception problem.

9. The paper's ultimate argument
The conclusion, particularly §§16–17, brings the different strands together.

The author's argument can be reconstructed as five propositions.

First: Philo has an integrated theological architecture.

The Logos is the divider, the central seventh, the principle that orders the lampstand and the soul. The sun occupies the middle of the seven planets. The Logos is also the principle through which divine speech is transmitted.

These are not disconnected ideas: they occur together in Quis rerum divinarum heres sit.

Second: Philo's Logos is named and embodied through a system of transmission.

The Logos has many names, including Ἀνατολή. Its pattern can be impressed upon human beings through renaming and imitation. The prophetic voice is another instance of the same underlying structure: a human bearer communicates what originates elsewhere.

Third: Revelation transforms this architecture.

It takes the sevenfold lampstand configuration and makes it seven independent stations. The central figure addresses each station, judges its condition and offers correction, confirmation or removal.

The figure in the middle bears a cluster of Philonic characteristics: the solar face, the priestly robe, the sword, the Logos identity and the names associated with divine authority.

Fourth: Revelation transforms the naming and stamping system.

The omission of Jesus from Philo's quotation of Zechariah becomes significant when read alongside Revelation's named figure, its white stone, its hidden name and its new name.

The paper proposes that Revelation fills the textual socket left by Philo.

Fifth: the sevenfold system is preserved through correction rather than abolition.

The Haran Gawaita supplies an illuminating comparative model: seven stations remain, but their occupants are replaced. Revelation likewise retains the seven, although its operation is correction and conditional confirmation rather than wholesale replacement.

The paper's final formulation is that Revelation's opening vision and seven letters are built on the Philonic construction of the lampstand, high priest and Logos.

It explicitly argues dependence, rather than merely resemblance.

But it leaves open whether the Apocalypse received Philo's actual texts or inherited a tradition that carried his interpretation.

There are several limitations worth emphasizing.

The planetary mapping is not equally secure across all seven churches. The paper acknowledges this and proposes an independent constraint test.

The Haran Gawaita is chronologically later in its surviving transmission. It cannot establish the direction of dependence upon Revelation.

The historical route remains unknown. A shared tradition remains possible.

Philo's intention concerning the omitted high priest is not established. The omission is demonstrable; its interpretation as a deliberate or functional socket is the author's argument.

The paper does not establish that Philo knew of Jesus of Nazareth or the Jesus movement.

These qualifications do not negate the central thesis. They identify precisely where textual observation ends and historical reconstruction begins.

11. My assessment: what is most significant about the argument?
I see three particularly important contributions.

A. The concentration of evidence in one Philonic treatise
The strongest feature is the concentration in Quis rerum divinarum heres sit.

The Logos as divider, the lampstand, the planetary seven, the central sun, the sevenfold soul and prophetic displacement are not scattered randomly across the entire Philonic corpus.

They are brought together in one sustained interpretive construction.

That makes the comparison with Revelation more substantial than a collection of superficial numerical parallels.

The author is identifying a coherent symbolic system.

B. The distinction between preserving a structure and correcting its occupants
The comparison between Revelation and the Haran Gawaita gives the sevenfold argument a genuinely interesting conceptual dimension.

The question is not simply whether the seven planets are good or evil.

It is whether the sevenfold structure itself is retained while its powers are judged.

The Mandaean material provides replacement; Revelation provides correction and conditional retention.

This is a useful interpretive distinction that organizes otherwise disparate material.

C. The relationship between naming, embodiment and transmission
I would regard Part I as especially important to the whole paper.

The argument about Philo's Logos is not just that the Logos has many names.

It is that the Logos can be named, impressed, embodied, transmitted and received.

That gives the later discussion of the seven messengers a conceptual foundation.

The Logos is not simply a figure standing in the middle of the lampstands. It is also the principle through which the stations communicate, receive judgment and potentially transmit further revelation.

This is what allows the paper to join its discussion of Philo's prophetic doctrine to Revelation's commission to write.

12. The argument in one paragraph
The most concise statement of the paper's thesis would be:

Philo constructs a theology in which the Logos is the seventh, dividing principle at the centre of the lampstand, the principle corresponding to the sun's central position among the seven planets, and the divine voice that speaks through human bearers. He also describes the Logos through names, impressions and transformations of human character. Revelation takes this architecture and transforms it: the single lampstand becomes seven independent stations, the central figure addresses and judges each through its messenger, and the names, priestly imagery, sword, manna and white stone reorganize the Philonic system around a named figure. The Haran Gawaita offers a comparative interpretive key to the preservation and replacement of the seven, while the commission to write extends the architecture into a system of prophetic transmission. The result, the paper argues, is literary dependence on Philo, whether directly or through an intermediary tradition.
