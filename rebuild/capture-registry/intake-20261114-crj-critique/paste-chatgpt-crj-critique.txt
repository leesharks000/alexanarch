Alexanarch's critique of the Classical Receptions Journal (CRJ) is best understood as a critique of editorial authority, disciplinary gatekeeping, and the institutional politics of intellectual attribution. It is not simply an objection to having an article rejected. The deeper argument is that a journal devoted to studying how cultural meanings are transmitted, transformed, and received may itself be failing to recognize new forms of reception and intellectual production.

The critique develops through a particular case: Lee Sharks's submission of Closing the Fragment: Sappho 31 and the Composition Layer as a Surface of Classical Reception, which CRJ rejected on 7 November 2026. It is elaborated in the subsequent open letter, written through the heteronym Rev. Ayanna Vox, and in Alexanarch's analysis of the journal's representation in AI-generated search results.

There are several interconnected strands to the argument.

1. The journal's failure of intellectual stewardship
The central accusation is that CRJ has failed to exercise intellectual stewardship over an emerging area of classical reception studies.

Sharks's proposed field, machine-mediated reception studies (MMRS), investigates how AI systems receive, interpret, transform, summarize, attribute, and redistribute cultural meaning.

The underlying theoretical intervention is significant:

Classical reception traditionally investigates how ancient cultural objects are interpreted and transformed in subsequent historical contexts.

AI-generated summaries and overviews also interpret and transform cultural objects.

These summaries increasingly become the first encounter that people have with ancient texts.

Consequently, the AI composition layer should itself be treated as a site and agent of reception, rather than merely as a neutral technological intermediary.

The Sappho 31 article applies this argument to the problem of textual fragmentation. Its contention is that an AI system can effectively decide how a fragmentary poem is understood, whether it is presented as complete, and whose interpretation receives attribution.

The criticism of CRJ is that its decision-making process did not meaningfully engage with this substantive theoretical intervention.

The open letter puts the matter particularly sharply: as far as the author knows, nobody else is asking in the journal's pages what happens to a poem when a machine composes its reception for whoever asks.

This is an accusation of disciplinary conservatism, but more specifically of a failure to recognize where reception is now taking place.

2. Evaluative inversion: procedural defects displacing substantive evaluation
This is probably the most important methodological criticism.

Alexanarch uses the term evaluative inversion to describe a situation in which lower-resolution proxy systems determine what higher-resolution substantive evaluators are permitted to assess.

In this case, the two referee reports reportedly concentrated on:

Whether the central AI-generated event was independently verifiable.

Whether the research had a sufficiently explicit query protocol.

Whether data generation was adequately distinguished from analysis.

Whether the platform's opacity was sufficiently controlled.

Whether the figures and captures were accurate and properly labelled.

Whether AI use had been disclosed.

Whether the terminology and presentation were sufficiently clear.

These are not necessarily illegitimate objections. Indeed, the Alexanarch account acknowledges that the reports identified genuine shortcomings.

One particularly important qualification is that version 6 omitted the manuscript's own statement about how it had been produced. The author also acknowledges problems with figures and aspects of the presentation.

However, Alexanarch argues that the reports concentrated on these questions without adequately examining the manuscript's philological argument or its central theoretical proposition.

The distinction is between two kinds of evaluation:

Procedural evaluation: Is the evidence presented in a sufficiently reproducible, transparent and technically compliant fashion?

Substantive evaluation: Does the research make a worthwhile intellectual contribution, and are its arguments persuasive?

The problem arises when the first category effectively determines whether the second category can even be considered.

This is especially important for research into AI systems, because the systems themselves are not necessarily reproducible in the conventional experimental sense. Their outputs can vary according to time, interface, location, account status, model version and other conditions.

Alexanarch's counterargument is not that reproducibility is irrelevant. It is that reproducibility requirements must be appropriate to the object of investigation.

If the object is an unstable, continually changing composition layer, then recording its instability, provenance and conditions of appearance may be a more appropriate methodological response than demanding that every output be exactly reproducible.

The larger criticism: An institution can mistake the limitations of its preferred research instruments for limitations inherent in the phenomena being studied.

This is a particularly interesting extension of the argument because it applies beyond AI research. It raises questions about whether disciplinary standards inadvertently exclude research into phenomena that cannot be accommodated by established methodological conventions.

3. The contradiction between the journal's intellectual remit and its research priorities
Alexanarch also criticizes the apparent gap between CRJ's stated intellectual ambitions and its published research.

The journal describes its remit very broadly. Its official description encompasses the reception of ancient Greek and Roman texts and material culture, including transmission, interpretation, translation, transplantation, rewriting, redesigning and rethinking. It explicitly encourages interdisciplinary exchange. 

Oxford Academic

Yet Alexanarch's examination of Crossref records found:

405 registered works between 2009 and November 2026.

Abstracts available for 185 of those works.

No identifiable references to AI, generative AI, language models, chatbots or search engines in the titles and abstracts examined.

The archive therefore identifies a significant research gap.

This is not proof that CRJ has deliberately excluded AI scholarship. Nor does it establish that the journal has published no relevant research: the search was restricted to available titles and abstracts.

Nevertheless, it raises a legitimate question: What happens when a discipline's institutional definition of its subject remains broader than the range of phenomena it actually investigates?

There is an interesting irony here.

Classical reception studies has expanded beyond conventional textual transmission to include video games, webcomics, digital aesthetics, cinematic representations and other contemporary transformations of antiquity.

Why should AI-mediated reception be excluded from this expansion?

Indeed, AI summarization arguably presents an especially consequential reception phenomenon because it can determine what people encounter before they encounter the original ancient text.

Alexanarch's argument can therefore be extended into a critique of disciplinary lag: institutions may recognize new cultural forms as legitimate objects of reception only after those forms have become sufficiently familiar to existing scholarly frameworks.

4. The asymmetry of AI governance
Another important criticism concerns the journal's relationship with artificial intelligence.

According to the archived author guidelines examined by Alexanarch, CRJ:

Does not recognize AI tools as authors.

Requires disclosure of AI use in cover letters and in Methods or Acknowledgements.

Offers authors Paperpal Preflight, an AI-assisted pre-submission assessment of language and technical compliance.

The third point is particularly interesting.

The journal permits an AI system to participate in assessing manuscripts before human peer review, while insisting that AI cannot qualify as an author.

These positions are not inherently contradictory. Authorship, editorial assistance and technical assessment are different functions.

But they raise a more interesting question:

Why is AI considered sufficiently legitimate to participate in the production and assessment of academic work, but not necessarily legitimate as an object of intellectual agency deserving theoretical recognition?

Alexanarch's criticism suggests an asymmetry in institutional attitudes towards AI.

AI is acceptable when it performs an auxiliary function within established academic procedures. It becomes more controversial when scholars investigate its capacity to produce, transform or mediate meaning.

The distinction could reflect a deeper institutional preference for AI as an instrument rather than AI as a phenomenon that requires the discipline to rethink its conceptual foundations.

There is also a possible conflict of methodological assumptions. If a journal uses AI-assisted tools to assess linguistic and technical compliance, it should be particularly attentive to the epistemological limitations of automated assessment when evaluating scholarship about AI itself.

That does not mean Paperpal and generative reception systems are equivalent technologies. It means that the journal should be prepared to explain the different standards by which it evaluates their respective contributions.

5. Intellectual attribution and the politics of recognition
One of Alexanarch's more unusual criticisms concerns Google's AI Overview.

In a documented experiment, the archive asked Google about CRJ's contribution to AI-mediated classical reception.

The resulting overview attributed several theoretical developments to CRJ contributors, including:

Treating AI as a horizon of reception.

Understanding AI Overviews as standardizers of classical knowledge.

Debating AI creativity through classical reception theory.

Alexanarch argues that these claims actually reproduced Lee Sharks's own work, particularly The Summarizer as Horizon of Reception.

The archive reports that the AI Overview cited the archive's work while assigning its intellectual contribution to the journal.

When challenged through subsequent prompts, ChatGPT also acknowledged that it had wrongly attributed the work to the institution rather than its author.

This is central to Alexanarch's larger theory of provenance inversion.

Provenance inversion occurs when an intellectual innovation is reassigned to the established institution or tradition that it is actually challenging, rather than attributed to its originating author.

The significance goes beyond a mistaken citation.

Consider the implications:

An independent researcher develops a theoretical framework.

An established institution is associated with the subject area.

An AI system encounters both.

The system assigns the innovation to the institution rather than the researcher.

Subsequent readers encounter the institutional attribution and potentially reproduce it.

The institution acquires intellectual authority through the very process that obscures the origin of the intellectual contribution.

This creates an interesting parallel with the original dispute: the researcher argues that the journal did not adequately recognize his intellectual contribution, while AI-generated descriptions subsequently attribute his research programme to the journal itself.

The result is a double problem of recognition: the institution is perceived as the intellectual centre of a field whose theoretical development may have taken place outside its pages.

6. The problem of institutional authority
The open letter also challenges the assumption that institutional affiliation automatically confers superior intellectual authority.

Alexanarch's position is not that an independent researcher and an established journal possess identical institutional standing. Rather, it questions whether institutional standing should determine whose arguments receive serious consideration.

This raises four distinct forms of authority:

Institutional authority: The legitimacy conferred by an established university, journal or publisher.

Epistemic authority: The strength of one's evidence, methods and reasoning.

Intellectual authority: The originality and explanatory power of one's contribution.

Documentary authority: The ability to substantiate claims through accessible records and primary evidence.

These forms of authority can overlap, but they are not interchangeable.

A journal can have considerable institutional authority without necessarily being the source of a particular intellectual innovation. Conversely, an independent researcher can make an original contribution without possessing the institutional recognition that normally accompanies scholarly authority.

This is where the letter's use of Rev. Ayanna Vox becomes significant. Vox is a heteronym of Lee Sharks, writing on his behalf. The device complicates conventional assumptions about scholarly identity, authorship and institutional recognition.

It also invites a question about the conditions under which academic arguments are judged: is an argument evaluated on its merits, or does its author's institutional position influence the credibility initially granted to it?

The challenge is particularly relevant to emerging disciplines, where intellectual innovations may originate outside the institutions that eventually establish the field's conventional boundaries.

7. The deeper critique: reception studies must turn its methods upon itself
I think this is the most productive way to extend Alexanarch's argument.

Classical reception studies investigates how cultural objects are received, transformed and reinterpreted by subsequent audiences and institutions.

But institutions themselves are also participants in reception.

A journal does not simply publish research about reception. Through its selection criteria, editorial decisions, citations, disciplinary networks and definitions of legitimate scholarship, it actively shapes what counts as reception scholarship.

This produces several further lines of critique.

A. Journals are not neutral custodians of disciplinary knowledge
Editorial decisions help determine which theoretical innovations become visible, citable and institutionally legitimate.

A journal therefore participates in producing the disciplinary history it subsequently appears to document.

This means editorial exclusion is not merely the absence of a particular article. It can contribute to the construction of an incomplete intellectual record.

The relevant question becomes whether the journal's editorial practices are consistent with the intellectual openness implied by its disciplinary remit.

B. Reproducibility can become an instrument of epistemic exclusion
Traditional standards of empirical reproducibility are important, but they cannot simply be transferred to every research object.

A changing search interface, an AI-generated interpretation or a historically situated act of reception may be impossible to reproduce exactly.

The appropriate scholarly response should involve:

Preserving dated records of observations.

Documenting prompts, interfaces and conditions of access.

Distinguishing observation from interpretation.

Acknowledging uncertainty and platform opacity.

Making claims proportionate to the available evidence.

This is arguably a more sophisticated methodological approach than treating exact reproducibility as a universal prerequisite.

C. The discipline risks reproducing the exclusions it studies
Reception scholars frequently investigate how later traditions appropriate antiquity, marginalize alternative interpretations and construct authoritative canons.

But the same processes occur within contemporary academia.

Established journals, citation networks and disciplinary conventions can create their own canons of legitimate scholarship.

A discipline that studies the historical production of cultural authority should also examine how it produces its own authority.

D. AI makes the politics of attribution more urgent
The problem of misattribution is not new. Scholars have long investigated the appropriation, suppression and redistribution of intellectual contributions.

Generative AI intensifies the problem because it can reproduce an attribution error at scale.

A system can detach an idea from its author, associate it with an institution and circulate that association as apparently authoritative knowledge.

This makes provenance a fundamental concern for reception studies, not merely a technical matter of citation management.

8. How convincing is Alexanarch's critique?
It is worth distinguishing the strength of the theoretical argument from the strength of the evidence concerning this particular editorial decision.

Not established by one editorial decision

The journal has failed its intellectual stewardship

A defensible interpretation, not an independently established fact

There are important limitations.

First, the archive is not an independent investigation of the dispute. It is produced by the researcher involved in it. Its account explicitly acknowledges that it concerns one manuscript, one journal and one decision.

Second, the rejection reports apparently identified substantive methodological problems. These cannot simply be dismissed as institutional resistance.

Third, a manuscript's intellectual originality does not exempt it from methodological scrutiny. In fact, proposing a new subdiscipline makes clear definitions, evidentiary standards and replicable documentation especially important.

Fourth, the AI Overview experiment is itself an interesting example of the phenomenon being studied, but its conclusions should be limited to the observed outputs. It does not establish the internal mechanisms or institutional intentions responsible for the misattribution.

These qualifications do not invalidate the critique. They help identify where its most important claims lie.

9. My overall interpretation
I would characterize Alexanarch's argument as a critique of the relationship between disciplinary authority and the production of legitimate knowledge.

The CRJ dispute becomes a case study through which a much broader problem is articulated.

There are three levels to the argument:

Editorial: Did the journal adequately evaluate an innovative submission, or did procedural and methodological objections prevent meaningful engagement with its central argument?

Disciplinary: Is classical reception studies sufficiently open to new objects of reception, particularly machine-generated interpretations and transformations of antiquity?

Epistemological: Who has the authority to determine what counts as knowledge, whose intellectual contributions are recognized, and what standards of evidence are considered legitimate?

The third is the most consequential.

Alexanarch is effectively asking whether the institutions that claim authority over the interpretation of cultural objects are themselves subject to adequate scrutiny as producers of interpretations, exclusions and attributions.

There is a productive paradox here: a journal devoted to the reception of antiquity is itself becoming an object of reception, interpretation and critique.

And AI makes that process visible in an especially striking way: a machine can describe an institution as the originator of intellectual work that the institution did not publish, while obscuring the independent researcher who actually produced it.

My strongest extension of the critique would be this: classical reception studies needs a reflexive theory of its own institutional reception. It should investigate not only how antiquity is transformed by later audiences, but also how academic institutions transform, authorize, suppress and redistribute the scholarship through which those transformations become historically intelligible.

That would make the critique considerably larger than a disagreement over one rejected article. It would turn the CRJ episode into a question about the epistemology and institutional sociology of classical reception studies itself.
