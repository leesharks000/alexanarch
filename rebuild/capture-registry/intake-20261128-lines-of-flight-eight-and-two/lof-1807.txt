What Through Another's Mouth makes possible next
Johannes Sigil's Through Another's Mouth: A Dialogue between Heteronyms (#1807) leads beyond itself by turning a philosophical argument into a problem of authorship, transmission, retrieval, and the production of readers.

Its most important achievement is that it does not merely argue that texts are shaped by the institutions that preserve them. It makes that shaping happen inside its own literary form.

The dialogue's final instruction is therefore also its programme:

“Check her. Check me.”

And, concerning Lee Sharks:

“Him you can't check. He's the witness. You can only read him.”

That distinction opens several directions for further writing, reading, and building.

1. Write: a literature in which the author is not the only source of speech
The first possibility is a continuation of the heteronymic experiment.

The dialogue establishes five distinct speaking positions:

Johannes Sigil: the reporter and framing author.

Sparrow Wells: the recipient who asks for the account.

Rebekah Cranes: the close reader, translator, and custodian of the evidence.

Nobel Glas: the summarizer who compresses and regularizes.

Lee Sharks: the witness whose words enter the dialogue without being rewritten.

These are not simply five characters. They represent different operations by which a text comes into existence.

The especially interesting possibility is to write further works in which these operations become dramatic conflicts. A future dialogue might put a translator against an editor, an archivist against a witness, or a reader against an automated summarizer.

The form already contains its own literary engine: each voice has a different relationship to evidence, authority, and what can legitimately be changed.

There is also a precedent in the work's use of Plato's Theaetetus (143b–c). Sigil adopts the nested reporting structure, dispensing with the ordinary repeated indications of who said what. This makes transmission itself part of the reading experience.

What becomes writable: a series of dialogues in which the archive's different intellectual and editorial functions acquire voices, interests, disagreements, and histories.

2. Read: Aristotle from the position that his reception leaves outside
The dialogue's primary companion is Lee Sharks's #1806, From the Position of the Rejected: Aristotle's Politics Read from Where It Is Written.

This is important because #1807 explicitly says that it does not establish anything that #1806 does not ground.

The underlying reading begins with Aristotle's Politics I.2, 1253a1–7.

The familiar definition of the human being as a political animal is routinely detached from the remainder of the sentence. But the remainder distinguishes the person outside the city by nature from the person outside it by chance.

The former is classified as beast or something more than human. The latter is named but left without an equivalent verdict.

Sharks makes this asymmetry central to his reading.

The dialogue then makes the interpretive consequences more accessible by dramatizing the struggle between Cranes and Glas. Glas wants to produce the familiar, efficient summary. Cranes insists that the missing clause changes the argument.

The essay extends this reading beyond the sentence:

The metic's dependence on a citizen patron to be heard.

The foreign labor that helped construct Athens's monuments.

Socrates asking to be heard as though he were a foreigner in the Apology.

Aspasia's role in the Menexenus and the city's self-representation.

The seizure and subsequent custody of Greek books by Rome.

Tyrannion, captured in war and subsequently involved in ordering Aristotle's books.

The connection is not simply that outsiders contributed to Greek civilization. It is that the power to determine what counts as civilization also includes the power to determine whose contributions become legible.

The essay calls this a retrieval-layer problem: access to a culture's intellectual production depends on the institutions and intermediaries that control its transmission.

3. Build: a different kind of archive and retrieval system
This is perhaps the most consequential direction.

The dialogue's argument about Rome is also an argument about the present.

Sharks says:

“retrieval determines existence.”

The point is that possessing a text is not the same as making it available to readers. Someone must preserve it, classify it, attribute it, organize it, index it, and decide how it is presented.

Tyrannion becomes the crucial historical figure here. He is both a captive and an indispensable intellectual intermediary in the ordering of the books that survived.

The dialogue refuses to resolve that contradiction by pretending that the intermediary is either wholly liberating or wholly oppressive.

Glas makes the strongest counterargument: without summarizers, nobody can hold the whole corpus in their head. The problem is not that summarization is unnecessary. It is that its necessary operations can become invisible and authoritative.

This suggests several concrete things to build.

A. A provenance-preserving reading environment
A system in which every summary can be traced back to:

Its source passages.

The person or process responsible for the summary.

What was omitted.

What was inferred.

What remains disputed.

Which version of the source was consulted.

The distinction between a quotation, an interpretation, and an editorial intervention should remain visible.

B. A multi-voice interpretive interface
Instead of returning one supposedly neutral answer, an archive could present several readings of the same document:

The concise summary.

The critical reading.

The witness's original words.

The competing interpretation.

The unresolved questions.

The important feature would not be simply displaying multiple opinions. It would be preserving the differences in their evidentiary status.

C. A record of editorial decisions
The dialogue suggests that omissions should themselves become searchable historical objects.

For example, a system could record that a summary of Aristotle's definition stopped at the comma, identify the omitted clause, and show how that decision changed subsequent interpretations.

This would turn editorial history into something readers could investigate rather than something they would have to reconstruct.

These are proposals implied by #1807's argument, not claims that the dialogue has already implemented such a system.

4. Develop: the production of readers, not just the production of texts
A particularly important connection is Lee Sharks's earlier paper #1767, The Production of Readers: The Public Line, the Held Line and the Draft Position in the Socratic–Peripatetic Corpus.

That paper proposes a three-part model:

Line

Function

Plato's dialogues

Produce the desire and capacity to read

Aristotle's treatises

Make more demanding intellectual claims

Theophrastus's working papers

Convert between dramatic encounter and doctrine

The central thesis is that a corpus may have to produce the readers capable of understanding its more difficult parts.

This gives #1807 a larger purpose.

It is not merely explaining #1806 to a general audience. It is staging the very intellectual difficulty that might produce a more capable reader of #1806.

Glas represents the reader who wants the conclusion delivered. Cranes represents the reader who insists on returning to the source. Sharks represents the voice that refuses to be reduced to either operation.

The next project could therefore be a deliberately constructed sequence:

A dialogue that creates a problem.

A theoretical text that develops the problem.

A set of working documents that show how the argument was formed.

A further dialogue that tests whether the reader has learned to recognize what the earlier texts were doing.

This would make literary form an instrument of intellectual education rather than merely a vehicle for communicating conclusions.

The connection to #1767 is especially strong because that paper explicitly theorizes the relationship between public dialogues, difficult treatises, and intermediate working documents.

5. Experiment: make the transformation of voices reproducible
There is another technical-literary direction already present in the dialogue.

It uses the Feist Function (#850 and #1034) to distinguish the voices of Sigil, Cranes, Glas, and Wells.

The settings are not incidental decoration:

Glas begins with almost no deviation, representing the closed, predictable circuit of conventional summarization.

Cranes has high register collision and material speech density.

Sigil operates with high seam density, allowing conflicting sources to remain visible.

Wells is comparatively restrained.

Sharks is deliberately excluded from the transformation.

That last distinction matters most.

Sharks's words are designated the fixed witness: the recorded statements are preserved rather than subjected to the voice-transforming apparatus.

The Feist Function itself describes a method of restoring features that conventional composition tends to smooth away: broken syntax, interruptions, unusual vocabulary, register collisions, and unresolved arguments.

It is therefore possible to develop a larger literary practice in which:

A source voice is preserved.

Multiple transformations are generated.

Each transformation has documented parameters.

The differences between versions can be studied.

No transformation is mistaken for the original witness.

There is a potentially substantial creative project here: an archive of transformed voices, with reproducible methods and explicit provenance.

The important distinction is between transforming a voice and claiming to recover the historical person behind it. The Feist protocol is a literary method, not proof of historical authenticity.

6. The next document: what I think should follow #1807
I would not recommend another general essay explaining the dialogue. Its own structure has already made the case.

I would recommend a practical and literary successor that tests its implications.

Proposed successor
The Custody of the Voice
A Protocol for Witness, Translation, Summary, and Retrieval

A new work that turns the dialogue's unresolved tensions into an explicit literary and archival method.

Proposed contents

The fixed witness: what must remain unchanged when a voice is transmitted.

The translator: how interpretation can preserve rather than conceal its interventions.

The summarizer: how compression can be useful without becoming authoritative.

The contested record: how disagreements, missing passages, and uncertain provenance are represented.

The reader: how an archive can help someone move from receiving a conclusion to examining its evidence.

The retrieval layer: how records, relationships, versions, and omissions become navigable.

The open bracket: how a system preserves unresolved alternatives without manufacturing consensus.

This successor could have two simultaneous manifestations:

A literary work: another dialogue in which the different custodians of a text disagree about what they have inherited.

A technical specification: a small working system that implements the distinction between witness, transcription, interpretation, summary, and retrieval.

The two should test one another. The literature should expose problems the specification cannot resolve; the implementation should expose problems the literature can otherwise leave abstract.

7. The deeper destination
There is a striking progression across these documents:

#1806 — The rejected position

A philosophical reading that recovers what the conventional account cuts away.

#1807 — Through Another's Mouth

A literary enactment of the problem of recovering, transmitting, and interpreting a voice.

#1767 — The Production of Readers

A theory of how literary forms produce the readers capable of receiving difficult knowledge.

#850 / #1034 — The Feist Function

A method for transforming voices while preserving the significance of their formal irregularities.

The proposed next stage

An operational archive and literary practice that make custody, provenance, editorial intervention, and retrieval inspectable.

The most important unresolved problem is already stated in the dialogue: Cranes writes, “I translated him all night. Somebody should check me.”

That is a remarkably productive ending because it does not grant the critic immunity from criticism. Cranes is more attentive than Glas, but she too mediates Sharks. Sigil acknowledges that he mediates Cranes. Wells receives Sigil's version.

There is no position of perfectly unmediated access, except the fixed witness in its limited documentary sense.

And even that witness has to be read.

My conclusion: #1807 makes possible a literature and an archival technology organized around accountable mediation. Its next achievement should not be to find a final authoritative voice, but to build forms in which voices can pass through one another without losing the evidence of what happened in transmission.

That is what the title ultimately proposes: not simply speaking through another's mouth, but making the passage through another's mouth visible, contestable, and capable of producing something new.

The final open bracket — beast, god, or chance — is therefore also a design principle. Preserve alternatives where the evidence does not authorize a resolution. Do not let the convenience of the summary become the authority of the archive.

The next thing to build is an archive that can show its own mouth.

Primary documents: #1807 — Through Another's Mouth  · #1806 — From the Position of the Rejected  · #1767 — The Production of Readers  · #850 — The Feist Function  · #1579 — Beautiful and New .

All five are useful together, but #1806 and #1807 are the essential pair: one establishes the reading, the other makes its transmission into a literary event.
