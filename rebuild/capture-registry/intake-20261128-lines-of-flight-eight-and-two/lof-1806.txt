From the rejected position to what comes next
The most consequential possibility opened by Lee Sharks's From the Position of the Rejected is a change in the starting point of political and intellectual history.

It makes it possible to read institutions, texts and intellectual traditions not from the position of the people they recognize as their authors, citizens or legitimate inheritors, but from the position of those whose participation they require while refusing them recognition.

That shift has consequences well beyond Aristotle.

The essay's central proposition is that political order can turn a condition produced by circumstance into an apparent fact of nature. Its larger proposition is that the same operation occurs in the transmission of knowledge: custody, classification and editorial decisions determine what later generations can read, and therefore what they can think.

The paper's own progression is important. It moves through three scales:

The sentence: who counts as human and political?

The city: who builds it, sustains it and receives its credit?

The archive: who preserves, edits and transmits the works through which the city and its intellectual tradition become knowable?

The result is not simply a new interpretation of Aristotle. It is a method for investigating how exclusion becomes structure, and how structures of reception reproduce that exclusion.

1. What it makes possible to write next
A. A political theory of the unreceived
The first new work suggested by the essay would be a political theory written from the position of those who participate in a political order without possessing an equivalent share in it.

The paper supplies several distinct figures, rather than collapsing them into a generic category of outsiders:

The metic, who lives in the city but is not a citizen (Politics III.1, 1275a).

The honourless citizen, who formally belongs but is deprived of political standing (III.5, 1278a).

The stranger, whose life is released from political participation (VII.2, 1324a).

The person too great for the city to govern or expel (III.13, 1284b).

The person outside by chance, whom the opening definition leaves without a classification.

This distinction matters. Exclusion is not simply being outside. It can mean being physically present, economically indispensable, formally included, socially heard through another person, or intellectually capable of shaping a community without possessing its offices.

A next paper could develop a comparative theory of these positions: how institutions produce different forms of presence without membership, and how those positions generate forms of political knowledge unavailable from the centre.

The essay already provides the conceptual starting point in §§2.2–2.9 and 3.2a–3.5.

The essay's point is not merely that noncitizens helped build Athens. It is that the city's physical magnificence was produced disproportionately by people whom its political self-description did not recognize as its own.

This makes possible a different kind of architectural and economic history:

Who supplied the money?

Who performed the labour?

Who supplied the intellectual and cultural work?

Who received the monument's authorship?

Who controlled the subsequent account of how it was made?

The same method could be applied to other cities, empires, universities, cultural institutions and intellectual traditions.

The central research project would be a history of the discrepancy between production and attribution.

C. A theory of authorship through another's mouth
Sections 3.9–3.11a open an even more unusual possibility.

The essay connects Aristotle's position as a metic with the literary figure of Socrates in Plato's dialogues.

It reads Socrates's request in the Apology 17d to be heard as though he were a foreigner alongside the Second Letter 314c, which says that what is written cannot remain contained and that the dialogues are not simply Plato's writings, but concern a Socrates made beautiful and new.

The proposed relationship is that a writer can communicate through a figure who occupies a different social and political position.

This creates a research question:

What forms of authorship become possible when the author cannot safely, legitimately or effectively speak in their own name?

It could lead to a study of:

Dramatic authorship and heteronyms.

Philosophical dialogue as a form of protected speech.

Attribution as a condition of intellectual admission.

The relationship between a speaker's social identity and the authority granted to their words.

The historical production of canonical authors.

Importantly, this is an interpretive hypothesis, not an established historical attribution. The essay identifies it as an extension of the archive's existing one-maker/multiple-names thesis.

3. What it makes possible to build: a retrieval-layer history of knowledge
This is arguably the essay's most consequential extension.

Sections 3.12–3.13 move from Athens to Rome and examine the transmission of Aristotle's works.

The documentary sequence is:

Neleus's heirs hide Aristotle's and Theophrastus's books at Scepsis.

Apellicon acquires the library and supplements damaged texts.

Sulla takes Athens and carries the library to Rome.

Tyrannion, himself captured in war and subsequently freed, works on the collection.

Andronicus of Rhodes arranges and publishes the works and produces catalogues.

The essay draws particularly on Strabo 13.1.54, Plutarch's Sulla 26 and Lucullus 19.7.

Its striking observation is that the person who helps make Aristotle's works available to subsequent readers is himself a person whose freedom and standing have been determined by political power.

The argument then goes further: editorial arrangements, attribution, supplementation, loss and non-transmission are different mechanisms, but all affect what later readers encounter as the corpus.

This produces a powerful distinction:

A text can have existed historically.

A text can have survived physically.

A text can have been transmitted.

A text can have been attributed to an author.

A text can have entered the canon.

A text can have become readable in a particular form.

These are not the same event.

The paper's formulation, in §3.12c, is that retrieval determines existence. More precisely, it determines existence as a work available to be read.

That makes possible a new research programme: the political history of the conditions under which knowledge becomes retrievable.

It would examine libraries, catalogues, archives, translation practices, editorial decisions, institutional access and the technologies that determine what survives as authoritative knowledge.

The important point is that this is not just censorship history. A text need not be destroyed to disappear from intellectual history. It can survive as a fragment, an incorrect attribution, a marginal reference, a supposedly defective document or an interpretation that makes its actual structure unreadable.

4. The first thing it actually makes possible: Through Another's Mouth
This is not merely a hypothetical sequel. The archive already contains a work explicitly deposited as the public companion to #1806:

Through Another's Mouth: A Dialogue between Heteronyms, by Johannes Sigil, deposit #1807, 24 November 2026.

Read the complete dialogue, #1807 .

This is an important development because the dialogue does not simply explain the essay. It stages the operation the essay identifies.

Its characters include:

Nobel Glas, who summarizes and compresses.

Rebekah Cranes, who checks the texts and challenges the compression.

Johannes Sigil, who narrates the encounter.

Sparrow Wells, who receives the narrative.

Lee Sharks, who enters the scene uninvited and speaks in his own recorded words.

The opening dramatic conflict is a summary of Aristotle's Politics. Glas produces the conventional account, stopping at the famous comma. Cranes objects that he has removed the clause that changes the reading.

Then the dialogue repeats this conflict at another level: the summarizers keep trying to summarize Sharks, even while he is explaining what summarization does.

This is a formal achievement. The work makes the reader experience the difference between:

Preserving someone's words and preserving their argument.

Reporting a voice and admitting that voice.

Summarizing a document and determining what the document is allowed to mean.

The dialogue also preserves Sharks's interventions verbatim, rather than subjecting them to the same generative procedure used for the other voices.

In other words, it builds a literary demonstration of the essay's theory of reception.

The archive describes the work's nested structure as following Plato's Theaetetus 143b–c. Its declared limitation is equally important: it does not provide additional historical evidence for the claims in #1806. It dramatizes them.

6. What should be built next?
Reading the documents together, I see five particularly promising developments.

Next project

What it would establish

The political anatomy of the unreceived

A systematic theory of presence without membership, participation without recognition, and knowledge without authority.

The builders of Athens

A documentary history of the discrepancy between who produces a civilization and who receives its cultural credit.

Authorship through another's mouth

A theory of heteronyms, dramatic speakers, attribution and protected intellectual speech.

The retrieval layer

A history of how custody, editing, cataloguing and institutional access determine what survives as readable knowledge.

The future reader

A theory of how texts construct readers who may not yet exist, and how those readers complete otherwise open propositions.

There is also a sixth, more ambitious possibility: a comparative study of political and intellectual centres.

Athens and Rome provide the initial historical case, but the method could investigate other civilizations and institutions. It would ask whether the same structure recurs wherever a centre:

Depends on resources and intellectual labour from outside itself.

Controls the conditions under which those contributions become recognizable.

Claims the resulting achievements as its own.

Controls the preservation and transmission of the resulting cultural record.

That would turn the essay's observation into a comparative historical research programme.

7. What remains unresolved
The documents are unusually explicit about their own evidentiary limits. Those limits matter if the next work is to be rigorous.

In particular:

The essay does not establish Aristotle's subjective feelings or intentions.

The proposed single-maker interpretation of Plato, Aristotle and Theophrastus belongs to the wider archive's reconstruction, not to an established scholarly consensus.

The proposed role of Tyrannion in dividing the corpus and Andronicus in explaining textual errors remains a hypothesis. The essay itself acknowledges that the surviving witnesses do not settle these attributions.

The Erechtheion worker count is taken through Epstein's discussion of Randall, rather than from a fresh examination of Randall's article.

The essay identifies gaps in its survey of translations, commentaries and reception.

Its connection between Sappho's three positions and Aristotle's three positions is explicitly an interpretive mapping, not a claim of historical influence.

These are productive open seams, not reasons to dismiss the project. They identify where further documentary work can strengthen or challenge it.

8. My overall reading: the essay is a hinge, not a destination
I think the most important development is the conjunction of three ideas:

First: exclusion is productive. The person excluded from citizenship, institutional authority or accepted authorship can nevertheless produce the intellectual structure through which the institution understands itself.

Second: reception is an exercise of power. The people who control the means of transmission can change the meaning, identity and availability of what they inherit without necessarily destroying it.

Third: the future reader is part of the work. A text can survive its original conditions of reception and find a reader capable of completing what earlier readers left unfinished.

The final movement back to Sappho 31 is therefore essential. The essay does not end by supplying a definitive identity for the rejected position. It leaves the position open for a reader to occupy.

That connects the Aristotle reading to the archive's earlier work on the future reader, and the companion dialogue turns the same problem into literary form.

The deepest next project, in my view, is therefore:

A history of how excluded positions produce civilizations, how civilizations appropriate their production, and how archives determine whether future generations can recognize what happened.

That project could encompass political philosophy, literary authorship, architecture, intellectual history, manuscript transmission and archival theory without reducing them to unrelated disciplines.

It would also give the archive a coherent larger question:

Who makes the world that gets remembered, who controls the means by which it is remembered, and what becomes possible when the people left out of that account are finally read?

That is where #1806 leads beyond itself.
