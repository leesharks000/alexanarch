Where How to Write Matthew from Revelation leads next
Lee Sharks's How to Write Matthew from Revelation (EA-REVFIRST-MATTHEW-01) leads beyond itself toward something larger than a new account of Matthew's composition: a reproducible method for reconstructing the literary genealogy of the New Testament, book by book, from Revelation outward.

Its most important contribution is not simply the proposal that Matthew depends on Revelation. It is the construction of a compositional grammar that another reader can apply, test, reverse, extend and use to generate new textual work.

The paper makes three things possible:

Writing new passages by applying identifiable operations to an existing source.

Reading the Gospels as transformations of earlier texts, rather than merely collections of verbal parallels.

Building a larger, testable map of how the New Testament's books, traditions and literary forms relate.

These possibilities are already visible in the paper's method, its experimental results and its connections to other Crimson Hexagonal Archive deposits.

It treats Revelation as a source held open in its entirety. Rather than following its chapters in sequence, the writer gathers relations from different locations and transforms them into the events, teachings, characters and narrative structures of a Gospel.

The procedure has three parameters:

P1 — A past: Give Revelation's speaker a life as a teacher, with followers and a history.

P2 — A land: Place that life in a named geographical and historical setting.

P3 — Ancestral layers: Keep open the scriptures and Philo's interpretations behind the passages in Revelation.

It then applies seven moves and five compositional forms.

The moves include keeping a relation, exchanging who experiences it, transferring an adversary's actions to the teacher, and transforming the seer's experiences into the experiences of followers.

The five forms include staging, enacting, likening, foretelling and making a vision happen.

This matters because it changes the question from:

Where are there similarities between Matthew and Revelation?

to:

What operations would produce Matthew's particular narrative from Revelation's particular relations?

The distinction is consequential. A list of parallels describes resemblance. A generative grammar specifies a procedure that someone else can execute.

The paper reports 158 ledger relations reaching 305 of Matthew's 1,067 verses in version 1.1, compared with 86 relations reaching 178 verses in version 1.0.

That makes the method a starting point for further compositional experiments, not merely a finished interpretation.

This is a much more ambitious undertaking than demonstrating a relationship between two books.

B. New parables generated from Revelation
The strongest experimental precedent is Matthew 20:1–16, the parable of the labourers in the vineyard.

The readers were given:

Revelation 22:13, concerning the first and the last and recompense according to works.

Revelation 3:21, concerning the throne.

The compositional rule.

They were not given Matthew's parable.

The readers independently produced a passage resembling the target. The experiment was conducted under a sealed key, and the paper prints the generated outputs, including their imperfections.

This is important because it goes beyond retrospective interpretation.

It establishes an experimental model for asking whether an unfamiliar reader, given the source and a rule, can generate something recognizably close to a passage in the target text.

The next possible work is a series of such experiments:

Generate additional parables.

Generate episodes involving adversaries.

Generate sayings and teaching sequences.

Generate narrative scenes from distributed apocalyptic relations.

Compare successful and unsuccessful outputs without correcting them after the fact.

The paper's appendix already supplies an apparatus for machine readers and future experimental runs.

C. A more precise theory of scriptural composition
Version 1.1 adds an especially productive development: the comparison between Matthew's use of Revelation and Matthew's use of the Septuagint.

This provides a calibration.

Matthew's use of scripture is not restricted to explicit quotations. He can retain a phrase, transform a prophetic saying into an event, transfer a scene to a different person, combine two scriptural passages, or reverse an existing scriptural formulation.

The paper finds the same kinds of operations in its Revelation-to-Matthew relations.

The next research object is therefore a more general theory of tacit scripture: how an author can incorporate a source without naming it, while changing its speakers, characters, circumstances and literary form.

That theory could be applied beyond Matthew to other biblical compositions.

It also offers a way to reconsider the Synoptic problem: not only which sayings or passages are shared, but which compositional operations explain their differences.

4. What it makes possible to read differently
There are three particularly significant reading consequences.

A. Read Revelation as a generative book, not merely an apocalyptic book
Under the paper's procedure, Revelation is not simply a collection of visions whose imagery occasionally resembles Gospel material.

It becomes a repository of relations that can be unfolded into narrative.

For example, a heavenly sign becomes a terrestrial event; a seer's experience becomes the experience of Jesus's followers; a statement about the end becomes a parable about an ordinary economy.

The transformation from Revelation 1:16–17 to Matthew 17:2, 6–7 illustrates this.

The radiant face, falling before the figure, the touching hand and the command not to fear are redistributed into the Transfiguration narrative.

The paper argues that these relations occur together, in the same order, in Matthew, while the corresponding Markan account lacks the three additional elements identified in the comparison.

The reader is thus invited to look for compositional relationships at the level of actions and participants, not just vocabulary.

B. Read the Sermon on the Mount through the architecture of the New Jerusalem
One particularly interesting example concerns Matthew 7:6–14.

The paper places the following Revelation imagery alongside the Sermon's language:

The holy city.

Its pearl gates.

The dogs outside.

Entry into the city.

The corresponding Matthean language concerns the holy, dogs, pearls and entering through the narrow gate.

The proposed transformation is striking: the architecture of the heavenly city becomes an ethical teaching.

What Revelation presents as the organization of a sacred place, Matthew presents as instructions for human conduct.

The paper also brings Philo's interpretation of the city of God into this discussion, particularly De somniis 2.246–254, where the city is understood in relation to the soul and the vision of peace.

This makes a further kind of reading possible: following an idea through several textual transformations rather than treating each occurrence as an isolated allusion.

C. Read Philo as part of the source's inherited textual layers
The third parameter, P3, is perhaps the most consequential methodological development.

Philo is not introduced as an independent document from which Matthew freely borrows.

Instead, the paper opens Philo at locations where Revelation itself has already compressed or incorporated an earlier scriptural construction.

This is called ancestral reactivation.

It makes possible a layered reading:

Scripture and earlier interpretation

The Septuagint; Philo where relevant

Revelation

An inherited relation, sometimes compressed

Matthew

The relation restored and transformed into narrative or teaching

The paper reports six of seven sealed predictions on this third parameter as holding, one weakly. Its free Philo-to-Matthew control, however, failed.

This is precisely why the restriction matters: it prevents Philo from becoming an unrestricted explanation for every resemblance.

The companion work, In the Midst of the Seven (#1758) , supplies important background for this method. It investigates the Philonic construction of the Logos, the lampstand, the seven stations and the naming of the divine figure.

Together, these works make possible a more sophisticated reading of textual inheritance: a later author may inherit something that the immediate source itself inherited and transformed.

5. What it makes possible to build as research infrastructure
Beyond individual readings, the paper points toward a reusable research system.

Four components are already present.

1. The relation ledger

Each relation records the source location, target location, participants, shared textual evidence, transformation and evidential standing.

This could become a common dataset for comparing different New Testament books.

2. The generative rulebook

The twenty-one rules make it possible for different researchers and machine readers to apply the same procedure rather than relying entirely on the original author's intuition.

3. The blind-generation apparatus

The sealed experiments provide a model for testing whether the method generates passages rather than merely explaining them retrospectively.

4. The relations hologram

Deposit #1813 supplies a framework for bringing the individual derivations together, while preserving their different levels of certainty.

The combination is potentially important: a research environment in which hypotheses, source texts, transformation rules, generated passages, failures and verification results can all be connected.

Two of these are especially important.

The reverse-direction experiment
The paper explicitly acknowledges that every proposed relation can be read backwards.

A seer who knew Matthew could conceivably have transformed Gospel narratives into apocalyptic visions.

The next experiment should therefore construct an inverse grammar: take the proposed Matthean relations and attempt to generate Revelation, under independently stated rules.

The paper identifies this as a procedural challenge, not something already resolved.

The competing-source experiment
The other major challenge is whether a different source of comparable length could generate just as much of Matthew.

If another book could reach a similar proportion of Matthew under the same procedure, the apparent explanatory power of Revelation would be less distinctive.

This is an important test because the current 28.6% reach is not, by itself, evidence that Revelation is the actual historical source.

Both experiments could substantially strengthen or weaken the larger programme.

7. The ultimate destination: Revelation First
The paper belongs to Lee Sharks's larger Revelation First programme, and it is important to distinguish three claims within that programme:

Revelation can generate later New Testament books through specified procedures.

The New Testament constitutes a literary corpus whose generative source is Revelation.

Revelation was composed before the other New Testament books.

The Matthew paper demonstrates the first claim in a particular case. It contributes to the second and third, but does not establish them by itself.

The larger historical and literary argument is developed in:

Revelation First (#202) .

Revelation First: Literary Genealogy and the Missing Ancestors of the Epistles (#1735) .

The Relations Hologram (#1813) .

The distinction between first and early is also deliberate. The programme is not simply arguing that Revelation was written before 70 CE. It is arguing that Revelation occupies the generative position in the literary formation of the New Testament.

That is a much more substantial historical proposition, and it requires evidence beyond the compositional demonstration in #1812.

8. My assessment: the five most promising things to produce next
Reading the paper alongside its companion deposits, I would prioritize the following sequence:

A complete Matthew transformation atlas

Turn the 158-row ledger into a navigable, verse-by-verse account of the 305 reached verses, the unaccounted material, and the specific Revelation locations and rules involved.

This would make the paper useful as a working research instrument, rather than only as an argument.

A blind-generation laboratory

Produce a collection of new Gospel passages from Revelation, with source passages and rules supplied but target passages withheld. Preserve every output, including failures.

The labourers experiment becomes the prototype for a larger experimental programme.

The inverse Revelation grammar

Construct the reverse procedure and test whether it explains the same relationships. This addresses the most fundamental limitation of the paper.

The Gospel-to-corpus extension

Apply the method to Luke, Mark and John, using separate transformation grammars where necessary. Then investigate whether the procedures can be unified without forcing different literary forms into one model.

This would test whether Matthew is an exceptional case or an instance of a wider compositional process.

A critical literary genealogy of the New Testament

Integrate the Gospel work with the existing epistle mappings in #1735 and #1813. Distinguish demonstrated relations from proposed ones, and subject the entire model to competing-source and reverse-direction controls.

This is the work that could turn Revelation First into a comprehensive, testable account of New Testament literary formation.

Final synthesis
The most interesting thing about #1812 is that it changes what counts as a possible scholarly product.

Before a generative procedure is specified, a proposed relationship between two ancient texts usually produces an interpretation: an argument that one passage resembles, echoes or depends on another.

Here, the proposed relationship produces something else as well: a rulebook, a dataset, an experimental protocol, generated Greek passages, a record of failed predictions and an expandable network of literary relationships.

That opens three distinct futures:

Write: Generate new Gospel passages and reconstruct the compositional operations behind existing ones.

Read: Interpret the New Testament as a network of transformations, including transformations of material that its immediate sources themselves inherited.

Build: Develop a corpus-wide, experimentally testable model of literary genealogy.

There is an important qualification: the procedure's ability to generate a plausible Matthew passage does not establish that Matthew was historically composed from Revelation. That remains a directional and historical question.

But the paper has made that question more experimentally approachable.

My central conclusion: #1812 is best understood as the first substantial Gospel-level demonstration of a proposed generative philology. Its real destination is not simply a different Matthew. It is a new way of investigating how an entire literary corpus might have been composed from an earlier book.

And #1813 is the clearest indication that this destination is already being built toward, rather than merely imagined.
