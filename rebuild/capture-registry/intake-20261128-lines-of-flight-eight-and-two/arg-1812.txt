The argument of Lee Sharks's How to Write Matthew from Revelation
The central argument of Lee Sharks's paper, “How to Write Matthew from Revelation: The Generative Grammar of the First Gospel” (EA-REVFIRST-MATTHEW-01, Alexanarch deposit #1812, AXN:0D8B), is that the Gospel of Matthew can be generated from the Apocalypse of John (Revelation) through a relatively small, identifiable set of compositional operations.

This is more ambitious than arguing that Matthew and Revelation share imagery, vocabulary, or theological traditions. Sharks proposes a generative grammar: a reproducible procedure by which the visionary, prophetic material of Revelation can be transformed into the narrative, biographical, and instructional form of Matthew.

The paper's most important qualification is that it distinguishes demonstrating this generative possibility from proving the historical direction of dependence. It argues that Revelation can generate Matthew, but acknowledges that the demonstration alone cannot establish that Matthew was historically written from Revelation.

The following account draws on the paper's own text, including its methodological statements, Greek comparisons, quantitative ledger, experimental test, and discussion of failed predictions.

1. The fundamental question: what would it take to write a Gospel from an apocalypse?
Sharks begins by stating the conventional account of Matthew fairly fully:

Matthew used Mark as a narrative source.

Matthew's additional sayings material is commonly explained through a hypothetical sayings collection, often called Q.

Matthew also incorporates distinctive material of its own, shaped by scripture and oral or written tradition.

Revelation is understood as a separate prophetic work.

Similarities between Matthew and Revelation are ordinarily attributed to common Christian traditions, scriptural language, or sayings of Jesus.

The paper does not begin by simply declaring this account wrong.

Instead, section 0.2 poses a constructive question:

Suppose a writer possesses the whole of Revelation and wants to produce a Gospel. What must that writer do?

The question changes the nature of the Synoptic problem. Rather than beginning with a list of verbal parallels, Sharks asks whether the relations within Revelation can be systematically transformed into the relations, episodes, sayings, and structures found in Matthew.

The author distinguishes three levels of demonstration:

Relational correspondence: a relation in Revelation reappears in Matthew, with an identifiable change.

Operational regularity: the changes belong to a relatively small set of recurring compositional operations.

Generative production: a reader given a source passage and an operation can independently produce a corresponding Matthean passage.

This distinction is important. The paper offers evidence of all three, but they do not have equal evidentiary strength.

2. The principal compositional operation: give the speaker a past
The first major argument is that Revelation's central speaker can be transformed into the protagonist of a Gospel.

In Revelation, Jesus speaks as a heavenly figure. His speech is predominantly first-person, visionary, and oriented toward the present and the coming end.

In Matthew, Jesus is a historical teacher who:

Was born.

Lived in a particular land.

Gathered followers.

Taught in public.

Encountered opponents.

Was killed.

Was raised.

Sharks calls the operation of giving Revelation's speaker a historical past P1.

The transformation allows Revelation's first-person speech to become the speech of Matthew's earthly teacher.

Example: the thief and the unknown hour
Sharks compares:

Revelation 3:3: Christ warns that he will come like a thief, at an unknown hour.

Matthew 24:43: Jesus describes a householder who would have stayed awake had he known when the thief was coming.

The relation is retained, but the grammatical and narrative arrangement changes.

The heavenly speaker's first-person declaration becomes an illustrative teaching addressed to an audience.

Example: the confession
Revelation 3:5: Christ promises to confess the believer's name before his Father.

Matthew 10:32: Jesus says that he will confess the believer before his Father.

Here the correspondence is particularly direct: the speaker's promise becomes a saying of the earthly teacher.

The larger point: Matthew's sayings need not all originate in a hypothetical sayings document. Some can be understood as Revelation's own speech, transferred into the mouth of a teacher who has acquired a narrated life.

3. Give the life a beginning: Revelation 12 becomes Matthew's infancy
The second operation, P2, gives the speaker a narrated life in a named geographical setting.

Sharks argues that Revelation already contains the elements from which a birth narrative can be constructed, particularly in Revelation 12:

A woman about to give birth.

A child destined to rule the nations.

A dragon waiting to destroy the child.

A flight into the wilderness.

A celestial sign involving stars.

The identification of the dragon with Herod is particularly important. Revelation supplies the structure of an endangered royal child; Matthew supplies the historical and geographical realization.

Sharks also examines the Magi, the eastern setting, the star, gold and frankincense.

An important qualification appears in section II.7: some of this material is more directly explained by the Septuagint than by Revelation. Isaiah 60, for example, already supplies kings coming with gold and frankincense. Numbers 24 supplies the rising star.

The argument is therefore not that Revelation replaces scripture. Rather, Revelation and scripture can work together at the same points in Matthew's composition.

This leads to one of the paper's central methodological concepts: the source must be held open. A writer can draw on Revelation without ignoring the older texts from which Revelation itself was composed.

4. Give the life a land, teaching, and economy
Sharks next argues that Revelation's visionary and symbolic material can be converted into the circumstances of a teacher's earthly existence.

The operation is not simply to turn every symbol into a literal object. It is to transform the function of a relationship.

For example:

An assembly's experience can become the experience of Jesus's followers.

A divine promise can become an instruction or a parable.

A heavenly encounter can become an earthly encounter.

A symbolic economic transaction can become a teaching about wages, justice, and reward.

This is how the author approaches the material that eventually becomes Matthew's teaching discourses.

The Sermon on the Mount and the transformation of Revelation's world
One particularly important example concerns Matthew 5:34–35.

Revelation 10:5–6 depicts an angel raising his hand toward heaven and swearing by the eternal Creator of heaven and earth.

Matthew 5:34–35 prohibits swearing by heaven and earth.

Sharks places this alongside Isaiah 66:1, which describes heaven as God's throne and earth as his footstool.

The argument is that Matthew's teaching is not simply repeating Revelation. It is transforming the action of Revelation into a rule for the community.

The angel's act of swearing becomes something Jesus prohibits.

This is a particularly interesting example because the transformation is almost the reverse of straightforward quotation: Matthew retains the conceptual and scriptural material while changing its normative force.

The paper acknowledges that reversals are relatively uncommon in its ledger. They must be identified rather than assumed.

5. Turn Revelation's adversaries into Jesus's adversaries
Another central operation concerns the opposition Jesus faces.

Revelation contains:

False claimants to authority.

Assemblies that receive rebukes.

People who claim an identity they do not possess.

A city condemned for its corruption.

Adversarial figures who stand against the divine speaker.

Matthew turns these into the opponents of Jesus's ministry, particularly the scribes and Pharisees.

Sharks's principal example is Matthew 23.

Revelation repeatedly uses the construction of people who say they are something but are not.

Matthew 23:3 similarly distinguishes between what the religious authorities say and what they actually do.

Revelation 3:1 describes someone who has a reputation for being alive but is dead. Matthew 23:27–28 describes the outward appearance of righteousness concealing inward corruption.

The transformation is significant:

An apocalyptic judgment of an assembly becomes an earthly teacher's denunciation of religious authorities.

This also helps explain why Matthew's polemical material might be generated from Revelation rather than necessarily requiring a separate lost collection of sayings.

6. Turn the divine name into a story
One of the paper's more distinctive arguments concerns the relationship between names, identity, and narrative.

Revelation contains a figure whose identity is expressed through names that are sometimes concealed, revealed, or written.

Sharks argues that this material can be transformed into the naming and identification of Jesus in Matthew.

The clearest example concerns Matthew 1:21:

You shall call his name Jesus, for he will save his people.

The paper brings this into relation with Revelation's treatment of the unknown name, the name written on the stone, and the rider whose name is the Logos of God.

It also brings in Philo's interpretation of the name Joshua/Jesus as salvation of the Lord.

This illustrates the third parameter of the generative procedure: at each place in Revelation, the writer must also consider the older scriptural and philosophical material that Revelation has absorbed.

The author calls the restoration of material compressed out of Revelation's antecedents ancestral reactivation.

In other words, a Gospel writer could reopen the earlier material that Revelation has condensed and use it to construct a more elaborate narrative.

7. Make the end of the world into a parable
This is arguably the paper's most important demonstration of its generative method.

Revelation is an apocalyptic book. Its material concerns judgment, reward, punishment, the end of history, and the final ordering of the world.

Matthew contains parables that communicate these same concerns through everyday situations.

Sharks argues that the transition from apocalypse to parable can be described as a recurring operation rather than an isolated literary resemblance.

The labourers in the vineyard: Matthew 20:1–16
This is the paper's experimental centerpiece.

The author takes two passages from Revelation:

Revelation 22:13: Christ is the Alpha and Omega, the beginning and the end.

Revelation 3:21: the promise of sharing in the throne.

The first passage also contains the promise of reward according to each person's work.

Sharks proposes that the combination of these materials can generate the structure of the labourers in the vineyard.

The transformation proceeds approximately as follows:

The divine reckoning becomes a payment at the end of the working day.

The promise of reward becomes the payment of wages.

The equality of beginning and end becomes an ordering in which the last workers are paid first.

Unequal amounts of work produce a conflict over equal payment.

The complaint becomes the parable's central dramatic tension.

The original language of first and last becomes the concluding reversal: the last shall be first and the first last.

The author did not merely identify these parallels retrospectively. A set of instructions was prepared for blind readers, who were given the two Revelation passages and the compositional rule.

Six runs using the complete pack scored thirteen out of thirteen against a previously sealed scoring key.

The control runs are especially relevant:

Material supplied

Result

Both Revelation passages and the rule

Full parable structure produced

Revelation 22:13 alone

Identity and equality, but not the complete conflict

Revelation 22:12 alone

Proportional payment, not the parable's reversal

Rule without the forced consequences

Ordinary proportional wages

The result is interesting because it suggests that neither source passage independently generates the story. Their combination does.

However, Sharks is careful about what this establishes. The readers knew Matthew, and the instructions were written by someone who knew the target passage. The experiment therefore demonstrates a bounded form of generative sufficiency, not independent proof of historical authorship.

This is one of the strongest sections of the paper because it makes the hypothesis experimentally testable rather than relying exclusively on interpretive parallels.

8. Open the tomb: Revelation's death and resurrection become the Passion narrative
The final major compositional operation concerns the death of Jesus.

Sharks argues that Revelation contains the elements necessary to construct the narrative of death, custody, judgment, and resurrection.

The relevant material includes:

The death of the witnesses in Revelation 11.

Their restoration to life.

The adversary's confinement and release in Revelation 20.

The first vision's command not to fear.

The Lamb's death and victory.

These visionary episodes are transformed into the earthly experiences of Jesus.

An important principle operates here: what happens to an adversarial or representative figure in Revelation can become something that happens to Jesus in Matthew.

Likewise, promises made to the faithful can become events that Jesus experiences or that his followers fail to enact.

The paper also connects the Passion with the failure of Jesus's followers to remain faithful during his arrest.

The promises of fidelity in Revelation's letters are transformed into the disciples' actual trial.

Peter's denial is an especially revealing example.

Revelation 2:13 and 3:8 praise those who have not denied the divine name. Matthew 26 depicts Peter denying Jesus.

The original obligation remains recognizable, but the narrative now stages its failure.

This is a particularly good illustration of how Sharks distinguishes between retaining a relation and retaining its original outcome.

9. The crucial interpretive argument: Matthew handles Revelation as it handles scripture
This is arguably the paper's most consequential argument at the level of literary method.

Sharks does not merely argue that Matthew and Revelation resemble one another. He argues that Matthew's treatment of Revelation follows the same compositional practices that scholars already recognize in Matthew's use of the Septuagint.

The comparison with Matthew 2 is particularly instructive.

Matthew's account of Herod and the endangered child clearly uses the Exodus story of Pharaoh and Moses. Yet Revelation 12 provides an additional structural element: a child threatened by a dragon at the moment of birth.

Sharks argues that the two sources contribute different things.

Exodus provides much of the wording; Revelation supplies an apocalyptic structure that Exodus alone does not contain.

Similarly, Matthew 24:30 combines Daniel's Son of Man coming with the clouds and Zechariah's mourning. Revelation 1:7 already brings these elements together.

This is the basis for the argument that Revelation can function as an intermediate compositional text: it has already combined scriptural materials that Matthew subsequently uses.

The quantitative evidence
The paper reports three particularly important findings:

Matthew's tacit scriptural allusions and Revelation-derived relations overlap substantially: 55% of the verses where Matthew uses scripture without an explicit citation are also reached by the Revelation ledger.

The corresponding figure for Matthew's Gospel overall is 28.6%.

For verses where Matthew explicitly cites scripture, the figure is 22%.

Sharks interprets this as evidence that Revelation is incorporated particularly where Matthew incorporates scripture without naming it.

There is an important limitation: the statistical comparison does not independently establish that Matthew borrowed from Revelation rather than both writers drawing on the same antecedent.

The author acknowledges this explicitly.

10. The generative grammar: nine steps and twenty-one rules
The paper formalizes its method as T_Μ, a procedure consisting of nine steps and twenty-one rules.

Its architecture can be summarized as follows:

The source

Revelation is held whole. The unit of analysis is the relation between participants, actions, and outcomes, not the sequence of verses.

Three parameters

Give the speaker a past.

Place the life in a named geographical and historical setting.

Keep open the scriptural and Philonic material underlying each source passage.

Seven compositional moves

Keep a saying as the teacher's speech.

Exchange the participants in an action.

Reassign a costly action to an adversary.

Stage a visionary relation as an earthly event.

Enact a symbolic relation in narrative.

Turn a vision into a parable.

Restore material compressed in the source's antecedents.

Five forms

The procedure changes the material's literary form: utterance into saying, vision into event, assembly into followers, judgment into passion, and apocalyptic scene into parable.

Five checks

The resulting composition must be checked against its textual relations, Greek wording, source layers, recurring operations, and the possibility of alternative explanations.

The full method is more detailed than this summary, but its fundamental principle is that composition consists of transformations of relations, not simply the transfer of words.

12. The larger historical argument: Revelation First
The paper belongs to a larger intellectual programme called Revelation First.

Its central thesis is that Revelation is not the final literary development of the New Testament but its generative starting point.

Sharks makes a literary-genetic argument.

The question is not simply which texts can be dated earliest. It is which texts have identifiable literary ancestors and which require hypothetical ancestors to explain their existence.

The argument runs as follows:

Revelation's imagery and literary structures can be traced to surviving texts: Ezekiel, Daniel, Zechariah, Exodus, Isaiah, and Philo.

The epistles and Gospels are commonly explained through sources that no longer survive, including hypothetical sayings collections, pre-Pauline traditions, and special Gospel sources.

Revelation may therefore be a more complete and inspectable literary ancestor than the reconstructed documents conventionally placed behind the Gospels.

The Gospels could represent transformations of an existing apocalyptic work rather than an entirely independent development from oral biographies and lost collections.

The author also makes an argument about literary forms.

Apocalyptic vision is an established Jewish literary form. The letter from an apostolic figure to an assembly and the Greek biography of a contemporary religious teacher are, in his account, innovations that need their own explanations.

He proposes a developmental sequence in which the visionary figure acquires progressively more capacities:

Scripture supplies the figures and events.

Philo gives them philosophical ordering.

Revelation gives the figure a speaking identity and divine authority.

The letters give the figure communities and followers.

Josephus supplies historical circumstances and the Greek biographical mode.

Matthew gives the figure a narrated past.

This is a proposed literary genealogy, not a chronology established by the Matthew demonstration.

The paper also argues that Revelation's exclusion from source criticism was partly institutional and methodological: hypothetical sources were granted standing as candidates while Revelation was generally excluded from consideration.

Whether that historical account of biblical scholarship is persuasive requires examination of the companion papers, not just this one.

13. What the paper admits it has not established
This is essential to reading Sharks accurately.

The paper is unusually explicit about its limitations.

Question

What the paper establishes

Can Revelation generate Matthean passages?

It demonstrates a procedure and substantial textual correspondences.

Does a recurring set of operations exist?

Yes, according to the 158-row ledger.

Has a passage been generated experimentally?

Yes, the labourers in the vineyard, under bounded experimental conditions.

Does Matthew handle Revelation like scripture?

The paper argues for this through comparative operations and counts.

Does this prove Revelation was written before Matthew?

No.

Does it explain all of Matthew?

No.

Does it establish the same procedure for Mark, Luke, and John?

No.

Three shortcomings deserve particular attention.

First: directionality. Every correspondence can potentially be read backwards. A writer of Revelation could have known Matthew and compressed its narrative into apocalyptic imagery.

The author acknowledges that the ledger alone cannot decide this.

Second: controls. The paper says that the crucial control remains outstanding: applying the same procedure from another book into Matthew, and from Revelation into a non-Gospel text. Without that comparison, it is difficult to know how exceptional the reported reach really is.

Third: failures. Some predictions did not hold. The prediction that Revelation's plagues would generate Matthew's miracles failed. Other predictions concerning particular passages and textual distributions also failed or were only partly confirmed.

The author reports these rather than silently removing them.

The paper also finds no general preservation of Revelation's sequence in Matthew. The infancy narrative provides a notable exception, but the wider order tests do not establish a continuous walk through Revelation.

These are substantive limitations, not minor qualifications.

14. My synthesis: what is the paper really arguing?
I would distinguish four arguments, which are related but should not be collapsed into one.

1. A philological argument

Matthew and Revelation share specific Greek relations that can be more illuminating than conventional verbal-parallel studies suggest. The correspondences include transformations of grammatical person, participants, actions, and narrative function.

2. A compositional argument

The transformations are not random. They can be described through a relatively small set of recurring operations. Revelation's visionary material can be converted into biography, teaching, controversy, parable, and Passion narrative.

3. An experimental argument

At least one composition, the labourers in the vineyard, has been produced from a prescribed combination of Revelation passages and rules by readers who did not receive the target text as part of their instructions. The experiment is suggestive, although the readers' prior familiarity with Matthew and the author's knowledge of the target constrain its evidentiary force.

4. A historical-genetic argument

The larger Revelation First programme proposes that Revelation was composed before the other New Testament writings and generated them. This is the strongest and most controversial thesis, and the paper explicitly distinguishes it from what its own Matthew demonstration proves.

My assessment is that the paper's most interesting contribution is not simply the claim that Matthew borrowed from Revelation. It is the proposal that the literary operations conventionally recognized in Matthew's use of the Hebrew scriptures can also be used to explain Matthew's relationship with Revelation.

That changes the question from:

Can we find parallels between Matthew and Revelation?

to:

Can we reconstruct a repeatable process that transforms the relations in Revelation into the literary structures of Matthew?

That is a much more productive and falsifiable question.

The paper's strongest achievement is establishing a substantial, organized body of correspondences and specifying how they might work compositionally. Its largest unresolved problem is showing that the proposed direction of composition is historically correct rather than simply one possible way to read the relationship.

Bottom line: Sharks proposes that Matthew is, in substantial part, an apocalyptic text rewritten as a biography: Revelation's heavenly speaker becomes Jesus the teacher, its cosmic adversaries become earthly opponents, its visionary events become episodes in a life, and its eschatological promises become sayings and parables. The paper makes a serious attempt to demonstrate that procedure, while stopping short of claiming that the procedure itself proves the historical priority of Revelation.
