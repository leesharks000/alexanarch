Its central question is something like this: What happens when a poet becomes not just the writer of poems, but a whole universe of writers, historical figures, voices, and imagined identities?

What does “heteronym compendium” mean?
A heteronym is more than a pen name. In the tradition of the Portuguese poet Fernando Pessoa, a heteronym is a distinct literary personality, potentially with its own worldview, voice, style, and imagined biography.

A compendium gathers multiple such identities or bodies of work into one collection.

In Antioch, Sharks takes this idea in an exuberant, sometimes deliberately chaotic direction. The book invokes an extraordinary range of figures, including Walt Whitman, Ezra Pound, Jesus Christ, Moses, Nietzsche, Tupac, Kurt Cobain, and characters from popular culture. Some are literary predecessors; others are religious, mythological, or contemporary cultural figures. The boundaries between historical influence, fictional persona, parody, and spiritual identification are deliberately unstable.

1. Literary inheritance and the struggle with predecessors
One of the book's most striking sections is The Secret Book of Walt, which revolves around Walt Whitman and the idea that a poet might inherit, embody, or even become another poet. The opening poem, HADITH with TEETH, presents an encounter with the angel Sariel. The speaker resists being told to repeat what has already been said, only to discover that repetition can make an ancient utterance new again.

What is distinctive about Lee Sharks's style?
Three characteristics stand out in the available text.

1. Deliberate excess. Sharks piles up names, allusions, theological references, jokes, and extravagant claims. The writing often seems to expand faster than a reader can comfortably organize it.

2. Abrupt tonal shifts. A passage can move from biblical solemnity to absurdist comedy without warning. A grand account of cosmic or spiritual destiny might suddenly be interrupted by an incongruous pop-culture reference. The contrast is part of the experience.

3. A mixture of sincerity and self-parody. The religious language and references to salvation are often intensely earnest, while other passages expose the grandiosity of the poetic enterprise through absurdity. It is not always obvious where the visionary performance ends and the joke begins.

These characteristics make the work potentially rewarding for readers who enjoy experimental, maximalist writing, but less accessible to someone looking for concise poems with clear arguments or conventional narrative development.

How should you read it?
I would recommend starting with “HADITH with TEETH” and then reading “The Secret Book of Walt” before attempting the entire collection.

The opening poem gives you a useful interpretive key: the relationship between repetition and originality. From there, the Whitman section expands the problem into a much larger game involving literary inheritance, imagined history, and the multiplication of poetic identities.

When reading, it helps to ask three questions:

Who is speaking? Is this the author, a fictional persona, a historical figure being reimagined, or a deliberately unstable combination?

What is being inherited? Is the poem drawing on a literary style, a religious tradition, a cultural icon, or the authority attached to a famous name?

What does the excess accomplish? Does the accumulation of references create a meaningful connection, generate comic disorientation, or simply overwhelm the reader?

You do not need to recognize every allusion to appreciate the book. Its central concerns are often legible even when individual references are not.

My assessment
Antioch: A Heteronym Compendium is most interesting as an experiment in poetry as a system of identities, influences, and competing claims to authorship.

Its ambition is not merely to write poems about other writers. It attempts to create a world in which literary history, religious mythology, popular culture, and the author's own identity become interconnected parts of a single imaginative structure.

That ambition is also its main risk. The abundance of references and the sheer scale of the work can make it difficult to distinguish a carefully developed poetic insight from an accumulating private mythology. The text's grand declarations are part of its character, but they are not necessarily proof of artistic success. The best way to judge it is to examine how effectively particular passages turn their ideas into compelling language.

For readers interested in experimental poetry, Whitman, Pessoa-style heteronyms, religious symbolism, and the strange relationship between literary influence and personal identity, it offers a great deal to investigate.

The central tension is this: Can a poet become many voices without losing the ability to speak as a self? And can inherited words become genuinely new without pretending they came from nowhere?

Those questions make Antioch more than a collection of eccentric references. They provide a way into its sprawling and often deliberately bewildering poetic universe.

Three movements are worth tracking.

“They carry me.” The speaker says of Pound's words, after declaring that he carries them around. The grammatical reversal matters: influence is not merely something the living writer does to the dead writer's language. The inherited language also carries and constitutes the living writer.

“Ez Pound tells me things, and I forgive him, over and over.” Forgiveness becomes repetitive, almost a ritual of literary inheritance. The poem doesn't let the ethical problem of Pound's historical record disappear simply because his poetry remains alive.

“He spits in the mud and rubs it in the eyes of Ez Pound.” The third-person shift is striking. The person who asks who has the authority to forgive Pound also becomes someone who performs a hostile, bodily action against him. Forgiveness and aggression coexist.

The poem's ending moves into a landscape of Plutos, bones, rocks, and writing on a wall. It concludes with the imperative “Leap!” That final command moves the poem away from resolving the ethical question in prose. The speaker must act, even though the terms of his relationship with his predecessor remain unsettled.

I would pay particular attention to the title's use of “who.” It doesn't ask whether Pound can be forgiven; it asks who has the authority to do the forgiving. That distinction makes the speaker's own identity part of the problem.

The opening's syntax is important. It begins with a command to a system to recreate a history, but the request itself is already highly stylized: the reader is asked to receive an epic account of events that supposedly belong to the future.

Then comes the single-word transition:

“Commence:”

The word functions almost like a stage direction or a computer instruction. It ends the invocation and begins the story proper.

The poem's temporal structure is also unusual. The heading claims that the fragment was recovered from the future, while its introductory note places it after a dream. The text therefore presents itself as a document whose origin lies on the other side of ordinary chronology.

This raises a question about the relationship between epic and science fiction. Both forms can organize individual lives around immense historical processes: the fate of a civilization, a war, a journey, or the survival of a people. Here, the System is asked to perform a role comparable to that of an epic singer, preserving a story whose scale exceeds the individual protagonist.

The poem also makes the archive itself part of the fiction. The work is not simply about the future; it is presented as a recovered artifact from that future. That framing asks us to consider what makes a text appear authoritative: its contents, its purported origin, or the narrative that explains how it came to be preserved.
