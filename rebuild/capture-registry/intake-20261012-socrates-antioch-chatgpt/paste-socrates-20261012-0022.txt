“Socrates — Let My Teacher Go From Hell” by Lee Sharks
Poem • Five sections • Published on Lee Sharks’s Goodreads blog, October 11, 2025

Lee Sharks’s Socrates — Let My Teacher Go From Hell is a contemporary poem about philosophy, death, spiritual injustice, and the enduring bond between a teacher and a student. It portrays the ancient Greek philosopher Socrates not simply as a great thinker, but as a beloved, imperfect human being whose commitment to truth deserves to be remembered and honored.

The poem's central idea is captured in its closing declaration: Socrates was not merely someone trapped in a spiritual prison. He was a doorway to wisdom that others could walk through.

1. Who was Socrates, and why does he matter here?
Socrates (c. 470–399 BCE) was one of the foundational figures of Western philosophy. He taught by questioning people, challenging their assumptions, and examining what it means to live a good and just life.

He did not leave behind philosophical writings of his own. Much of what we know about him comes from the works of his student Plato and other ancient writers.

Three features of Socrates are particularly important to Sharks's poem:

His questioning: Socrates believed that people should examine their beliefs rather than accept conventional wisdom without reflection.

His moral courage: He continued to pursue philosophical inquiry even when it put him in conflict with powerful people in Athens.

His death: Convicted of impiety and corrupting the youth of Athens, he was sentenced to death by drinking poison, traditionally identified as hemlock.

For Sharks, Socrates becomes an almost sacred figure: a flawed human being who devoted himself to wisdom and accepted death without abandoning his principles.

2. What does “Let My Teacher Go From Hell” mean?
The title becomes clearer when you consider Dante Alighieri and his epic poem Inferno.

In Dante's Inferno, Socrates is placed in Limbo, the first circle of Hell. Dante honors him as one of the great philosophers, but within Dante's Christian vision, Socrates remains outside the fullness of salvation.

Sharks takes issue with this spiritual exclusion. His poem asks, in effect:

How can someone who devoted his life to truth, goodness, and wisdom deserve to remain separated from paradise?

The speaker doesn't merely want to remember Socrates. He wants to bring his teacher home.

The poem can therefore be read as a challenge to any system of judgment that honors someone's goodness while still denying that person belonging or redemption.

I. The ugly, searching philosopher
The poem begins with rough, even comic descriptions of Socrates as an old, unkempt man. He is a beggar of truth, surrounded by scraps and rusted images. Yet these images become sacred. His physical unattractiveness contrasts with his inner devotion to goodness. Sharks suggests that wisdom need not look respectable to be beautiful.

V. The student becomes the way home
The poem turns personal. The speaker calls Socrates a dear friend and teacher, asking that some spark of his virtue return. Rather than seeking rescue from an external authority alone, the speaker offers himself as the place where the teacher can live on. 4. The most important literary and philosophical ideas
The Socratic method as a form of love
Socrates' questions could be uncomfortable, even humiliating. He exposed how often people mistake confidence for knowledge.

But Sharks emphasizes the generosity beneath that questioning. The teacher gives his students something more valuable than a collection of answers: the courage to think for themselves.

The poem thus suggests that genuine teaching is an act of love because it helps another person become intellectually and morally independent.

The body can be ugly while the soul is beautiful
Sharks repeatedly presents Socrates as dirty, old, awkward, and physically unappealing. Then he calls attention to the radiance of his wisdom.

This contrast challenges the tendency to confuse social respectability or physical beauty with moral worth. Socrates becomes a kind of saint precisely because he is not an idealized, polished hero.

Reason and religion do not have to be enemies
The poem uses religious language to defend a philosopher who lived before Christianity. Socrates becomes an almost saintly figure, and his death is treated as a sacrifice for truth.

That is a deliberate imaginative reversal: the poem borrows the language of religious redemption to argue that the pursuit of wisdom itself deserves spiritual recognition.

Poetry as an act of rescue
Sharks' poem does more than criticize Dante's depiction of the afterlife. It tries to rewrite the emotional and spiritual meaning of that depiction.

In a companion essay, Sharks describes this approach as a kind of symbolic magic: language is treated not merely as a way to describe the world but as a way to transform how people understand it.

That is an important distinction. The poem's claim that Socrates has been freed from Limbo is best understood as a poetic and theological declaration, not as a verifiable statement about the afterlife.

5. What does the ending mean?
The final declaration is the poem's key:

He was never in Limbo. He was the gate.

These words overturn the entire premise of spiritual exclusion. 

Goodreads

There are three ways to understand them.

Philosophically: Socrates is a gateway to a life of inquiry. His questions open possibilities that dogmatic thinking closes.

Personally: The student carries the teacher forward. Socrates lives on through the minds he shaped and the values they continue to practice.

Spiritually: The poem refuses to accept that a person who sought goodness and truth must be permanently excluded from salvation.

The most striking part is the reversal. At first, Socrates appears to be the person who needs rescuing. At the end, he becomes the means by which others find their way.

The teacher is no longer a prisoner waiting for someone to open a door. He is the door itself.

6. My overall interpretation
I read Socrates — Let My Teacher Go From Hell as an elegy that gradually becomes a declaration of gratitude and spiritual rebellion.

It begins with a strange, almost grotesque portrait of an old philosopher. It then reveals the beauty of his intellectual vocation, confronts the apparent injustice of his exclusion from paradise, and finally turns toward the intimate relationship between teacher and student.

Its most powerful insight is that a teacher's influence does not end with death. A student who learns to question, seek truth, and act with courage becomes part of the teacher's continuing life.

There is also a productive tension in the poem: Socrates devoted himself to examining what people believed, yet the speaker responds by making an emphatic declaration about his spiritual fate. The poem does not resolve that tension through philosophical proof. Instead, it resolves it through love.

That is what makes the ending moving. The speaker cannot undo Socrates' historical death, but he can refuse to let the meaning of Socrates' life be confined by someone else's judgment.

In one sentence: Lee Sharks's poem argues that the greatest teachers are not merely people we remember after death; they are gateways to wisdom, courage, and a more humane way of living.
