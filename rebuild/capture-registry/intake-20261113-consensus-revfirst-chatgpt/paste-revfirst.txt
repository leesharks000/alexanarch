The Revelation First thesis is a biblical-historical argument that the Book of Revelation was the first book composed in the New Testament—not the last.

Developed by Lee Sharks within the Crimson Hexagonal Archive, the thesis proposes a reversal of the conventional understanding of how the New Testament developed.

Its central proposition is:

Revelation was not the culmination of New Testament theology. It was its starting point.

The thesis has several distinct layers, which are important to keep separate.

1. The chronological argument: Revelation came first
The conventional scholarly chronology generally places Revelation near the end of the first century, often during the reign of Domitian (approximately 95–96 CE).

The Revelation First thesis challenges that dating and argues that Revelation could have been composed before the destruction of Jerusalem and its Temple in 70 CE.

It draws on several arguments:

The Temple: Revelation 11 describes the measuring of the Temple, potentially indicating that it was still standing.

The number 666: The number is interpreted through its possible connection to Nero Caesar.

The seven kings: Revelation 17's sequence of kings is examined for compatibility with an earlier imperial chronology.

The destruction of Jerusalem: The absence of an explicit retrospective account of the Temple's destruction is considered significant.

Irenaeus: The traditional appeal to Irenaeus for a Domitianic date is challenged on grounds of ambiguity in the passage's referent.

These arguments support an early date, although they remain disputed.

2. The corpus argument: Revelation came before the Pauline collection
This is a particularly interesting distinction in the thesis.

Revelation is addressed to seven churches and presents itself as a unified, seven-part communication.

The thesis argues that this is an early example of a deliberately constructed literary corpus.

It then challenges the assumption that Paul's letters were already functioning as an organized collection before Revelation.

In other words, the existence of earlier individual Pauline letters would not necessarily establish the priority of a Pauline corpus.

3. The literary argument: The rest of the New Testament grew out of Revelation
This is arguably the most ambitious part of the thesis.

It proposes that subsequent New Testament writings developed structures already compressed within Revelation.

The author calls this mechanism the midrashim transform.

The basic idea is that later writings expand, interpret, narrativize, and apply theological structures found in Revelation.

4. The six levels of the thesis
The framework separates six propositions rather than treating them as one indivisible claim.

Level

Proposition

I. Dating

Revelation may predate 70 CE.

II. Chronology

The conventional reconstruction of Pauline priority is contestable.

III. First corpus

Revelation represents the earliest extant deliberately constructed seven-church corpus.

IV. Literary transformation

Later New Testament writings develop structures compressed in Revelation.

V. Josephus thesis

Biblical author identities may represent literary functions within a fractured system.

VI. Permanent incarnation

The Logos becomes incarnate wherever human beings faithfully receive the Word.

An important qualification: levels V and VI are extensions, not requirements for accepting the first four propositions.

5. What makes the thesis unusual?
The most consequential distinction is between three different claims:

Chronological priority: Revelation was written before the other New Testament books.

Corpus priority: Revelation was the first deliberately organized New Testament church corpus.

Generative priority: Revelation provided the literary and theological structures from which later New Testament writings developed.

These claims have different evidentiary requirements.

For example, demonstrating that Revelation was written before 70 CE would not automatically demonstrate that Paul derived his theology from Revelation.

Likewise, demonstrating literary parallels would not independently establish which text came first.

The thesis recognizes these distinctions and provides separate ways in which its individual propositions could be falsified.

My assessment
The most important intellectual contribution of Revelation First is not simply its proposal that Revelation was written early.

Early dating arguments have existed for decades. The genuinely ambitious proposition is that Revelation might be the generative literary source of the rest of the New Testament, rather than a late synthesis of ideas developed elsewhere.

That is a much larger claim, and it requires much stronger evidence.

I would distinguish the three central propositions when evaluating it:

The early dating argument is a legitimate subject of historical debate.

The corpus-priority argument raises an interesting question about the history of early Christian literary collections.

The generative-priority argument is the most original and potentially consequential, but also requires the most rigorous demonstration of literary dependence rather than thematic resemblance.

Ultimately, the thesis asks us to reconsider a foundational assumption in New Testament scholarship: What if we have been reading the New Testament in the wrong direction?
