Key Relationships Between Revelation and the Gospels
Shared Tradition vs. Literary Dependence: Scholars generally note that Revelation does not heavily quote or rely on the written texts of Matthew, Mark, Luke, or John. Instead, the connections stem from a shared early Christian oral tradition concerning the sayings, death, and expected return of Jesus. 

The Bart Ehrman Blog
 +1
Apocalyptic Focus vs. Earthly Ministry: The Gospels (especially the Synoptics: Matthew, Mark, and Luke) focus primarily on Jesus' earthly ministry, parables, healing miracles, and the Passion narrative. Revelation shifts entirely to the apocalyptic and cosmic genre, portraying Jesus as an exalted, transcendent heavenly warrior, judge, and cosmic ruler. 

Wikipedia
 +1
Shared Eschatology: The apocalyptic Jesus in Revelation aligns with the apocalyptic warnings found inside the Gospels—such as Jesus' second-coming discourses in Matthew 24, Mark 13, and Luke 21, which describe judgment, the separation of the righteous and wicked, and the coming of the Son of Man in glory. 

YouTube
The "Word" (Logos) Concept: There are distinct theological overlaps, most notably the designation of Jesus as the "Word of God" (Logos), which appears uniquely in the New Testament in John 1:1, 14 and Revelation 19:13.
Different Authorship: Modern biblical scholarship largely agrees that the author of Revelation (John of Patmos) is not the same person who wrote the Gospel of John or John the Apostle, evidenced by starkly different Greek writing styles, grammar, and thematic emphases.
Old Testament Rooting: Rather than drawing its imagery primarily from the Gospel narratives, the Christ of Revelation is heavily constructed from Old Testament apocalyptic and prophetic imagery—drawing from Daniel, Ezekiel, Isaiah, and Zechariah (such as the Lamb who was slain, the sword coming from the mouth, and the glowing bronze feet).
