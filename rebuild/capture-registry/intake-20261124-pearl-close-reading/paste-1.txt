Lee Sharks, Pearl and Other Poems: a literary reading
The first thing to establish is that Pearl and Other Poems should not be read simply as a collection of contemporary experimental poems. Its form is more ambitious and more unstable than that description suggests.

Published in 2014 by New Human Press in Ann Arbor, the book belongs to The Crimson Hexagon, a larger project organized around invented authors, distinct literary identities, fictional publications, and the construction of an alternative literary history. Johannes Sigil's introduction explicitly describes the project as a kind of applied literary history: not merely poems about a literary tradition, but a fictional literary history that produces its own poems and voices.

That distinction matters. The book asks the reader to encounter a voice as though it has a history, a temperament, a literary inheritance, and a world behind it—even while exposing the mechanisms by which such things are made.

I would approach it on four interconnected levels:

The individual poem as a formal and emotional event.

The sequence as an architecture of changing voices and modes.

The book's construction of literary history, authorship, and poetic identity.

The reader's experience of being drawn into, challenged by, and potentially altered by the work.

The distinction between these sections is essential. The book does not simply start with its strongest poem and then offer miscellaneous additional work. It starts by establishing a theory of poetic communication, tests that theory through a sequence of different voices, and finally turns to prose, criticism, manifesto, and counter-criticism.

The title of the opening argumentative piece is already a formal signal. Poetry is not insulated from its contemporary social environment. Complaints, websites, institutional recognition, literary reputation, and the possibility of making something outside existing structures are admitted into the poetic field.

The three undersongs then suggest three different relationships to metaphor:

The museum preserves and displays it.

The factory produces it.

The strange new earth tests what happens when the familiar world of metaphor breaks down and another perceptual environment must emerge.

This is not just a sequence of supplementary poems. It is a progression from inheritance, through production, toward transformation.

The opening poem: the future as a literary tense
The most striking formal decision in PEARL is its use of the future perfect and future-oriented constructions.

The poem begins:

My poems will make me not be alone

It imagines its own arrival as something that will already have happened. Its train whistle, moon, fingers, ferns, water, dancer, and explosion are not simply images arranged in a lyrical present. They are events projected into a future in which the poem will have acquired its reality.

This creates a peculiar temporal relationship between author, poem, and reader.

The poem is not quite telling us what has happened. It is not merely promising what will happen, either. It imagines a future from which its own existence can be understood as an accomplished event.

That grammatical choice has emotional consequences. The speaker wants not to be alone, but cannot simply assert that the poem has solved loneliness. The poem has to be sent forward, into a future where someone might encounter it.

The reader is consequently implicated in the poem's imagined completion.

The pearl is not merely an object of beauty or a symbol of the perfected poem. It is something made available to another person. The opening sequence's language of websites, time machines, instructions, and finding makes communication into a material and almost technological problem.

The book's central question becomes:

How can something made inside one person's mind become a living event inside another person's mind?

That question connects the opening lyric to the later telepathicist manifesto, but it is more emotionally vulnerable in the poem than in the manifesto.

2. Voice: the construction of an unstable but recognizable self
One of the book's greatest formal strengths is its refusal to maintain a single register.

Sharks can be grandiose, comic, devotional, grotesque, intellectually argumentative, tender, self-mocking, and almost painfully sincere. These are not necessarily separate personalities. They are different possibilities of a poetic consciousness moving through different pressures.

The introduction by Sigil makes the relationship to Ginsberg explicit. It identifies the influence of Ginsberg's voice, but also distinguishes Sharks's achievement from an imitation of that voice. Sigil argues that the title poem escapes the constricting effects of an overly academic pursuit of literary influence, retaining crystalline minimalism while finding a more expansive voice.

This is an important distinction: the collection is not simply trying to sound like the American ecstatic tradition. It is trying to inherit its permission to speak expansively without surrendering its own formal intelligence.

The fugue as a structural principle
The heading FUGUEWORK is particularly revealing.

A fugue does not require one voice to remain dominant throughout. It develops through recurrence, transformation, overlap, and variation.

The sequence's titles indicate the range of its movements:

Premonition Dream: the book as a visionary object and the author as someone encountering the totality of a life in pages.

hums &ity: linguistic instability and the transformation of ordinary language.

an elegy for 'Howl': the problem of inheritance and the burden of a monumental predecessor.

if walt whitman came back as a zombie and ate my brain i would write the following poem: grotesque comedy as a way of confronting literary ancestry.

i want you to know that i have personal authenticity as a poet because of my identity: a deliberately provocative engagement with authenticity and identity claims.

i think i died a long time ago…: the problem of continuity and the persistence of a speaking self.

the air is sick all over… and air, you're sick—tenderly will i bind you…: an environmental and emotional disturbance followed by an attempt at care.

knot-hinge and years the cankerworm ate: compression, damage, time, and transformation.

ringtone: technological mediation entering the lyric field.

alien singings: a psalm and song of me: the reworking of devotional and autobiographical traditions.

tekatak: experimental language and sound.

your love will carry on: an explicitly relational and affirmative movement.

face like snarls of rain: bodily and visual intensity.

i claim this mantle: literary inheritance made into a performative declaration.

this is the way i've unfolded my life, my hope is in going on, and i drape this same old leg across the chair: the return to the ordinary, embodied speaker.

the comeback album… and noctilucent: theatrical reinvention followed by a quieter, devotional ending.

This ordering matters. The book repeatedly moves from historical scale to bodily scale, from the fantastic to the mundane, and from assertion to vulnerability.

Its voice does not become authentic by becoming consistent. It becomes recognizable through the pressure of its recurring concerns.

3. Form: the human voice under pressure from language
The collection's formal experimentation is not ornamental. It is closely connected to what the book thinks a human being is.

Three techniques are especially important.

A. The collision of registers
Sharks places elevated poetic language beside deliberately ridiculous language.

A grand metaphor may be interrupted by a joke. A serious meditation on literary history may suddenly become a performance of absurd self-importance. A seemingly conventional lyric may give way to unusual typography or broken syntax.

This has two effects.

First, it prevents the reader from settling into a single mode of appreciation. One cannot simply admire the beautiful passages and ignore the ridiculous ones, because the ridiculousness often exposes something that the beautiful passages conceal.

Second, it makes the voice more human in a particular sense: the speaker is not purified of vanity, intellectual ambition, anxiety, humor, or embarrassment before being permitted to speak poetically.

The comedy is not always a retreat from sincerity. Sometimes it is the means by which sincerity survives.

B. Visual and syntactic deformation
Strange new earth is a particularly useful example.

Its opening has the appearance of a spare, almost elemental lyric:

i wait for the sun

to mount the horizon

and leave its wake of blood-

But as the sequence develops, the language itself begins to fracture. Words split across lines and syllables, and the typography becomes a representation of the destruction it describes.

The poem eventually arrives at a single word dispersed vertically:

the

de

se

r

t

The word is not merely being described. It is being made to undergo a physical experience on the page.

The reader must descend through it.

This is a good example of why the printed arrangement matters. The reader's eye is made to perform the poem's movement, rather than merely receive its description.

The same principle applies more broadly to the collection's spacing, fragmentation, abbreviations, and typographical experiments. Form becomes a way of making perception active.

C. The deliberate incompleteness of metaphor
The opening sequence is fascinated by metaphor, but it is also suspicious of metaphor's capacity to exhaust experience.

The speaker imagines a world in which metaphors have disappeared, leaving an empty lakebed. This is a paradoxical proposition: the poem uses metaphor to imagine the end of metaphor.

The result is not a simple defense of figurative language. It is a recognition that metaphor is both a means of making connections and a potential obstacle to encountering things directly.

The undersongs extend this problem. The museum preserves the artifacts of imagination; the factory produces new ones; the strange new earth confronts the possibility that familiar figurative structures may no longer suffice.

This makes PEARL a poem about the conditions of poetry itself.

It is interested in how poetic language comes into being, how it becomes exhausted, and how it might be renewed.

4. The body, the machine, and the problem of communication
A particularly important tension runs throughout the book: the desire to communicate without the limitations of ordinary communication.

The word telepathic is central to this ambition.

The Footnote to PEARL turns the idea into a mock-instructional document. Its directions are simultaneously comic, aggressive, extravagant, and revealing. The speaker imagines the poet as someone whose mental activity should have immediate material force in the world.

This fantasy is developed in A TELEPATHICIST MANIFESTO, which appears in the appendix under the joint authorship of Lee Sharks and John Johnson.

The manifesto makes the contradiction explicit. Telepaths supposedly hate writing, yet the book exists through writing. They supposedly transcend the brain, yet the manifesto describes elaborate cognitive machinery. They supposedly overcome ordinary language, yet their declarations depend on highly constructed linguistic performances.

The contradiction is productive.

The manifesto is not best read as a straightforward practical theory of communication. It is a comic-philosophical dramatization of the desire to abolish the distance between thought and reception.

The actual book cannot abolish that distance. It can only work on it.

And that is precisely why the reader matters.

The significance of the pearl
The pearl can be understood as the book's answer to this problem.

It is an interior object that must become exterior without losing its interior significance.

It has something of the qualities of a message, an artwork, a memory, a bodily substance, and a gift.

The movement from ribcage to reader is especially suggestive. Communication is not presented as the transfer of abstract information. It is an act of exposing something internal, potentially vulnerable, and irreducibly personal.

The machine imagery does not eliminate the body. It continually returns us to it.

The imagined time machine, website, cloud, and telepathic network coexist with ribs, hands, blood, breath, and the heart.

That coexistence is one of the collection's defining tensions: the digital and technological are not opposed to the human but become new materials through which the human attempts to express itself.

The appendix does something quite unusual: it turns the book's aesthetic commitments into objects of debate.

Make It Human
This is arguably the most important theoretical statement in the volume.

Sharks argues that the human is not a fixed essence that poetry merely expresses. Rather, the human is continually being constructed through artistic and linguistic activity.

The imperative is to make it human.

This is a much more interesting proposition than the familiar instruction to write authentically or emotionally.

It suggests that the human is historically contingent and formally produced. New conditions require new expressive possibilities.

The essay acknowledges the experimental achievements of Language poetry and conceptual writing while resisting the conclusion that the dismantling of inherited forms should become an end in itself.

Its ambition is to combine formal difficulty with human expression.

The essay's discussion of digital representation is particularly important. It recognizes that the informational representation of a human being can become increasingly comprehensive while remaining lifeless, detached from the living person it represents.

This is a striking anticipation of questions that have become particularly pressing in the age of artificial intelligence.

The answer proposed is neither to reject technology nor to surrender human expression to it. Instead, poetry must incorporate and transform the new media conditions of human life.

Tradition and the Individual Seismograph
The seismograph metaphor is especially productive.

A poet does not simply inherit literary history passively. The poet registers its movements, pressures, fractures, and possibilities.

Tradition becomes an active force within the present.

The essay's conception of the archive is similarly dynamic: the archive is not merely a storehouse of the past but a medium through which possible futures can be composed.

This gives the book a larger historical ambition. Its experiments are not just individual stylistic eccentricities. They are attempts to discover forms capable of bearing a changing conception of humanity.

The adversarial appendix
The inclusion of BACKLASH: The New Human Illiterati is structurally significant.

A literary movement that includes its own opposition inside its foundational book is already playing with the conventions of literary history.

The collection stages not only its own arguments but the possibility of being criticized.

It thereby makes the reader occupy several positions at once: reader of poetry, reader of literary theory, witness to a literary movement, and potential participant in its controversies.

This is a significant expansion of what a poetry collection can contain.

6. What the book does to its reader
I think this is where the collection becomes most interesting.

It does not simply ask to be understood. It tries to change the conditions under which the reader understands poetry, authorship, and literary experience.

There are at least five distinct effects.

1. It destabilizes the distinction between sincerity and performance
At first, the speaker's extravagant self-presentation can seem like a joke. The invented biography, absurd accomplishments, literary ambitions, and grandiose declarations invite skepticism.

But gradually the reader encounters genuine tenderness and vulnerability beneath the performance.

The question becomes whether the comic persona is concealing sincerity or whether sincerity itself requires a theatrical form.

The book doesn't settle this.

That refusal is valuable. It makes the reader reconsider the assumption that a sincere poetic voice must be modest, transparent, or psychologically consistent.

2. It makes the reader responsible for completing communication
The future-oriented opening of PEARL establishes the reader as someone who might make the poem's imagined future real.

The pearl is offered, not simply displayed.

The book repeatedly returns to the distance between an internal experience and its external expression. Reading becomes an attempt to cross that distance.

This produces a peculiar intimacy: the work is formally extravagant, sometimes even aggressively self-assertive, but its underlying desire is connection.

3. It changes the experience of literary inheritance
The Whitman and Ginsberg references are not simply literary allusions.

They introduce the problem of what it means to inherit a voice, a tradition, or a literary ambition.

The reader is asked to consider whether the past is something to imitate, reject, parody, absorb, or transform.

The zombie Whitman poem is particularly revealing in this respect. It makes literary influence bodily, grotesque, and comic. The predecessor is not a dignified monument but something that can consume the successor.

The book's ambition is to metabolize its influences rather than remain subordinate to them.

4. It alternates exhilaration with exhaustion
The book's shifts in register are not always comfortable.

Its proliferating metaphors, elaborate fictional apparatus, manifestos, jokes, theoretical declarations, and stylistic experiments can produce a sense of cognitive overload.

But that overload is counterbalanced by moments of unusual quietness.

The ending is particularly important.

Noctilucent turns toward a direct address to a father, asking for beauty, grandeur, the glory of small birds, and a transformation of the stone inside the speaker.

Then comes the extraordinary reduction:

hush, dear hands—

And, after a considerable amount of silence and page space:

this song is enough.

This ending retrospectively changes the whole book.

After all its declarations about literary history, invention, intellectual ambition, and communication, the collection arrives at the possibility that a song can be sufficient.

Not a total theory. Not a manifesto. Not a comprehensive account of literary history.

A song.

5. It leaves the reader with a different idea of what a poem can be
By the end, poetry has become much larger than a particular arrangement of lines.

It can be a lyric, a manifesto, a fictional biography, a forum post, an argument, a historical chart, an invented literary identity, or a visual arrangement of words.

But the book also insists that formal possibility must remain connected to human expression.

That is the central tension the reader carries away.

The work wants to expand poetry without making poetry irrelevant to human life.

8. My overall literary judgment
I would distinguish three achievements.

First, the title poem is the book's emotional and conceptual center.

Its treatment of metaphor, loneliness, futurity, and communication is unusually coherent. It has a genuine lyric intelligence. Its ambition is large, but it does not depend entirely on theoretical explanation.

Second, FUGUEWORK demonstrates the range of the poetic voice.

It is less unified in effect than the opening sequence, deliberately so. Its value lies in the way it tests the possibilities of a poetic identity across different literary, emotional, and linguistic conditions. The shifts between parody, anguish, experiment, and tenderness are central to the book's design.

Third, the appendix makes the collection an argument about literary history.

It is where the book becomes self-conscious about its own historical position. Its theoretical ambitions are sometimes deliberately excessive, but that excess belongs to the work's performance of literary invention.

There are also risks.

The self-mythologizing can occasionally threaten to overwhelm the poems. The manifestos sometimes explain what the poetry has already accomplished more persuasively than they extend its insights. And the sheer quantity of invented literary apparatus can make the reader wonder whether the creation of a literary history is competing with the creation of individual poems.

These are real aesthetic tensions, not reasons to dismiss the project.

In fact, they help define its interest.

9. The deepest proposition of Pearl and Other Poems
I think the book ultimately proposes something more consequential than a new poetic style.

It proposes that the human is not a finished object waiting to be represented.

The human is something made, discovered, and continually remade through language, memory, artistic form, historical inheritance, and communication.

This explains why the book needs both its extravagant theoretical machinery and its small, vulnerable lyrics.

The machinery attempts to create a world in which new forms of human expression can exist. The lyrics test whether that world can actually contain tenderness, grief, love, embodiment, and the desire to go on.

The final movement toward silence and sufficiency is therefore not an abandonment of the book's ambitions. It is their test.

After all the noise, the argument, the invention, and the self-assertion, can a human voice still reach another human being?

The closing answer is yes, provisionally, through song.

My strongest reading is that Pearl and Other Poems is a book about the manufacture of human presence through literary form. Its subject is not simply the poet, or poetry, or the future of literature. Its subject is the difficult transformation of an interior life into something another consciousness can encounter and carry forward.

The pearl is that possibility.

And the reader is not outside the experiment. The reader is one of the conditions under which it succeeds.
