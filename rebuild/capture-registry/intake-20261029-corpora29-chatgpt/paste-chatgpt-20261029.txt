EA-CORPORA-29 by Lee Sharks: what it is and how it works
EA-CORPORA-29 is a multilingual, historically wide-ranging digital literary and textual archive. More precisely, it is the twenty-ninth deposit, or seating, in Lee Sharks's EA-CORPORA series, organized as part of the Originals Shelf of the Crimson Hexagonal Archive.

Its full title is:

EA-CORPORA-29 — The Twenty-Ninth Seating, the Pluriform: Horace and Ovid, the Art of Poetry in Twenty Traditions, the Avant-Garde II, Telepathic Prose, and the Buddhist Canons in Pali, Sanskrit, Chinese, Korean, Japanese and Tibetan.

The document is dated October 28, 2026.

The important distinction is that this is not simply an anthology, a bibliography, or a collection of literary recommendations. It is a structured deposit of primary texts, editions, documentary records, and explicitly declared absences, assembled with provenance and verification information.

The document describes eleven seats containing 196 works, 876,386 lines, and 564 files.

One particularly significant feature is that the Buddhist texts are deliberately represented across languages. For example, the Lotus, Diamond, Heart, and Vimalakīrti sutras appear in Sanskrit, Chinese, and Tibetan textual traditions.

2. How does it work?
The central organizing mechanism is what Sharks calls a seat.

A seat is a separately assembled and documented collection of works within the larger corpus.

Each seat has its own files and documentary apparatus. The overall process works as follows.

Selection

The author determines which works and traditions belong in the deposit. The selection is guided by a larger intellectual purpose rather than by a single language, author, genre, or historical period.

Source acquisition

The source texts are retrieved from repositories such as Perseus, Wikisource, GRETIL, Project Gutenberg, Aozora Bunko, Internet Archive, and SuttaCentral.

Preservation of originals

The retrieved source materials are retained in an original/ layer. This preserves the source representation, including relevant edition and repository information.

Text extraction and normalization

Scripts produce a more consistent text/ layer. Depending on the source, this can involve extracting TEI text, joining separate pages, preserving verse divisions, retaining headings, or normalizing line endings.

Verification

The compiler identifies particular passages, called verified loci, within each work. These provide concrete points at which the extracted text can be checked against the intended source.

Provenance and integrity recording

Each seat receives a source.json and a MANIFEST.sha256. These document the source and edition, licensing, normalization, verification locations, and file hashes.

Deposit and cross-referencing

The completed seats are placed on the Originals Shelf and connected to related works and earlier deposits.

The document explicitly identifies the construction scripts:

pali29.py: the Pali Buddhist canon.

tib29.py: Tibetan material.

absences29.py: declared absences.

absences29_wanted.py: wanted works and their documentary treatment.

The builders are located under rebuild/corpora/ea-corpora-29/.

This is important: EA-CORPORA-29 is not just a manually assembled folder. It has a reproducible technical construction process.

3. Why are hashes, source records, and verified loci important?
These are central to understanding the project's method.

The SHA-256 manifests

Every seat has a MANIFEST.sha256 file. Its purpose is to establish file-level integrity: someone rebuilding or inspecting the deposit can check whether the files match the recorded hashes.

The source records

The source.json files document where the material came from, which edition was used, what licence applies, and how the text was normalized.

The verified loci

These are identifiable passages within individual works that the compiler has checked.

For example, the Horace seat identifies Ars Poetica 1 and lines 453–476 as verified locations. The Ovid seat identifies the opening of Amores and the closing of Metamorphoses 15.

These details matter because a digital text can be present without necessarily being a faithful representation of the edition it claims to represent.

The project therefore tries to preserve not only the text, but also the evidence needed to understand and inspect its textual origin.

There is an important qualification: a verified locus is evidence of checking a particular passage, not proof that every character of every text is error-free.

4. A particularly interesting feature: declared absences
One of the more distinctive aspects of EA-CORPORA-29 is that it documents works it does not actually possess.

The clearest example is Seat 29/01 — Jack Kerouac.

It records 14 declared works, but holds none of their texts. Instead, it documents:

Which works are wanted.

Their publication information.

Why they cannot yet be included.

Their copyright status.

When they may become available.

What documentary evidence is still needed.

For example, the document identifies On the Road as potentially opening on 1 January 2053, conditional on copyright renewal status.

This is a deliberate archival decision.

Rather than silently omitting unavailable works, the archive records their absence as information.

The same approach appears in the poetics and Buddhist seats, where works that could not be retrieved, or whose licensing prevented inclusion, are identified as queued or wanted.

This creates a distinction between three states:

Present: the work has actually been deposited.

Declared absence: the work is identified, but its text is not held.

Queued: the work is intended for a future deposit, subject to specified conditions.

5. What is the underlying intellectual purpose?
I think the most revealing word in the title is pluriform.

Sharks is trying to construct an archive in which different intellectual traditions can coexist without being forced into one historical or linguistic framework.

Consider the implications of the arrangement:

Horace and Ovid are preserved in Latin, rather than represented only through English translations.

Indian Sanskrit poetics is placed alongside classical European poetics.

Chinese literary theory is represented through its own primary texts.

Hebrew, Arabic, Persian, Japanese, and Korean traditions receive their own textual presence.

Modern experimental writing is situated alongside much older theories of literary composition.

Buddhist texts are represented across multiple canonical languages.

The result is a comparative intellectual infrastructure.

The archive makes it possible to investigate questions such as:

How do different civilizations understand what poetry is?

What constitutes literary technique?

How do traditions understand the relationship between language and thought?

How do different cultures conceptualize artistic creation?

What happens when modern experimental writing is considered alongside ancient literary theory?

The juxtaposition of T. S. Eliot's Tradition and the Individual Talent with Johannes Sigil's Tradition and the Individual Seismograph is particularly revealing. The document explicitly places these essays together because Sigil's essay develops Eliot's historical sense into a different conception of the poet's relationship with tradition.

This demonstrates that the archive is not simply accumulating texts. It is also making deliberate intellectual connections.

6. What are its limitations?
The document is quite candid about incompleteness.

Some important qualifications:

Not every tradition is represented comprehensively.

Some texts are available only as OCR, which can contain transcription errors.

Some editions are incompletely identified or have explicitly unverified edition-level status.

Some contemporary and copyrighted works are listed but not deposited.

The Tibetan collection contains eight selected volumes, not the entire Derge Kangyur.

The Chinese, Japanese, and Korean Buddhist collections are selections, not complete national or regional canons.

The Pali collection is described as complete in its specified root-text scope, but excludes translations.

The archive also preserves licensing distinctions rather than treating every file as freely reusable. For example, the Sanskrit GRETIL material carries its NonCommercial licensing conditions.

These qualifications are important because they distinguish the project's actual achievements from its ambitions.

8. My overall reading
I would characterize EA-CORPORA-29 as an attempt to build a reproducible, provenance-conscious, multilingual library of primary intellectual materials.

Its significance lies in three connected ideas:

1. Plurality: Literary and philosophical traditions should be represented in their own languages and historical contexts, rather than subordinated to a single literary canon.

2. Documentary accountability: Every deposited work should have a traceable source, edition, licence, and record of how its digital text was produced.

3. Archival honesty: An unavailable work should be recorded as unavailable, not made to appear present through a bibliographic reference alone.

There is also a deeper conceptual distinction here: the project treats an archive as something that must document not only what it contains, but also how it came to contain it, what it excludes, and what remains to be done.

In short: EA-CORPORA-29 is both a collection of texts and a technical method for assembling, preserving, verifying, and relating those texts. Its ambition is to make a much wider range of human literary and intellectual traditions available as inspectable primary-source material.
