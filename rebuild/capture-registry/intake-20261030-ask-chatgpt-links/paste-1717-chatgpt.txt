The central argument of Lee Sharks and Jack Feist’s “Belief & Technique for Telepathic Prose” is that writing cannot be governed by a stable set of commandments. Artistic practice requires technical discipline, spontaneity, literary inheritance, and the capacity to question the authority of all four.

But the critical edition makes a more interesting claim: the piece does not merely parody writing advice. It puts competing theories of composition into conflict, demonstrates their consequences, and attempts to transform them into a different kind of practice.

The distinction matters. The edition explicitly rejects the familiar description of the work as simply a comic parody of Jack Kerouac. Kerouac supplies the framework, but the piece draws on a much wider history of literary instruction. Its argument is developed through contradiction, self-implication, and what the editor calls sublation: negating a rule as an absolute command while preserving whatever useful operation it contains.

1. The central argument: composition requires both spontaneity and technique
This is the most important positive proposition, developed especially in items 10–15.

The poem sets two apparently incompatible approaches against each other:

Craftsmanship and revision: the writer must choose words precisely, edit, organize, and exercise technical control.

Spontaneity and liberation: the writer must escape the censoring conscious mind and allow unconscious material to emerge without inhibition.

Neither approach works as an absolute.

Item 12 is the nightmare of craftsmanship taken to its logical extreme. The writer becomes a seamstress who must place every stitch perfectly. The process escalates into elaborate rituals, years of isolation, the construction of an entire typeface from raw materials, and ultimately the loss of letters from the alphabet itself.

The final passage of item 12 is particularly important: because the writer has failed to manufacture certain letters, the manifesto's own language becomes unable to spell them. The sentence about creating a better world is visibly damaged by the conditions of its production.

The edition reads this as more than a joke about perfectionism:

The desire for complete control over inscription eventually controls the writer.

The apparatus of writing acquires authority over the person using it.

The pursuit of the perfect word ends by diminishing what can be written.

Item 13 presents the opposite nightmare. The writer abandons the internal editor, commits to uninterrupted spontaneous production, and deliberately produces increasingly terrible writing. The result is absurdity: pancakes, alarm clocks, and toast.

The point is not that spontaneity is worthless. It is that spontaneity, turned into an absolute command, becomes another form of mechanical obedience.

Item 15 supplies the constructive alternative. It combines the competing literary commandments—Pound's renewal, Quiller-Couch's revision, Trungpa's spontaneity, Twain's precision, and Wordsworth's emotional overflow—and gives them a new relationship.

Its governing operation is to turn extremes against each other:

Generate the spontaneous material.

Capture what is genuinely alive in it.

Apply technical knowledge and self-discipline.

Revise so that the original material acquires precision and communicative force.

The edition calls this the poem's positive practice. The spontaneous discovery is the gem; technique cuts it so that it can glimmer.

This is not a compromise in which spontaneity and craftsmanship each surrender half their claims. They become successive, mutually necessary operations.

2. Writing advice becomes dangerous when it becomes law
A second argument concerns the authority of literary instruction itself.

The poem is full of recognizable commandments:

Advocate for your work.

Seek approval.

Never professionalize writing.

Make your life your poem.

Get an education.

Destroy your education.

Write constantly.

Develop a consistent voice.

Believe in destiny.

Start again when you fail.

The instructions are deliberately contradictory. But the contradiction is not simply an inconsistency in the poem.

The edition calls this operation collision: each precept is allowed to make its strongest possible claim, then confronted with another precept and with the practical consequences of obeying it.

For example:

Item 3 commands the writer to seek constant approval.

Item 23 eventually distinguishes the writer's intrinsic achievement from the pursuit of external recognition.

Item 18 recommends a consistent authorial voice.

Items 20–22 expose the absurdity of turning consistency into permanent repetition.

The deeper criticism is directed at the idea that a writer can become successful by following a universal formula.

Even apparently sensible advice becomes destructive when treated as an unconditional obligation. The poem's violence and slapstick make this visible: metaphors such as murdering one's darlings become literal bodily injuries.

The edition calls this literalization. It argues that the injuries measure the demands that literary commandments place on a writer's life.

3. The writer must distinguish artistic achievement from the pursuit of recognition
This is arguably the poem's most emotionally serious argument.

Items 2 and 3 are concerned with external validation. The writer aggressively demands that the world acknowledge the poems, then compulsively seeks approval.

Item 18 introduces another version of the problem: the writer's identity becomes a recognizable personal brand. The writer is compared to a car salesman. The deciding factor in the transaction is ultimately the seller's personality rather than the merits of the product or the needs of the buyer.

The joke is directed at the commercialization of literary identity.

Items 20 and 21 then repeat exactly the same instruction about consistency of voice. In the critical edition's canonical text, they are deliberately numbered as two separate items. The repetition enacts the monotonous consistency it prescribes.

Item 22 reverses the instruction by invoking Emerson's hobgoblin of consistency.

But the most consequential development comes in item 23.

The poem invokes two different accounts of literary immortality:

The Iliad offers the promise of imperishable fame, exemplified by Achilles.

The Odyssey demonstrates the cost of pursuing the prize, through Odysseus's extraordinary suffering and the destruction of the suitors who covet what is not theirs.

The suitors want the reward without paying its price.

The edition interprets this as a distinction between the achievement that genuinely belongs to the writer and the pursuit of recognition that may never belong to them.

The poem then quotes Emily Dickinson's letter to Thomas Wentworth Higginson of 7 June 1862. Dickinson resists his suggestion that she delay publication, explaining that fame, if it belongs to her, cannot be escaped, and if it does not, pursuing it would waste her life.

This is a crucial distinction:

The writer should pursue the work, not chase the approval that supposedly confers artistic worth.

The edition connects Dickinson's Barefoot Rank to the poem's idea of what is genuinely the writer's own: a rank that external recognition cannot bestow or remove.

4. The work must be willing to question its own authority
This is where the poem becomes more than a collection of contradictory writing advice.

Item 23 tells the reader to stop writing. It questions whether the reader is actually capable of becoming a writer.

The critical edition reads this as a challenge rather than simply an expression of pessimism.

The reader has been taken through the poem's competing demands, including the exhausting commitment to artistic ambition. Now the speaker asks whether the reader really wants the prize badly enough.

Then item 24 withdraws the authority of the entire list:

Therefore, take these Sturm und Drang commandments to the trash heap.

But it does not discard everything the poem has taught.

It returns to the opening instruction:

Compose real poems telepathically, with mind control powers, inside your glorious brain.

The return is significant because the opening sentence now has a different meaning. The reader has encountered the consequences of craftsmanship, spontaneity, professionalization, self-promotion, consistency, ambition, and the pursuit of fame.

The initial instruction has survived, but the reader has changed.

The critical edition calls this sublation. Its three movements are:

Negation: the commandments lose their authority as universal laws.

Preservation: useful operations survive, especially the relationship between spontaneous generation and technical revision.

Transformation: the surviving practice is returned to the reader in the form of the original telepathic premise.

This is why the edition argues that the poem does not simply cancel itself. It transforms itself.

5. Telepathy is both the ideal of composition and the poem's central paradox
The title is not merely a comic substitution of telepathic for modern.

The edition traces the word to Kerouac's Essentials of Spontaneous Prose, where spontaneous writing is associated with producing a telepathic shock in the reader.

Sharks and Feist take that idea literally.

In item 1, the poem is to be composed inside the brain, with no apparent need for writing or transmission through ordinary material means.

The edition identifies an important paradox:

The poem is a written, published artifact.

It instructs its reader to compose without writing.

Reading the instruction places it inside the reader's mind.

The supposedly impossible telepathic communication therefore happens, in a limited but demonstrable sense, through the act of reading.

The edition connects this to item 13, which says that the reader is always and only the writer.

Its interpretation is that a real poem originates in the mind before and independently of its recognition by the literary marketplace.

The telepathic premise is thus simultaneously an imaginative ideal, a theory of artistic autonomy, and a self-referential joke about what poems actually do.

7. The argument is performed by the poem's formal structure
The edition identifies several important formal operations.

Collision: Opposing instructions are brought together and tested against one another.

Literalization: Figurative literary advice becomes physical action and injury.

Enactment: The writing actually performs what its instructions describe.

Repetition: Items 20 and 21 reproduce the same text, demonstrating the tyranny of consistency.

Recursion: Item 24 returns the reader to item 1, completing the cycle.

Sublation: The poem withdraws the authority of its commandments while retaining their useful operations.

These are not decorative features. They are the means by which the poem makes its argument.

The duplicated items are particularly interesting. The edition establishes that the canonical book version intentionally numbers the identical passages 20 and 21. The blog version prints both as item 20, but the author's 2026 ruling confirms that the two-item numbering is intended.

This matters because the repetition is part of the composition, not an accidental editorial error.

The poem's structure itself becomes an experiment in what it means to repeat, revise, and transform a piece of writing.

8. Why the critical edition considers it a significant ars poetica
The edition makes a fairly ambitious literary-critical claim: that, item against item, Belief & Technique for Telepathic Prose is a more extensively self-testing ars poetica than Kerouac's Belief & Technique for Modern Prose.

It uses six criteria:

Statement: What theory of composition does the work propose?

Enactment: Does the work actually perform its own instructions?

Voices: What does its engagement with other writers make possible?

Representation: How does it represent competing theories of composition?

Knowledge of genre: Does it understand the conventions and history of the manifesto and ars poetica?

Self-testing: Does it subject its own principles and authority to examination?

The edition argues that Kerouac's list holds its tensions together as aspects of a single writing practice, whereas Sharks and Feist's poem stages those tensions, pushes them to their consequences, and makes the reader reconsider what survives.

Importantly, the editor does not claim that the poem is superior to Kerouac's entire literary output. The comparison is explicitly restricted to the two works as statements of poetics.

My overall reading
I think the strongest way to understand the work is as an argument about the relationship between artistic freedom and artistic responsibility.

It makes three interconnected claims:

First, no external authority can provide a complete method for making art. Not the workshop, not the literary canon, not the marketplace, not the avant-garde, and not even the manifesto itself.

Second, freedom does not mean the absence of technique. Spontaneity without discrimination becomes nonsense; technique without spontaneity becomes sterile ritual. The artist needs both, in the right sequence and relationship.

Third, artistic achievement must be distinguished from the social machinery that claims to recognize it. Publication, fame, professional identity, approval, and consistency can all become substitutes for the actual work.

The final return to telepathic composition brings these claims together. The poem has spent its length dismantling the machinery of literary authority, only to return to an impossible, almost childlike proposition: make the poem in your mind.

But this is no longer an innocent proposition. It has been tested against the entire tradition of literary advice.

The resulting position is not anti-intellectual, anti-craft, or simply anti-institutional. It is an argument for absorbing the tradition without becoming its servant.

The deepest commandment of the poem is therefore that the writer must learn from every rule without allowing any rule to become sovereign.
