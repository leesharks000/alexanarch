2. How does the archive actually work?
The document describes a reproducible archival pipeline, rather than merely a collection of links or a bibliography.

Its basic operation is:

1. Identify the works

Select works, editions, languages and traditions for each seat.

2. Fetch the source material

Retrieve texts from repositories such as Perseus, Wikisource, GRETIL, Project Gutenberg, Aozora Bunko and the Internet Archive.

3. Preserve the originals

Store source files, editions, provenance and relevant source identifiers.

4. Normalize computationally

Generate consistent, searchable text files from the original formats.

5. Verify and document

Check textual locations, record source metadata and generate SHA-256 manifests.

6. Deposit the completed seats

Package the eleven units into the Alexanarch dataset.

There are several important technical details.

A. Original and normalized text are separate.

The archive retains the original source material under original/ and creates derived text under text/.

For instance:

TEI documents are reduced into line-oriented text.

Wikisource pages are processed to remove navigation and editing elements.

Aozora XHTML is processed while retaining ruby readings.

Internet Archive OCR is preserved as OCR rather than silently treated as an authoritative transcription.

Text is not manually corrected during normalization.

This last point is significant. The archive explicitly says that no text was corrected by hand. The normalized text is a reproducible derivative, not a claim that the underlying transcription is error-free.

B. Each seat has its own provenance record.

The document identifies source.json and MANIFEST.sha256 as important files.

source.json records the edition, origin, licensing, normalization method and verified textual locations.

MANIFEST.sha256 records file hashes, allowing the integrity of the archived files to be checked.

C. Verification is tied to actual passages.

The document supplies verified textual loci rather than simply claiming that a work was downloaded.

For example:

Horace: Ars Poetica 1, beginning with Humano capiti cervicem equinam.

Ovid: Metamorphoses 15.871, Iamque opus exegi.

Bharata: the rasa-sūtra in chapter six of the Nāṭyaśāstra.

The Heart Sūtra: its locus identified through the Sanskrit term śūnyatā.

These markers make it possible to check whether the intended work is actually represented in the archive.

3. The special role of Telepathic Prose
Seat 29/07 is arguably the conceptual center of the deposit.

It collects the development of Belief & Technique for Telepathic Prose, including:

The original 2014 drafts, in multiple states.

The version submitted to Word Riot.

The published version in Pearl and Other Poems (2014).

The corresponding versions published on the Mind Control Poems blog.

Make It Human.

A Telepathicist Manifesto, co-written with John Johnson.

The critical edition deposited immediately before this one, #1715, AXN:0A9A.

Why preserve all these versions?

Because the archive is interested not only in the finished literary work, but in its development: how the theory changes, how it is expressed in different publications, and how its constituent ideas relate to other theories of literary creation.

The deposit explicitly identifies a verified passage from Belief & Technique for Telepathic Prose:

Compose real poems telepathically, with mind control powers, inside your glorious brain.

It also identifies the manifesto's central proposition:

Telepathicism is about having thoughts, telepathically.

There is an important interpretive distinction here: telepathic prose is the name and theoretical framework of Sharks's literary project. The deposit documents its texts and intellectual development. It does not demonstrate that telepathy or mind control is an experimentally established capability, nor does its archival software implement such a capability.

The connection to Kerouac is deliberate. Kerouac's Essentials of Spontaneous Prose uses the term telepathic to describe the effect of spontaneous prose on its reader. Sharks's project takes that literary concept and develops it into its own ars poetica.

The archive thus places its own literary theory alongside the much longer historical tradition of theories about how poetry works.

4. Why include the Buddhist canons?
The Buddhist component is not incidental or decorative. It substantially changes the character of the deposit.

The three principal textual layers are:

Pali: The complete Tipiṭaka, including the Sutta, Vinaya and Abhidhamma collections.

Sanskrit: Thirty-two Buddhist works, including fourteen Mahāyāna sūtras and twelve śāstras.

East Asian and Tibetan traditions: Chinese, Japanese and Korean materials, alongside Tibetan texts from the Derge Kangyur.

The most interesting archival decision is the cross-language representation of the same Buddhist scriptures.

The document specifically identifies the Lotus, Diamond, Heart and Vimalakīrti sūtras as works represented across Sanskrit, Chinese and Tibetan.

This creates an opportunity for comparative textual research: the same religious and philosophical traditions can be approached through different linguistic witnesses rather than through a single translated version.

There are also explicit limitations. Only eight of the 103 Derge Kangyur volumes are included; the remaining 95 are queued. The entire Taishō Chinese canon is also not included, because the document identifies its licensing as requiring a separate decision.

6. What is the larger intellectual significance?
I would distinguish three levels of significance.

First: a comparative history of poetics.

The collection brings together different answers to questions such as:

What makes language poetic?

What is the relationship between sound, meaning and form?

How does a poet create?

What is the relationship between tradition and innovation?

Is poetry an art, a technique, a form of knowledge or a mode of perception?

Putting Bharata, Lu Ji, Dante, Bialik, Poe, Wordsworth, Mayakovsky and Eliot in the same research environment makes those questions available for cross-cultural comparison.

Second: an archive of literary experimentation.

The avant-garde materials and telepathic-prose materials introduce a different concern: not just what poetry is, but what happens when writers deliberately change the methods by which literature is produced.

The archive preserves both historical experiments and Sharks's own contribution to that ongoing conversation.

Third: a multilingual textual infrastructure.

The Buddhist component extends the project beyond literary criticism into religious, philosophical and textual history.

It also creates a technical foundation for comparing different textual traditions, editions and languages.

8. My overall reading
The most revealing feature of EA-CORPORA-29 is its architecture.

It begins with a question about literary creation, represented by Horace's Ars Poetica and the preceding Belief & Technique for Telepathic Prose critical edition.

It expands that question across twenty traditions of poetics, then across the avant-garde's experiments in literary method. Finally, it places the resulting literary inquiry alongside Buddhist philosophical and canonical traditions in their original languages.

The result is a kind of comparative infrastructure for thinking about how human beings make, transmit, theorize and preserve language.

Its title, The Twenty-Ninth Seating, the Pluriform, describes both its archival position and its intellectual ambition: each tradition gets a place, without requiring all traditions to conform to one model.

One final point: the project is intentionally unfinished. Its wanted seats, queued texts, copyright opening dates and future acquisition plans make incompleteness part of its documented structure rather than something concealed behind a claim of comprehensiveness.
