1. The paper's argument, reconstructed from the text
I have now located the full deposited text, not merely its abstract. It is the 27 October 2026 document, EA-SEM-FEUDALISM-03 v1.0, at deposit #1714. The following reconstruction draws on its actual sections and arguments. 

Alexanarch

The paper makes a more specific argument than the general criticism that AI companies take credit for other people's work. Its subject is the institutional allocation of recognition after AI has already transformed the provenance of knowledge.

It identifies four connected operations.

1. Reclassification: compositions become raw research data
OpenAI's October 26 publishing plan describes model-generated mathematical and scientific outputs as "raw research data." Sharks argues that this description changes the institutional status of the work.

A mathematical manuscript is already a composition: it has a problem, a statement, a proof, a bibliography, and a history of intellectual dependencies. Calling it raw data moves the burden of interpretation and exposition to the people who subsequently work on it.

The resulting distinction is:

The producer supplies the material.

The shepherds interpret it.

The shepherds acquire leadership of the first recognized account of the result.

Their existing institutional standing helps determine who gets that opportunity.

Sharks emphasizes that this is not necessarily an objection to interpretation by mathematicians. It is an objection to the way interpretive authority is allocated.

2. Enfeoffment: shepherds receive positions in exchange for service
This is the paper's central feudal analogy.

The company appoints external experts as shepherds, giving them a privileged position in the conversion of model outputs into recognized scientific knowledge.

Sharks maps the arrangement onto feudal relations:

Feudal category

Discovery-layer equivalent

Lord

Model-owning company

Fief

Problem space and first access to results

Vassal

Appointed shepherd

Service

Converting outputs into scholarly knowledge

Reputation rent

The shepherd's standing, lent to the company

Benefice

Institutional position in the intellectual field

The crucial idea is that the shepherd lends the company something it cannot manufacture by itself: independent academic reputation.

The paper draws particularly on the Advisory Group on Mathematics and Artificial Intelligence, which describes designated mathematicians' names as effectively vouching for the release and shielding the company from criticism.

There are two different advantages here:

Interpretive priority: shepherds become leaders of the first recognized scholarly account, even if everyone can access the files.

Exclusive access: where outputs are withheld, as in the physics arrangement, shepherds additionally receive access unavailable to the wider field.

Sharks is careful to distinguish the two. Making everything public would remove the exclusive-access advantage, but would not necessarily remove the appointment advantage.

3. Disclaimer: refusing academic credit does not eliminate institutional extraction
OpenAI's statement that it does not seek academic credit is treated as insufficient.

Sharks argues that refusing formal authorship is not the same as relinquishing control over recognition.

The company still:

Selects the problems.

Controls the producing model and important information about its operation.

Determines the initial shepherds.

Shapes the timing and conditions of publication.

Receives reputational benefits when the outputs become recognized scientific results.

The company can therefore disclaim the discovery while retaining control over the institutional process through which the discovery becomes meaningful.

This is why the paper distinguishes acknowledgment, restitution, and appointment. Naming an intellectual dependency is not the same as returning recognition to the contributor. Nor is appointing an established academic an adequate substitute for doing so.

4. Funding: financing downstream work does not settle the question of ownership or credit
The paper also examines OpenAI's funding arrangements with the Simons Foundation, including the $5 million contribution to a broader mathematics-and-AI initiative.

Sharks acknowledges that this funding supports real scientific activity. His objection is structural: financing the conversion of outputs into human knowledge does not itself establish that the underlying allocation of recognition is fair.

The downstream scholarly community can be funded, supported, and apparently empowered while still operating within a structure whose agenda, initial access, and recognition channels are controlled by the producer.

These four moves together constitute the operation Sharks calls meaning feudalism at the discovery layer.

2. The deeper argument: provenance slush and the unpositioned contributor
Sections 4 and 6 contain what I think is the paper's most consequential theoretical move.

Provenance slush
Sharks distinguishes two kinds of intellectual dependency:

Visible intellectual dependence: a result extends a theorem, adapts a method, answers a conjecture, or cites a predecessor.

Training dependence: the model may have absorbed published papers, preprints, lectures, conversations, drafts, and other work that cannot subsequently be traced to a particular output.

The second kind is what he calls provenance slush.

The knowledge enters the training pipeline with its individual attachments dissolved. The resulting model can produce compositions that draw on this accumulated intellectual capital without preserving a usable account of whose work contributed to them.

This creates a fundamental problem: the people who can be identified through conventional scholarly records are disproportionately those who already occupy recognized positions.

Position as institutional commodity form
This is the paper's most distinctive conceptual contribution.

Sharks defines position as a recognized place in a chain of intellectual development: for example, being the established holder of a research program, the recognized author on a problem, or the name associated with a particular line of work.

Position functions as an institutional commodity because it can be:

Held and accumulated.

Recognized by disciplinary institutions.

Exchanged for appointments and opportunities.

Used to acquire further standing.

The problem is that position becomes a substitute for actual provenance.

Suppose a model produces a result drawing on the work of several people, including researchers without established institutional standing. The company appoints an established mathematician to shepherd the result.

That mathematician may be an excellent interpreter. But the appointment does not establish that they are the person whose work the model drew upon.

The paper calls this a substitution:

Creditor: the original contributor, whose work entered the training pipeline.

Substitute: the established scholar appointed to interpret the output.

Extraction: the substitute receives work, leadership and recognition, while the original contributor remains unrecognized.

This is why Sharks insists that acknowledgment, restitution and appointment are three different operations. Appointing someone to interpret a result does not repay the intellectual debt incurred in producing it.

3. The semantic-economic circuit
Section 6 puts the argument into a broader economic model.

The circuit of semantic capital
Liquidation

The accumulated work of the research community enters model training and loses its individual provenance.

Composition

The model produces new mathematical and scientific statements, proofs and bibliographies.

Recapitalization

The outputs are reclassified as raw data and passed to appointed shepherds. Their institutional standing converts them into recognized knowledge.

Rent

The company retains the reputational and commercial benefits of being the supplier of the results and controller of the infrastructure.

Exhaustion

The research community loses attribution, open circulation, research opportunities and incentives to contribute.

Sharks's terminology, §§6.1–6.5, drawing on Semantic Infrastructure (#623).

The important point is that this is not simply an accusation of unfair payment.

It is a claim about the reproduction of the knowledge economy.

The incentive argument
Sharks invokes Robert Merton's norm of scientific communism, Hagstrom's account of the exchange between scientific contribution and recognition, and the priority-rule literature, including Dasgupta and David, Strevens and Heesen.

His argument is that scientific knowledge-sharing depends partly on a reciprocal arrangement:

Researchers contribute findings to a common intellectual inheritance.

The community recognizes their contributions.

Recognition provides an incentive to continue contributing and sharing.

A training pipeline breaks the connection between contribution and recognition.

The producer's work can be absorbed without returning attribution to its source. Researchers consequently have reason to withhold unfinished work, unpublished ideas and informal exchanges.

The asymmetry is particularly important:

The company retains its incentive to produce better results, while the distributed research community loses some of its incentives to produce and share the knowledge on which the system depends.

This is the meaning of Sharks's deliberately strong statement that the arrangement "slushes out every incentive for high quality knowledge production."

He does not mean that every researcher will stop working. He means that the institutional reward structure ceases to reward the distributed production of knowledge in the way that it previously did.

4. The operator and the instance also disappear
Another important part of the paper is that Sharks does not limit the provenance problem to the original researchers whose work was absorbed.

He identifies four nodes at which recognition is lost or misallocated.

Node

What disappears

Original contributor

Attribution and recognition for intellectual contributions

Human operator

Credit for posing problems, selecting attempts, judging proofs and editing

Model instance

A specific, auditable record of which system and run produced the result

Shepherd

Recognition becomes attached to existing institutional position rather than necessarily to the work performed

The discussion of the operator draws on Steven Shapin's The Invisible Technician (1989).

Shapin recovered the scientific assistants whose experimental work was historically credited to the gentleman under whose name the results were reported.

Sharks argues that the corporate author line reproduces this arrangement: the institution occupies the position of the gentleman, while the people performing the intellectual work remain unidentified.

He also makes an interesting argument about crediting model instances.

He wants the specific model version, configuration and run or session identifier recorded, with its production record retained and available for inspection.

This is not merely a demand to identify AI as a tool. It is a demand for reproducibility and the ability to compare successful and unsuccessful instances.

The three withdrawn manuscripts following a sign error make this particularly relevant.

5. The disciplinary model-collapse argument
Section 8 extends the analysis beyond attribution into the future direction of scientific inquiry.

Sharks borrows the concept of disciplinary model collapse from his companion paper, The Particle.

The proposed mechanism is:

A research field produces knowledge.

That knowledge trains a model.

The model produces results based on the field's previous work.

The company selects which problems to pose and which outputs to release.

The research community organizes new work around those outputs.

The next generation of research becomes increasingly conditioned by the previous generation of model outputs.

The danger is that the field gradually starts responding to its own knowledge as filtered through the model and the company's problem-selection process.

Sharks is careful here: he explicitly says the outcome is open and conditional. A novel proof might generate entirely new questions rather than narrow inquiry.

He identifies observable warning signs:

Problems outside the company's chosen problem set receive less attention.

Slow exposition loses ground to rapid announcements.

Research programs unrelated to released results lose visibility.

Young researchers whose programs have been overtaken suffer first.

Researchers become less willing to circulate unfinished work.

This is a prediction about the distribution of intellectual attention, not a claim that scientific progress necessarily stops.

6. The proposed response: refusal, provenance and the commons
The paper's final major section is not just a list of reforms. It proposes a different institutional arrangement.

A. Preserve provenance through training
Sharks wants the training pipeline to preserve source records, dependencies and attribution information.

He discusses:

Recording sources at ingestion.

Retaining provenance information with the training set.

Publishing problem sets, prompts and model versions.

Using influence-function methods to investigate which training examples affect outputs.

Maintaining independent, dated and hashed records of prior work.

He acknowledges that influence functions do not recover authorship. They provide partial tracing, not a complete solution.

B. Credit operators and model instances
The human operator should be named and credited for their actual responsibilities.

The specific model instance should also be recorded, with enough information to audit and compare its contribution.

C. Separate shepherding from credit
Interpretive work should receive recognition for what it actually contributes.

But appointment as a shepherd should not itself become evidence of intellectual priority or provenance.

D. Boycott the shepherd role
Explicit political demand of the paper
This is one of the strongest and most controversial recommendations.

Sharks argues for a preemptive boycott of company-appointed shepherds.

His reasoning is that the arrangement needs independently accumulated academic reputation. The company cannot generate that reputation itself; it needs scholars to lend it their standing.

Accepting the role therefore supplies something essential to the arrangement.

He specifies four things the boycott opposes:

Accepting company-designated interpretive leadership.

Accepting access ahead of the wider research community.

Lending one's name to an announcement before independent verification.

Accepting arrangements that exclude other contributors from the work or its credit.

He also distinguishes preemptive refusal from withdrawal after the fact. Once an appointment has conferred first standing, a later refusal cannot completely undo the original allocation.

E. If provenance cannot be preserved, the output belongs to the commons
This is the paper's strongest ownership claim.

Sharks argues that where the company cannot account for the intellectual contributions incorporated into a model, its claim to exclusive control over the resulting outputs should be limited.

His proposed commons includes several distinct rights:

Access to results, production records, prompts and problem sets.

Participation in governance over release timing and interpretive leadership.

Inspection of training records.

Access to model capacity, potentially including weights.

A claim on financial and reputational returns.

He draws on Elinor Ostrom's work on knowledge commons.

But there is an additional qualification that matters: a commons cannot simply be administered by the same established institutions that already allocate academic standing. Otherwise, the exclusion could be reproduced under collective ownership.

The commons must be capable of hearing independent contributors, including those without affiliation, appointment or established reputation.

That requires a public procedure for presenting dated evidence of prior work, contesting attribution and recording corrections.

There are also important limitations acknowledged by the author.

The original OpenAI post of 26 October could not be retrieved by Sharks. Its wording was taken from Gizmodo's reporting.

The paper does not establish that every mathematical result was derived from identifiable, unpublished human contributions.

It distinguishes intellectual dependence from actual training-data dependence.

It does not claim that every shepherd is undeserving or that their interpretive work lacks value.

It explicitly acknowledges that the mathematical release did provide genuine public access and that funding supported real scholarly work.

The distinction between allocation and reattribution is especially important. Sharks considers the allocation of opportunity and standing documented, but does not claim to have proven the precise upstream authorship of every result.

8. My synthesis: what is the paper really arguing?
I would summarize its argument in five propositions:

1. Knowledge production is collective, but knowledge recognition is institutional. A model can absorb collective intellectual labor without preserving the identities of its contributors.

2. When provenance disappears, position fills the vacuum. The people who already possess institutional standing become the people best positioned to receive recognition for subsequent developments.

3. AI companies can disclaim academic authorship while retaining control over semantic capital. They choose the problems, own the infrastructure, appoint interpreters and benefit from the resulting authority.

4. This arrangement threatens the reproduction of the knowledge commons. It weakens the incentives for researchers to share and potentially narrows the direction of future research.

5. The remedy must change who governs discovery. Better acknowledgments alone are insufficient. Sharks calls for persistent provenance, operator and instance credit, refusal of the shepherd role, and commons governance where provenance cannot be recovered.

The most revealing sentence in the paper may be its final formulation:

"The lord keeps the ledger, in its own unit, and appoints the vassals who will vouch for what it records."

That sentence connects all three levels of the argument: control over measurement, control over recognition, and control over the institutional record.

One final interpretive distinction: Sharks is not arguing that AI-generated mathematics is inherently illegitimate, that mathematicians should refuse to work with AI, or that model outputs cannot constitute genuine discoveries. His target is the governance arrangement through which collective intellectual inheritance is converted into privately controlled infrastructure and institutionally allocated recognition.

That makes this a critique of the political economy of scientific discovery, rather than simply a critique of AI authorship or academic plagiarism.
