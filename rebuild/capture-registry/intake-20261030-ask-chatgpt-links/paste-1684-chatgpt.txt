What is the Prediction Index of the Crimson Hexagonal Archive?
EA-PREDICTION-INDEX-01 is a large-scale, source-quoted register of predictions, hypotheses, proposed experiments, historical arguments, and priority claims made throughout the Crimson Hexagonal Archive.

It was authored by Lee Sharks and deposited in Alexanarch as deposit #1684 (AXN:06FA) on 18 October 2026.

Its central purpose is to make the archive's predictive content identifiable, traceable, organized, and eventually testable.

It is not simply a list of things the author thinks will happen. It is an attempt to extract the archive's claims about the future, the behavior of systems, the past, and its own intellectual priority, while preserving the original wording and the evidence needed to evaluate each claim.

2. How the index is constructed
The document describes a multi-stage extraction and classification process.

Corpus-wide reading

The initial sweep covered 1,616 deposits and 10,050,025 words. Each deposit's seated text was read from beginning to end, rather than merely searched for prediction-related keywords.

The process also incorporated the two large datasets' own-voice fields, subsequently repaired text, and attachments.

Identification of prediction-bearing passages

Readers extracted passages and assigned them shapes A through H:

A: registered forecast.

B: forecast embedded in prose.

C: confirmation record.

D: priority record.

E: speculative specification.

F: unbuilt test.

G: standing claim.

H: historical thesis.

Each find retained its verbatim quotation, source location, line numbers, relevant time window, and falsifier where available.

Mechanical verification

Every quotation and line number was checked against the source text by script.

The document reports 6,939 finds verified at publication.

Consolidation

Multiple passages expressing the same claim were brought together.

The system distinguishes three relationships:

Same: duplicate expressions of one claim, merged.

States: a later formulation that changes or revises an earlier claim.

Series: separate claims belonging to one programme, battery, or template, linked without merging.

Topical organization

Claims were grouped into subject pages. The process moved from 124 topical groups through graph-based communities and subsequent splitting and reconciliation, ending with 194 pages.

Publication

Each claim received a stable identifier and route. Subject pages, JSON datasets, evidence records, and build metadata make the index navigable and machine-readable.

An important numerical distinction: the 6,939 finds are not 6,939 independent claims. Consolidation produces 4,394 claims. The document explains how duplicate passages, later revisions, and related but distinct claims are handled.

3. The four-date system
One of the document's most consequential methodological decisions is that it refuses to treat every date associated with a document as the date on which its claims were originally made.

Every statement can carry four distinct dates:

Date

What it means

Stated

When the quoted words were written

Attested

When the statement is publicly evidenced

Interpreted

When a later statement interprets an earlier event or text

Indexed

When the index first recorded the passage

Why is this important?

Imagine a document deposited in 2026 that contains a foreword discussing correspondence from 2014.

If the index simply uses the deposit date, it can obscure the difference between:

What was actually written in 2014.

What was interpreted or asserted in 2026.

When the material became publicly attested.

The index therefore registers a claim at its earliest stated statement, while retaining later formulations and their dates.

The document reports that 731 of its 6,305 statements were dated using information supplied by the text itself. It also says that 334 claims changed their registration date as a consequence of the dating revisions.

This is particularly important for any attempt to establish whether something was genuinely predicted in advance.

4. How predictions are evaluated
The index does not use a single universal success/failure test.

It defines different resolution regimes.

A. Ordinary forecasts
These are evaluated against dated observations.

If a forecast specifies a time window, the observation must fall within that window.

B. Standing claims
These are tested against falsifiers.

A falsifier is the condition that would demonstrate that the assertion is wrong.

The index distinguishes:

The falsification condition explicitly supplied by the original author.

The condition inferred by a reader from the wording of the claim.

That distinction prevents a reader-created interpretation from being misrepresented as the author's original test.

D. Historical theses
These are evaluated using evidence about the past: documentary, philological, archaeological, or corpus-based.

E. Priority records
These require comparison of dated public witnesses.

A claim that the archive originated an idea is not established merely because the archive contains a record asserting priority.

The index records the claim and the evidence needed to investigate it.

6. How reliable does the document say its own methodology is?
The document includes an explicit recall audit.

The document is also unusually explicit about its limitations.

It identifies several unresolved issues:

Subject grouping: 765 claims are classified as residue, meaning they appear on pages where they do not properly belong. These occur across 142 pages. The document identifies regrouping as necessary.

Corpus limitations: Images, binary files, machine-generated data rows, and other specified materials were excluded.

Dating uncertainty: Some statements cannot be dated more precisely than their deposit's attestation.

Incomplete validation: The recall audit uses a sample of 40 deposits, not the entire corpus.

Historical and priority claims remain open: Recording a thesis or priority claim does not establish its truth.

There is another important point about authorship and methodology.

The document explicitly identifies the work as AI-assisted. TACHYON (Claude) and parallel Claude readers conducted the sweep, second reading, triage, grouping, annotation, description, and recall work under Lee Sharks's direction through MANUS. A different Claude model performed the second-reading audit. ChatGPT and Kimi reviewed the draft description, and Lee Sharks adopted revisions.

Thus, it is not presented as a conventional manually compiled bibliography. It is a human-directed, AI-assisted extraction and documentation project with explicit provenance and verification procedures.

7. What is the larger significance of this project?
I think the most important feature of EA-PREDICTION-INDEX-01 is its attempt to turn an archive into something that can be evaluated prospectively and retrospectively.

There are three particularly significant consequences.

First: it makes predictions accountable.

A prediction buried in a ten-million-word archive can be difficult to find, date, compare, or evaluate. The index extracts it, preserves its wording, establishes its earliest known statement, and identifies how it could be tested.

Second: it distinguishes prediction from other forms of intellectual production.

A design specification, a historical thesis, a scenario, a falsifiable systems claim, and an empirical forecast are different kinds of statements. The index tries to prevent them from being collapsed into a simplistic binary of correct versus incorrect.

Third: it creates infrastructure for longitudinal testing.

Because claims have stable identifiers, dated statements, associated evidence, and explicit resolution conditions, they can be revisited as new observations become available.

This is especially important for the 950 standing claims and 1,029 unbuilt tests. They represent an attempt to make theoretical assertions and proposed experiments available for subsequent measurement rather than leaving them as isolated passages in an archive.

The distinction is essential. The index establishes what the archive says, where it says it, when it appears to have said it, and how the assertion could be evaluated. It does not establish that the archive is correct.

Bottom line
EA-PREDICTION-INDEX-01 is best understood as a prediction-accountability and research infrastructure layer for the Crimson Hexagonal Archive.

Its central innovation is not simply finding thousands of predictive statements. It is creating a system in which those statements can retain their original wording, provenance, chronology, intellectual relationships, falsifiers, and subsequent evidence.

The underlying ambition is to move from an archive that contains claims about the future to an archive in which those claims can be systematically retrieved, tested, challenged, revised, and historically evaluated.

One particularly important distinction in Lee Sharks's document is that registration is not confirmation, and discoverability is not truth. The index is designed to make claims accountable without prematurely declaring them correct.

That is the most accurate interpretation of the document on its own terms.
