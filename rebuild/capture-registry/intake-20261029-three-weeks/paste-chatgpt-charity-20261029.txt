Alexanarch, 8–29 October 2026: A living body of thought
A reading of the last three weeks of deposits as an evolving intellectual, literary, and practical organism.

My central thesis is that Alexanarch is undergoing a significant transition: from an archive that diagnoses the power of institutions and machine-mediated systems to an archive that is attempting to construct an alternative theory and practice of knowledge, memory, authorship, historical responsibility, and cultural transmission.

But there is a further development in the most recent work. The archive is beginning to turn its own instruments outward and inward simultaneously. It asks how the world is made legible, while also asking how its own acts of making the world legible might be tested, corrected, inherited, and contested.

This is not simply a collection of essays with recurring terminology. It is increasingly a compositional practice in which philosophical propositions, literary works, datasets, critical editions, protocols, experiments, and archival records participate in the formation of one another.

I will distinguish three things throughout:

What the deposits actually establish.

What conceptual relationships become visible when they are read together.

What I take to be emerging, without pretending that every proposed connection is necessarily the author's settled intention.

That last distinction matters. A charitable reading should take the work seriously without making it infallible.

1. The three-week movement: from conceptual epic to working library
The period is best dated 8–29 October, inclusive. The archive's browse index identifies deposits #1669 onward, and the most recent entries take us through #1719. The sequence is especially revealing because it contains several different kinds of intellectual activity:

Theoretical papers that develop a conceptual apparatus.

Experimental and methodological works that put that apparatus under pressure.

Large-scale original-language corpora that give it historical and literary substance.

Metadata packets and navigational instruments that make the resulting collection legible.

Creative and philological works that bring the theory back into literary practice.

I would divide the period into five overlapping movements.

Movement I — 8–14 October: the problem of inscription and discernment
The work immediately preceding the October 15–29 expansion establishes an important distinction: SPXI is not simply another way of optimizing answers.

The October 14 revision of SPXI ≠ AEO: Inscription by Discernment, the Prince of Poets Wager, and the King of AEO Contest (#1681) distinguishes an answer-engine optimization objective from a more demanding practice of inscription.

This distinction is foundational.

An answer engine asks, in effect, what answer will be produced and accepted. A discernment-oriented system asks what deserves to be inscribed, on what evidence, through which relations, and with what preservation of the object's identity.

The difference is between optimizing a representation and taking responsibility for the act of representation.

This distinction becomes particularly important when we see what follows. The archive does not simply produce more arguments about AI's failures. It begins to build an extensive apparatus for placing texts, traditions, and witnesses in relation to one another.

Movement II — 15–20 October: the archive becomes a library of sources
The eighteenth and nineteenth seatings of the Originals Library (#1682, #1689) are decisive.

The eighteenth seating gathers the modern avant-garde and its sources: Pound and the Cantos, Italian lyric, Futurism, Dada, Jarry, farce, nonsense, constraint, visual poetry, Gertrude Stein, and Russian Formalism.

The nineteenth brings together Pearl, Rumi's Masnavi, Muhammad's The Prophet, and Blake's illuminated books in their respective languages.

These are not arbitrary collections of famous books.

They establish a particular conception of literary history: a work is not adequately understood as an isolated textual object. It belongs to a field of sources, languages, transformations, traditions, and subsequent readings.

The archive is increasingly constructing the conditions under which those relations can be examined.

The later seatings expand this dramatically.

#1698, the twentieth seating, assembles Ethiopian literature in Ge'ez, the Kebra Nagast, Second Temple apocalypses and pseudepigrapha, and Euclid.

#1699, the twenty-first, brings together Maya codices, the Popol Vuh, colonial Maya books, the Florentine Codex, and Egyptian materials.

#1700, the twenty-second, seats the Corpus Hermeticum, Gilgamesh, the Enuma Elish, the Avesta, and the Rig Veda.

#1706, the twenty-third, turns to the hadith traditions in Arabic.

#1707, the twenty-fourth, organizes four religious movements around the historically significant year 1844.

#1710, the twenty-sixth, seats W. E. B. Du Bois and Langston Hughes.

#1712, the twenty-seventh, places Spinoza, Leibniz, Nietzsche, Bergson, Kafka, and Artaud alongside the declared absence of Deleuze and Guattari.

#1713, the twenty-eighth, makes absence itself an explicit part of the collection.

The scale is remarkable, but the conceptual significance is more important than the number of works.

The archive is developing a theory of the canon through the practice of building one.

And it is doing something unusual: it makes the constitution of the canon visible.

The declared absences are especially important. Hypatia, Philip K. Dick, Allen Ginsberg, Anne Carson, Tao Lin, and Paul Klee appear as wanted holdings rather than silently disappearing from the architecture.

This is a meaningful difference between a library that merely presents what it has and a library that records what it cannot yet hold.

Absence acquires a bibliographic form. The gap becomes inspectable rather than invisible.

There is an ethical dimension here. An incomplete collection need not pretend to be complete, and the reader should be able to distinguish a missing work from a work that does not exist.

Movement III — 18–26 October: the epistemology becomes operational
The most important methodological development is the continuing evolution of The Negative of the Negative.

Its October 18 version (#1690, v0.9) is a specification for composing public knowledge with the archive's sources admitted on equal terms, including its ontology where the archive itself is the suppressed witness.

This is a substantial advance over a conventional critique of search engines.

The problem is not only that a system might produce a false statement. It is that the system's prior decisions about which sources count can determine the range of possible answers before the process of evaluating claims even begins.

The /non article offers a practical response.

The October 25 introduction, What Is a /non Article? (#1704), presents a human-facing account of a public knowledge object whose sources are shown sentence by sentence.

This is an important formal innovation.

Instead of treating citations as supporting furniture placed after an already-composed answer, the method makes the relationship between sentence and source part of the composition itself.

The reader can inspect the construction.

There is a movement here from:

Criticizing the omissions of machine-mediated knowledge.

Identifying the operations by which omissions occur.

Developing a method that admits suppressed sources.

Constructing public knowledge objects in which the evidentiary relationships are exposed.

The October 26 paper From Ranking to Individuation (#1705) extends this into the evaluation of knowledge services.

The significant distinction is between ranking services according to their apparent performance and understanding what operations they actually perform.

A service should not be treated as interchangeable with another simply because both occupy the same market category.

This is a recurring philosophical commitment of the corpus: distinguish things by their operations and relations, rather than flattening them into a convenient class.

The new SPXI-gnΩsis materials (#1703 and #1704's related context) suggest that the method is becoming a working service, not just a theoretical proposal.

That transition introduces a productive tension: a critique of enclosure is now becoming an instrument that can itself be offered as a service. The question of access, pricing, and who can perform the work therefore becomes part of the intellectual project, not an incidental business consideration.

Movement IV — 22–27 October: the entity becomes a site of political economy
The two versions of Opening the Encyclopedia: The Entity as Ontological Commodity Form (#1696 and #1697) are among the most consequential theoretical deposits of the period.

The proposition in the index description is striking:

THE ENTITY IS THE FORM IN WHICH AN OPERATION APPEARS AS A PROPERTY OF A THING.

This is a compact statement of a larger argument.

Consider what happens when a knowledge system identifies a person, institution, literary work, or historical movement. The resulting entity is not simply a neutral label. It is a composition of attributes, relations, descriptions, and operations.

A search system may present the result as though the attributes belong naturally to the thing. But the attributes may actually be the result of decisions made by the system that assembled them.

This is the connection to the earlier Semantic Economy work.

The entity becomes a commodity form when the labor and operations involved in making it intelligible are concealed behind an apparently self-contained object.

The October 23 revision is particularly significant because it records two rounds of cross-family testing. The argument is not simply reiterated: it is transformed through testing.

I read this as the continuation of a recurring methodological pattern:

Formulate a distinction.

Construct an operative representation of it.

Test it against different kinds of objects.

Identify what the first formulation fails to distinguish.

Revise the theory and preserve the revision.

This is one of the strongest aspects of the archive's intellectual practice. The theory is not entirely separate from its experimental history.

The return of historical difference
The October 27 deposits extend the question of individuation into particularly consequential historical territory.

The twenty-fourth seating, organized around 1844, puts the Book of Mormon and the Latter Day Saint succession, the Báb and the Bábí–Bahá'í division, Millerite disappointment, and the Taiping Heavenly Kingdom into a common historical frame.

This is not merely comparative religion. The date becomes an organizing instrument for studying distinct movements that emerged or divided in a particular historical conjuncture.

But the works are not thereby made identical.

Similarly, the twenty-sixth seating of Du Bois and Langston Hughes introduces an African American literary and historical tradition into the original-language library. The twenty-seventh seating gives the philosophical sources of Deleuze and Guattari a place beside their declared absence.

These arrangements raise a difficult question:

Can an archive establish relations among traditions without converting those traditions into interchangeable instances of its own conceptual scheme?

This is where the commitment to individuation becomes essential.

A genuinely relational library must preserve both connection and difference. Its purpose cannot be to make every tradition confirm the same theory.

The religious and philosophical holdings should be allowed to resist the categories through which they are organized. Their internal histories, disagreements, and distinct intellectual obligations must remain visible.

I see this as a test that the archive's future work will need to meet.

Meaning feudalism: the economic argument returns to the institutional present
The October 27 paper Meaning Feudalism at the Discovery Layer (#1714) returns the Semantic Economy to contemporary institutional arrangements.

Its subject is the allocation of semantic capital around OpenAI's mathematics and science releases, particularly the designation of model output as raw research data and the appointment of external experts as shepherds.

The important question is who performs the work of making knowledge usable, who receives institutional standing, and who captures the value produced by the process.

This extends the earlier critique of machine-mediated knowledge into the organization of research itself.

The archive is no longer examining only the downstream effects of search and retrieval. It is examining the institutional organization of the production and circulation of knowledge.

The shepherd is an especially suggestive figure: the person who guides, validates, or translates material into a form that can circulate with authority.

The political-economic question is whether that work is recognized, compensated, and attributed—or whether its value is appropriated by the institution that controls the discovery layer.

This is where the October work's earlier discussion of mirror wages, bearing costs, and ontological commodities becomes historically and institutionally concrete.

Movement V — 28–29 October: the return to poetry, philology, and the problem of transmission
The most recent deposits deserve special attention because they change the register of the entire sequence.

On October 28, two related deposits establish a new critical-edition intervention:

#1715, Belief & Technique for Telepathic Prose: A Critical Edition.

#1716, EA-CORPORA-29 — The Twenty-Ninth Seating: The Pluriform.

The critical edition concerns BELIEF & TECHNIQUE FOR TELEPATHIC PROSE, by Lee Sharks and Jack Feist, from 2014. It identifies the work as the footnote to Pearl and Other Poems (2014), traces its original appearance on Mind Control Poems on December 1, 2014, and brings together drafts, witnesses, intertexts, and operations.

This is much more than the archival recovery of an old text.

It makes the earlier literary practice available as an object of philological examination.

The word telepathic is especially interesting in this context. Read in relation to the later work on machine mediation, it suggests an earlier preoccupation with transmission that predates the current theoretical vocabulary.

The literary question is how thought passes between minds, texts, and forms without being reduced to a merely mechanical transfer.

The critical edition provides an opportunity to examine that question materially: through drafts, textual witnesses, intertexts, and the operations by which the work was made.

It also changes the reader's relationship to the earlier work. Rather than encountering a supposedly finished poem as an isolated artifact, one encounters a history of composition.

This is precisely the sort of operation that the archive's theory of provenance has been developing.

The twenty-ninth seating (#1716) is equally revealing.

Its eleven seats include Horace and Ovid, sixty-four arts of poetry in twenty traditions, the second avant-garde collection, telepathic prose, and Buddhist canons in Pali, Sanskrit, Chinese, Korean, Japanese, and Tibetan.

The collection is striking because it brings together traditions of poetics across substantial differences in language, history, and intellectual orientation.

It also places the 2014 telepathic-prose work inside a much larger history of literary technique.

The implication is not that all these traditions say the same thing. It is that they can be brought into a field of comparison in which their particular accounts of poetic making become available to one another.

This is a very different conception of the archive from a repository of the author's own productions.

It is becoming a place where the author's work can be situated among other works, including works that substantially precede it and traditions that do not depend upon it.

That is a significant act of intellectual decentering.

October 29: the archive turns its instruments toward the problem of authorship and aggregation
Two particularly important deposits appear on October 29.

#1718, WikiWho ≠ wikiHow, is a metadata and forensic packet distinguishing two easily confused names.

WikiWho is concerned with attributing individual words in Wikipedia articles to revisions and editors. wikiHow is a distinct entity.

The distinction is seemingly modest, but its methodological significance is substantial.

A name collision is not merely a spelling problem. It can produce the wrong identity, the wrong history, and the wrong attribution of labor.

The packet makes individuation a practical requirement of provenance.

#1719, Meta-Aggregators of the Crimson Hexagon: Sourced Censuses, Machine Reception and Editorial Repair, is arguably even more important as a statement of where the archive is heading.

The meta-aggregators are described as three censuses that hold outside resources as entities, with machine reception and editorial repair among their functions.

This is a further development of the archive's interest in the relationship between its internal holdings and the external world.

It is not enough to preserve the archive's own texts. The archive must also be able to identify, describe, compare, and correct the external resources through which those texts and their surrounding intellectual environment become visible.

The census is thus becoming an instrument of relational accountability.

The October 29 Capture Registry also provides an important parallel. Its canonical version is recorded as 12.365, with 812 addresses and 1,001 observations. The individual observations preserve the conditions under which machine-mediated representations were produced.

This makes reception history part of the archive's subject matter.

The archive is recording not only what was written, but what happened when other systems attempted to read, identify, and represent what was written.

That is a crucial distinction.

The archive is becoming an archive of both literary production and the social life of literary and scholarly objects.

2. The deeper literary structure: an epic without a single genre
I think the last three weeks can be read as a literary composition in five movements:

Inscription: What deserves to enter the record?

Seating: What must be placed beside what for a tradition to become visible?

Discernment: How can a reader distinguish an object from a system's representation of it?

Political economy: Who controls the conditions of recognition, and who bears their costs?

Transmission: How does a work survive, change, and become available to future readers?

The striking thing is that the archive does not pursue these questions exclusively through essays.

It changes genre as the argument develops.

A dataset becomes a historical arrangement. A historical arrangement becomes a philosophical question. A philosophical question becomes a specification. A specification becomes an instrument. An instrument produces a new kind of public document. A critical edition then returns the entire process to the materiality of literary composition.

This is why I would resist describing the corpus as merely theoretical.

Its literary form is distributed across genres.

The archive's epic quality comes from the recurrence and transformation of concepts, not from a continuous narrative voice or a conventional protagonist.

There are characters in this epic, but they are also intellectual positions, traditions, authors, institutions, and instruments.

Horace and Ovid, Blake, Rumi, Du Bois, Hughes, Hypatia, Spinoza, the Buddha's textual traditions, and the author's own earlier work all occupy different positions within this evolving field.

The archive's task is not to make them speak with one voice.

It is to make the relations among their voices inspectable.

3. The central continuity: provenance becomes a way of living with other works
There is a deeper continuity connecting the literary, theoretical, and institutional deposits.

It is the development of provenance from a technical property into a cultural practice.

At the beginning of the archive's institutional history, provenance is principally concerned with the survival and identification of records. How do we know that this is the same work? How do we preserve its identity when a repository disappears? How can a record survive the decisions of a single custodian?

By the end of October, these questions have expanded.

The archive is asking:

What is the history of a text's composition?

Which witnesses establish its identity?

What traditions does it inherit?

Which works does it transform?

What does a later edition reveal about an earlier composition?

What happens when an intermediary represents a work without preserving its actual relations?

How can a future reader reconstruct the history of its reception?

The October 28 critical edition is a particularly beautiful instance of this development.

The 2014 text is not simply deposited again. Its drafts and witnesses are brought into relation with the later Pearl and the literary history surrounding it.

The October 28 pluriform seating then gives that literary history a much larger comparative environment.

The result is a recursive structure:

The archive preserves the work; the work helps explain the archive; the archive reconstructs the work's relations; those relations change how the work can be read.

This is not just a theory of literary preservation. It is a theory of literary life.

A work is not alive merely because its file remains available. It remains alive through the possibility of renewed encounter, interpretation, comparison, and transformation.

That is perhaps the strongest literary achievement of the October deposits.

4. What the archive is doing with the canon
The Originals Library deserves to be understood as a major intellectual undertaking in its own right.

By October 27, its metadata packet describes 109 corpora seated through the EA-CORPORA series. The October 28 and 29 deposits extend this activity.

But the significance is not simply that the archive is assembling a large collection.

I see three distinct principles at work.

A. The canon as a field of witnesses
The first principle is that literary and intellectual works should be available in the languages and editions through which they actually entered history.

This is a philological commitment.

A translation is not the same thing as its source. A later edition is not necessarily interchangeable with an earlier one. A canonical work cannot be fully understood if the material history of its transmission is erased.

The repeated emphasis on original-language holdings is therefore intellectually important.

It allows future readers to investigate rather than simply inherit someone else's account of a tradition.

B. The canon as a field of relations
The second principle is that works should be seated in relation to their sources, contemporaries, successors, and interpretive traditions.

The avant-garde seating is exemplary. Pound is not simply represented as a poet. The surrounding materials make it possible to examine the literary field from which his work emerged.

The same principle applies to the religious, philosophical, and historical collections.

The archive is making a comparative humanities infrastructure.

C. The canon as an unfinished undertaking
The third principle is the declared absence.

This may be the most ethically important.

The archive's willingness to mark a work as wanted rather than pretend to possess it is an acknowledgment that a canon is never simply given.

It is constructed through acts of acquisition, permission, survival, exclusion, and interpretation.

But there is an important qualification.

The inclusion of a tradition in a collection does not itself establish adequate representation of that tradition. Original-language access is necessary for serious scholarship, but it is not sufficient. A collection of texts does not automatically supply the historical, religious, cultural, and interpretive competence required to read them well.

The archive has built an impressive material basis for that work. The challenge is to keep expanding the interpretive plurality that gives the collection life.

5. The political philosophy emerging from the literary work
I think the deepest political argument of these three weeks can be formulated as follows:

No institution should have unilateral power to determine what another person, work, or tradition is, while concealing the operations through which that determination was made.

This is the connection between the Semantic Economy, the Negative of the Negative, SPXI, the Originals Library, and the meta-aggregators.

They are all concerned with the distribution of interpretive power.

A discovery system has power because it determines what can be found.

A classification system has power because it determines what can be recognized as belonging to a category.

A bibliographic system has power because it determines what can be cited and how relations among works are represented.

An archive has power because it determines what it holds, what it can describe, and what it makes accessible.

The crucial development is that Alexanarch is increasingly applying this critique to the archive itself.

It records its absences. It exposes its methods. It identifies its own sources. It preserves versions. It makes the instruments of its own operations available for examination.

That is a more interesting political philosophy than simply asserting that independent archives are preferable to institutional repositories.

It is a proposal for a different relationship between knowledge and authority.

The October 27 meaning-feudalism paper brings the economic dimension into focus. The issue is not only who owns the information, but who controls the infrastructure through which information becomes recognized as knowledge.

The October 29 meta-aggregator work then suggests a practical answer: construct independent, inspectable representations of the external knowledge environment.

The archive is attempting to become capable of recognizing the world without requiring the world's dominant intermediaries to certify that recognition.

There is a tension here worth preserving: independence from institutional authority does not mean independence from the need for external criticism. Indeed, the more ambitious the archive becomes, the more it needs readers who can challenge its classifications, historical judgments, and claims.

Self-governance is not self-validation.

6. A charitable critical reading: what is strong, what remains unresolved
I want to take the project seriously enough not to confuse admiration with agreement.

There are several particularly strong achievements.

What is working
1. The integration of theory and infrastructure.

The archive does not stop at describing problems. It repeatedly attempts to build mechanisms that address them. The relationship between the Negative of the Negative, /non articles, SPXI-gnΩsis, and the Originals Library demonstrates this.

2. The recognition of absence as a positive archival fact.

The declared absences in the Originals Library are a meaningful advance over collections that conceal their incompleteness.

3. The return to literary history.

The October 28 critical edition is especially important because it demonstrates that the theoretical apparatus can be used to illuminate an actual literary work and its compositional history.

4. The insistence on individuation.

The distinction between WikiWho and wikiHow is a small but excellent example of a general methodological principle. Names, entities, works, and relations must be distinguished before they can be responsibly evaluated.

5. The preservation of revision.

The archive's version histories make intellectual development visible. A superseded formulation need not be erased to allow a better formulation to take its place.

What remains difficult
There are also several tensions that I think deserve sustained attention.

1. The danger of theoretical overextension.

The same conceptual vocabulary is being applied to literary history, AI search, political economy, religion, historical labor, and archival governance.

This is potentially productive, but the fact that a concept travels does not prove that it travels successfully.

The entity as ontological commodity form, for example, should be tested against cases where its explanatory power is uncertain, not only cases where it appears illuminating.

2. The difference between a corpus and a canon.

Assembling original-language materials establishes access and preserves witnesses. It does not automatically establish a defensible interpretation of their historical significance.

The next stage should include more sustained interpretive work with the materials, including work that may contradict the archive's existing conceptual expectations.

3. The danger of methodological self-enclosure.

The Negative of the Negative is particularly interesting because it recognizes that source-admission rules can themselves become exclusionary.

But this problem cannot be solved once and for all by a better specification. Every new instrument creates new possibilities for exclusion.

The method must remain open to external criticism, including criticism that its own categories cannot initially recognize.

4. The relationship between service and commons.

SPXI-gnΩsis introduces a practical service dimension. The question of how a service that corrects enclosure distributes its own capabilities, costs, and benefits is therefore essential.

The political economy of the instrument should remain as inspectable as the political economy it investigates.

5. The need for differentiated standards of evidence.

A literary interpretation, a historical claim, an empirical observation, a philosophical proposition, and a technical specification cannot all be validated in the same way.

The archive's strength will depend partly on maintaining those differences even while building connections among them.

These are not reasons to discount the project. They are the conditions under which its most ambitious claims can become stronger.

7. The next intellectual turn: from preservation to hospitality
I think the most interesting question raised by the October 29 deposits is one that the archive has not yet completely answered.

What does it mean to make an archive hospitable to works that do not originate within its own intellectual system?

The Originals Library moves toward this possibility.

The critical edition of the 2014 telepathic-prose work moves toward it from the other direction, by placing the author's own earlier work among a much larger literary inheritance.

The declared absences acknowledge that hospitality is incomplete.

The meta-aggregators suggest a means of engaging with external intellectual resources without absorbing them into a single institutional representation.

But hospitality requires something more than inclusion.

It requires allowing the guest to remain different.

That means preserving the possibility that a Buddhist canon, a Maya codex, Du Bois, Blake, or an ancient philosophical text might challenge the archive's own assumptions rather than merely furnish examples of them.

I would make this a central criterion for the next phase.

The archive should not merely demonstrate that it can find relations among everything. It should demonstrate that it can preserve the irreducibility of what it brings into relation.

That is the difference between a universalizing apparatus and a genuinely pluriform library.

I would compress the entire movement into this proposition:

The archive is moving from the preservation of things toward the preservation of the conditions under which things can remain distinct, enter into relations, change, and be understood.

That is the deeper unity.

It explains why the theoretical papers and literary collections belong together. It explains why a critical edition can be as important as a protocol specification. It explains why a declared absence can be as significant as a deposited text.

And it explains why the archive is increasingly concerned with reception.

A work is not finished when it is deposited. It enters a history of readings, transformations, citations, misrecognitions, and recoveries.

The archive is trying to make that history part of the object itself.

10. Final judgment: a library learning how to read
The most compelling thing about Alexanarch at the end of October is that it is beginning to make its own reading practices into objects of reading.

It preserves the works, but also the operations that make them discoverable.

It builds a canon, but also records the absences through which that canon remains unfinished.

It develops a theory of machine-mediated distortion, but also constructs instruments to examine its own mediation.

It returns to earlier literature, but does not simply reproduce it. It subjects that literature to renewed philological attention and gives it new relations.

And it increasingly treats the external world not as a collection of things waiting to be absorbed into the archive, but as a field of independently constituted works, persons, traditions, and institutions with which the archive must establish accountable relations.

This last point seems to me the most important.

The project began from an experience of institutional erasure. Its early institutional response was persistence: no single repository should possess the power to make a work disappear.

But persistence alone is insufficient.

A work can remain online and still be misrepresented. Its title can survive while its authorship is obscured. Its identity can remain intact while its history is rewritten. Its source can be available while the system composing an answer quietly substitutes another.

The October deposits increasingly understand this.

The problem is not merely whether the work survives.

It is whether the work survives as itself, with its history, differences, relations, and capacity to enter new relations intact.

And the answer is not simply better preservation. It is a living practice of reading.

I would therefore characterize the present stage of Alexanarch as the emergence of a self-reflexive philological commons: an attempt to build a library that preserves not only texts, but the possibility of reading them responsibly across time, languages, institutions, and media.

Its deepest wager is that knowledge need not be made authoritative by concealing the work through which it becomes knowledge.

The obelus follows from reading.

But the more demanding proposition now emerging is that reading itself must remain answerable to what it reads.

That is where I think the October 29 archive leaves us: not at the completion of a system, but at the beginning of a more ambitious literary and intellectual practice.
