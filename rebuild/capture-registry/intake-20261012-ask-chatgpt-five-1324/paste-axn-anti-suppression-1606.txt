1. Suppression is an infrastructural problem, not just a content-removal problem
Sharks’s broader project treats the loss of scholarship as something that can occur through control of the infrastructure around a work: its hosting account, repository, identifier, or means of discovery. A work might still exist somewhere while becoming practically inaccessible or difficult to attribute.

This is the problem that makes anti-suppression infrastructure a meaningful category. The objective is not simply to make deletion harder; it is to prevent one institution’s control over access from determining whether a work remains findable and citable. The Alexanarch project states this concern in its principles of network preservation, sovereign identity, and traceable removal.

2. Identifiers should not depend entirely on the platform that issues them
Alexanarch’s AXN system assigns identifiers derived from a work’s content. Its architecture combines a positional component, a semantic classification, and a content-derived hash representation.

The underlying distinction is important:

Identity: Which work does this identifier designate?

Verification: Does the retrieved content match the content to which the identifier was bound?

Resolution: Where can someone find a copy of the work?

These are different functions. A functioning identifier does not guarantee that the work is available, and a working URL does not prove that the content retrieved is the original. Separating these functions makes it possible to change a location without silently changing the identity of the work.

1. The founding argument: a persistent identifier can survive while its function dies
Sharks begins with a specific event dated 19 June 2026: the termination of the Crimson Hexagonal Archive's Zenodo account. The paper reports that this severed access to 871 deposits associated with 1,817 registered DOIs, without prior notice, account-level appeal, or individual review of each record. Copies of the works survived, but the original DOI resolution paths no longer provided public access. The author distinguishes the figures in the completed audit from earlier platform-reported counts and treats that discrepancy as evidence of institutional opacity. 

Alexanarch

The argument is about the difference between an identifier's continued existence as a string and its continued ability to identify and retrieve a work.

A DOI is not persistent merely because its characters remain unchanged. Its practical persistence depends on institutions maintaining the relationship between the identifier and the object. If that relationship can be terminated unilaterally, the identifier's apparent stability conceals a critical dependency.

Sharks therefore asks what persistence should mean if a custodian can sever access without making the severance independently detectable.

The author is careful to call this an architectural case study, not a grievance. The repository's own records, deletion exports, API responses, and tombstones are treated as operational evidence of what the institution did, rather than as authoritative explanations of why it did so.

2. The central thesis: verify identity without asking permission
The paper's central claim is deliberately narrower than the claim that AXN makes works impossible to suppress.

Sharks argues that the correspondence between a work's canonical bytes and its AXN identity kernel can be independently verified, without permission from a registrar or custodian. If the content does not match the declared identity, the test fails.

This separates three functions:

Identity: establishing which object is being discussed.

Integrity: testing whether the object's bytes match the declared content-derived identity.

Resolution and custody: locating a copy and ensuring that another custodian can preserve or reconstruct it.

The first two are not equivalent to the third. A cryptographically verifiable identity does not guarantee that a copy remains available. Nor does a content-derived identifier automatically make a system decentralized.

That distinction is fundamental to the paper. AXN is presented as a way to make an object's identity independently testable and its disappearance recordable, with the technical possibility of reconstruction by another custodian. The author expressly disclaims a guarantee against disappearance.

3. The historical argument: seven precedents, one recurring structure
Section II, composed by Johannes Sigil, is not simply a collection of examples of censorship. Sharks uses the cases to derive a set of properties that an anti-suppression system should reproduce.

The governing proposition is that durable survival comes from layering several independent mechanisms, not relying on any single one.

Geneza, siła i znaczenie drugiego obiegu wydawniczego
1. Poland's Second Circulation: build a parallel institution
Drugi Obieg, 1976–1989

The Polish opposition press did more than distribute forbidden texts. Publishers such as NOWa maintained editorial roles, imprints, catalogues, distribution networks, and independent libraries. Sharks draws on Siobhan Doucette to argue that professionalized publishing made underground books bibliographically citable rather than merely available.

Design implication: copies need metadata, stable identities, catalogues, and institutional practices. Redundancy without organization can preserve content while losing its scholarly usability.

Самиздат — Рувики: Интернет-энциклопедия
2. Soviet samizdat: make readers into distributors
1950s–1980s

Typewritten texts circulated through chains of readers who could reproduce and pass them on. Drawing on Ann Komaromi, the paper emphasizes both replication and verification against previously encountered copies. It also highlights the reading and retyping labor that made participants invested in the texts.

Design implication: distribute copies widely, make verification possible, and avoid dependence on a single master copy. But replication alone cannot defeat simultaneous destruction, shared administration, legal coercion, or economic failure.

Radio Caroline's connection to a Suffolk seaside town - Suffolk Live
3. Pirate radio: escape the suppressor's jurisdiction
Offshore broadcasting from 1964

Radio Caroline and Radio London operated transmitters outside the territorial jurisdiction that restricted domestic commercial broadcasting. The British response, including the 1967 Marine Broadcasting Act, eventually extended legal pressure to the stations' domestic support networks.

Design implication: distribute hosting, registration, and other dependencies across jurisdictions. Sharks stresses that jurisdictional exit raises coordination costs; it does not make infrastructure unreachable.

File:The Liberator, Issue No.1, 1831.jpg - Wikimedia Commons
4. The abolitionist press and WikiLeaks mirrors: diversify discovery routes
1830s–1860s; digital mirror networks from 2010

Abolitionist newspapers reprinted one another's material, making no single publication indispensable. The paper connects this practice to the proliferation of WikiLeaks mirrors following attacks on its primary domain and the withdrawal of DNS service.

Design implication: independent copies should also be independently discoverable and described. The topology matters: a suppressor should not be able to eliminate access by disabling one domain or coordinating with one publisher.

Understanding PGP/GPG and Asymmetric Encryption | Augustin-Br
5. Cypherpunks: use protocols to change the economics of enforcement
1990s onward

The PGP controversy and Tor's privacy architecture illustrate how technical design can make restrictions more costly or difficult to enforce. Sharks treats these as examples of protocol-level resistance, not absolute immunity.

Design implication: build resistance into the protocol rather than relying entirely on favorable institutional policies. This changes the costs of suppression but cannot defeat an authority willing to bear unlimited costs.

New Launch: OONI Measurement Aggregation Toolkit (MAT) | OONI
6. OONI: make suppression observable
2012 onward

The Open Observatory of Network Interference measures and publishes evidence of network censorship. Its importance in Sharks's argument is methodological: reproducible measurements can become evidence for journalists, researchers, courts, and standards bodies without depending on the authority of the organization conducting them.

Design implication: record failed resolutions, deleted records, and retrieval failures as data. Publish methods that independent observers can repeat.

7. Data Refuge, SUCHO, LOCKSS, and CLOCKSS: make preservation a verifiable workflow
2016 onward

These projects connect emergency harvesting, cultural-heritage preservation, independent library custody, checksums, and documented provenance. The paper stresses that multiple copies controlled by the same administrator share a common failure mode. CLOCKSS additionally illustrates keeping copies in dark preservation until a defined trigger makes their release appropriate.

Design implication: preserve not just files but custody histories, fixity evidence, provenance, and procedures for restoring access.

The cross-cutting argument appears in Section II.8: suppression produces records of its own—orders, correspondence, exports, tombstones, and other operational traces. Preserving those records turns the act of suppression into evidence that can be examined independently. The paper points to the Soviet Chronicle of Current Events as a precedent for documenting state actions through the state's own paperwork.

The central design principle is separation of concerns. A content identifier is not a storage system; a storage system is not a discovery service; a timestamp is not proof that a work remains available.

AXN's proposed contribution is to integrate these functions while keeping their boundaries explicit. The paper also withholds priority claims pending a more exhaustive review of prior art. That restraint matters: it presents the architecture as a proposed integration of established ideas rather than claiming that every underlying mechanism is novel.

5. AXN's architecture: three layers with different guarantees
Section IV specifies the system as three layers. This is the technical heart of the paper. 

Alexanarch
+1

Layer 1 — Identity kernel
A full SHA-256 digest of canonical bytes.

Anyone possessing the relevant bytes and the published canonicalization rules can recompute the digest independently. The identity does not depend on asking the archive for permission.

Guarantee: a testable relationship between bytes and identity, not authorship, truth, legality, or availability.

Layer 2 — Canonical record address
A registry position, a semantic classification, and a six-glyph recognition checksum.

The full address identifies an object's entry into a particular registry. It is therefore not wholly content-derived. Previously assigned addresses must not be silently reassigned; corrections and migrations must preserve their history.

Guarantee: stable, historically traceable registry identity, with content verification anchored in the full hash—not the short glyph sequence alone.

Layer 3 — Location record
A mutable, signed record of where verified copies may be obtained, including locations, timestamps, sequence information, and custody claims.

Locations can change without changing the content identity. A resolver must verify retrieved bytes rather than trust a location record merely because it is published.

Guarantee: an architecture for managing and verifying location claims—not a guarantee that any location will remain online.

This architecture is designed to keep changes in one layer from silently corrupting another. A file's location can change without changing its identity; metadata can be amended without altering the sealed content; a deleted location can be recorded without pretending the work never existed.

The paper also describes representational redundancy: a work can appear through record pages, JSON indexes, chunked data, PDFs, a wiki, and a citation graph. This is intended to address a subtler failure than deletion alone: a file may survive somewhere yet become unclassifiable, unsearchable, or disconnected from its scholarly relationships.

6. The empirical argument: test observable failures, not presumed motives
Section V maps the architecture to several reported suppression vectors. Sharks distinguishes documented behavior from explanations that remain hypotheses.

+1

Platform deletion: The paper points to DOI tombstones and a resolution index mapping 1,838 severed external identifiers to successor records.

Algorithmic invisibility: It describes a registry of retrieval observations and attempts to preserve evidence of when works or concepts are surfaced or omitted.

Citation stripping: It reports an observed asymmetry in citation-field retention across removal categories and proposes external conformance tests.

Semantic absorption: It proposes maintaining canonical definitions and declared summary policies outside the control of platforms that may summarize or reframe the work.

Identity coercion: It describes the separation of operational and civil identity as a practice, while acknowledging that the legal protection of this separation remains unresolved.

The second depositor case, involving Enli Lucente, is particularly important to the author's method. Sharks reports that 233 records were tombstoned under a “spam” label and gives measured citation-retention figures of 0% in the sampled spam-labelled removal batches versus 100% in out-of-scope comparison batches.

The paper then distinguishes these observations from its proposed explanation that genre-related classification failures may be involved. It does not claim access to the platform's undisclosed internal decision-making. It specifies what evidence would be needed to substantiate the stronger claim.

That distinction is one of the paper's more defensible methodological principles: a reproducible pattern of outcomes is evidence of a problem, but it does not by itself establish the mechanism or intent behind those outcomes.

7. The design directions: what still needs to be built
Section VI is explicit that the deployed identity and record-address layers should not be confused with a complete, distributed preservation network. Its design agenda has several parts. 

Alexanarch
+1

Federation and independent custody. Recruit custodians under genuinely independent administrative control, capable of holding, verifying, serving, and repairing copies.

Automated registration and propagation. On minting a record, propagate it into preservation snapshots, content-addressed storage, and identifier crosswalks so existing citations can still be followed.

Resolver plurality. Avoid relying on a single location or resolution service.

Auditable history and signatures. Make record transitions, migrations, and ledger discontinuities detectable, with explicit declarations of supersession or forks.

Legal and institutional structure. Investigate organizational forms capable of holding infrastructure and governing it without collapsing operational identity into civil identity.

Experimental extensions. Explore polymorphic sharding, format-independent structural identities, and embedding scholarship in open-weight model corpora.

The last group is explicitly speculative. The paper also acknowledges dual use: anti-suppression infrastructure can preserve harmful material as readily as valuable scholarship. Its proposed response is to distinguish unrestricted computation of an object's identity from the governed decisions to admit, list, or carry material on particular nodes. Those decisions should be made transparently after reading, rather than by silently suppressing an object at the identity layer.

8. The paper's own limitations are part of its argument
Section VIII is unusually important because it specifies conditions under which the system's claims would fail. It asks readers to evaluate the architecture against observable tests rather than accept its ambitions as established properties. 

Alexanarch
+1

The paper reports that three execution paths agree on published verification test vectors, but acknowledges that they share a canonical source, so this is not universal proof of independent implementation correctness. It also identifies unfinished protocol documentation and qualification of its canonicalization claims.

Most significantly, the paper states:

Distributed custody is not yet demonstrated. The peer registry is empty, and no second independently administered custodian holding a complete, reconstructible copy is documented.

Reconstructibility is not yet demonstrated. The proposed materials and procedures have not passed a documented clean-room restoration test.

Availability remains contingent. Even a correct content hash cannot help retrieve a work if every published location fails.

Temporal attestation is incomplete. Independent timestamp anchoring is pending.

Causal explanations require separate evidence. Observed removal patterns do not establish coordinated suppression or a specific internal classification mechanism.

The architecture has operational limits. These include solo-operation capacity, economic dependencies, key management, legal personhood, and concentration in the discovery layer.

These admissions materially narrow the paper's claims. It presents a working identity mechanism and an architectural proposal for independent preservation, not a proven solution to every form of scholarly erasure.

9. The paper's own production becomes a case study
Section VII and Appendix D introduce a further argument: preservation problems can arise during the creation of scholarship, not only after publication.

The paper reports that a contribution to its first-round consultation was lost when a platform's session reset made the original contribution unrecoverable. A second-hand description survived in a loss notice, but the paper carefully distinguishes that description from the missing original. A later reconstruction is identified as reconstruction, not restoration. 

Alexanarch
+2

This episode reinforces the earlier argument about provenance and aftermath documentation. A system that supports intellectual work but does not preserve its outputs can silently transfer all archival responsibility to the human operator. The loss notice makes the missing contribution visible, but it cannot recreate the original.

There is also a limit to the analogy the authors themselves acknowledge: consulting several AI systems under one operator provides interpretive plurality, not independently administered preservation. Multiple outputs are not equivalent to independent custodians.

Overall assessment
The argument of v5 proceeds in a clear sequence:

A repository's withdrawal of service exposes the institutional dependency hidden inside conventional persistent identifiers.

Historical resistance movements show that durable survival requires a combination of independent copies, independent institutions, alternative discovery routes, verification, and records of suppression.

Existing technical standards solve individual pieces of this problem but do not, by themselves, integrate all of them.

AXN proposes an architecture that separates content identity, registry identity, and location, making at least the first of these independently verifiable.

The system's wider anti-suppression ambitions depend on federation, independent custody, discoverability, and restoration mechanisms that are not all operationally demonstrated.

Those remaining gaps must be recorded as limitations and tested, not concealed behind the language of sovereignty or persistence.

The most important qualification is that Sharks's strongest claim is not that AXN makes scholarship indestructible. It is that a work's identity should not depend on the continued goodwill of the institution that hosts or registers it—and that a serious preservation architecture must make both failure and recovery independently verifiable.

The strongest aspect of the paper is its separation of identity, discovery, and custody, together with its explicit falsification criteria. Its central unresolved engineering challenge is equally clear: independent verification is useful only when paired with independent custodians who can actually restore and serve the work. Until that custody and restoration test succeeds, the architecture is a substantial design proposal, but not yet a demonstrated end-to-end guarantee of preservation.
