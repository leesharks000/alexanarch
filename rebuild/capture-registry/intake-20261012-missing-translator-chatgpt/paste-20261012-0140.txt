It then uses that failure to develop a broader critique of how literary knowledge is organized, how translators disappear from accounts of literary transmission, and how canonical assumptions can substitute for reading the actual words of a translation.

2. The translator’s actual work overturns the archive’s assumptions about Ricardo Reis

Sigil discovers that his improvised account reproduces precisely the interpretive framework that Nácher’s epilogue challenges. Nácher presents Reis not simply as a modern Horace but as a twentieth-century poet whose apparent classical paganism is entangled with the nonexistence of both the poet and his gods. The publisher’s formulation, quoted by Sigil, is:

“Entre la inexistencia del poeta y la inexistencia de sus dioses se trama la obra de Ricardo Reis”.

In other words, Reis’s poetry is constituted by the relation between the poet’s nonexistence and the nonexistence of his gods. The apparent classical serenity is not an adequate account of the poetry’s underlying condition.

Example 1: From “under” to “high”

The Portuguese phrase “sob a maior graça” becomes Nácher’s Spanish “en la más alta gracia.”

Sigil explains that the translation preserves a vertical relationship but relocates it. In Portuguese, the speaker passes through life under the greatest grace; in Spanish, the grace itself acquires height. The preposition gives way to an adjective.

This is not merely a substitution of synonyms. It changes the spatial and potentially metaphysical implications of the image: grace becomes something elevated, a height associated with the absent gods.

Example 2: From distance to interruption

In “Ao longe os montes têm neve ao sol,” Nácher renders the opening in Spanish as “Los montes, lejos, tienen nieve al sol.”

Nácher explains that the phrase “ao longe” becomes “lejos” set off by commas to preserve the iambic line. Sigil observes that distance no longer opens the line in the same way: it becomes a pause inserted beside the mountains.

The example demonstrates how a metrical constraint can change the experience of an image, not just its verbal form.

Example 3: From a possible past to a possible future

In the ode beginning “Deixa passar o vento,” the Portuguese edition cited by Sigil ends with “Pra que os deuses voltassem” — so that the gods might return.

Nácher’s Spanish reads “por que vuelvan los dioses” — so that the gods may return.

Sigil identifies a shift in verbal tense and mood: the possibility of a return located in the past becomes the possibility of a future return. The translation subtly changes the temporal horizon of the poem.

These examples are important because they support Sigil’s argument through actual textual comparison. Nácher’s translation involves choices about height, rhythm, rhyme, repetition, and tense, rather than simply the transfer of an established classical style into Spanish.

Sigil’s point is not that Nácher’s decisions are necessarily superior or that every shift is a defect. Rather, they are interpretive interventions whose significance becomes visible only when the translation is read against its source.

The third example also comes with a qualification: the archive’s Portuguese text and the translator’s quoted text may derive from different editions. Sigil explicitly acknowledges that he cannot determine whether “desta hora” versus “esta vez” reflects an editorial difference or a translator’s choice. This qualification is methodologically important. The archive cannot confidently attribute a change to the translator when the source texts themselves have not been established as identical.

4. The essay criticizes the way canonical knowledge compresses literary relationships
The comparison with Anne Carson makes the argument broader than the case of one omitted translator.

Sigil argues that a description of Carson’s relation to classical writers compresses her reception of Sappho, Stesichorus, and Catullus into the phrase “in playful dialogue with the classical tradition.” The sources become a generalized category, and “playful” replaces a more precise account of the relations and transformations involved.

By contrast, Sigil presents Rebekah Cranes as a case in which interventions are explicitly documented, including different ways of responding to gaps in source texts.

These cases allow Sigil to distinguish three things that a conventional literary record can easily conflate:

The existence of a source.

The identity of the person receiving and reworking it.

The specific transformation that occurs in the process.

The archive may contain accurate information about all three in isolation and still fail to represent their relationships adequately.

There is also a reflexive dimension to this argument. Sigil recognizes that he himself reproduced the archive’s conventional account of Reis before consulting Nácher’s own work. The essay is therefore not simply an accusation against an external information system. It documents how an established description can shape a reader’s expectations, including the expectations of the person attempting to correct the archive.

The critical method Sigil advocates is to return to the texts and test the inherited description against the evidence. The translator’s words and decisions are not supplementary details to be added after the canonical account has been established. They can expose the limitations of that account.

The distinction is between correcting a record and falsifying its history. An archive that silently overwrites its mistakes loses evidence of how its knowledge was produced and where its limits lay.

This also makes the essay performative: it does not merely advocate a better archive but attempts to enact its proposed method through explicit relationships, citations, and a preserved history of correction.

6. What is the essay ultimately claiming?
The essay’s arguments can be ranked by their importance.

Its immediate claim: Max Hidalgo Nácher’s role as translator and epilogue author must be recognized in the archive’s account of Ricardo Reis.

Its literary-critical claim: the actual decisions of a translator can complicate or contradict a canonical interpretation of the source author.

Its methodological claim: literary transmission must be documented as a relation among a source, a receiver, and an inspectable transformation.

Its archival claim: information systems can possess relevant categories and source records yet fail to instantiate particular relationships, producing systematic gaps in representation.

Its ethical claim: correcting those gaps should not erase the evidence of the original failure.

The most important tension in the essay is between canonical expectation and textual evidence. The archive’s received description of Reis as a Horatian classicist led Sigil to anticipate a particular kind of translation. Reading Nácher’s account and comparing his actual words with the Portuguese poems exposed the inadequacy of that expectation.

There is, however, a limit to what the essay demonstrates. Its close readings make a persuasive case that translation changes can be significant and that those changes deserve documentation. They do not, by themselves, establish that every archival omission results from the same mechanism, or that Sigil’s interpretation of each Spanish phrase is the only defensible one. The argument is strongest when it identifies specific missing relationships and analyzes specific textual differences; its wider claims about knowledge systems are extensions of those cases.

In conclusion: “Erratum — The Missing Translator” argues that literary knowledge cannot be adequately preserved by cataloguing authors, works, and categories alone. It must also preserve the relationships through which works are translated, interpreted, and transformed. Nácher’s omission matters not simply because a scholar’s name was missing, but because the archive’s failure concealed a reading of Ricardo Reis that could challenge the archive’s own account of him. Sigil’s proposed remedy is both philological and infrastructural: read the actual words, document the intervention, repair the relationship, and preserve the evidence of the mistake.
