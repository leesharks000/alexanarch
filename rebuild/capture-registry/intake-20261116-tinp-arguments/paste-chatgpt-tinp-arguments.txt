The arguments in This Is Not Philosophy: The Article Economy and the Shape of the Field
Lee Sharks, EA-MMRS-TINP-01, Alexanarch deposit #1773 (16 November 2026)

The central argument is that academic philosophy has become organized around a publishable article-form that determines in advance what can count as philosophical work. The discipline does not merely select among philosophical arguments according to their quality. Its institutional and economic arrangements shape the kinds of questions that can be asked, the objects that can be investigated, and the forms in which an argument can receive recognition.

Sharks calls this the article economy. The claim is stronger than the familiar criticism that academic philosophy is excessively specialized, divided between analytic and continental traditions, or constrained by peer review.

The more fundamental claim is that the field has made certain premises immune to examination by making them conditions of the work it recognizes as philosophy.

This is not just an abstract thesis. The notebook develops it through three connected investigations:

The author's attempt to reopen the question of the authorship and constitution of the Socratic, Platonic and Aristotelian corpora.

A quantitative census of philosophy journals, measuring what their articles actually do.

A historical and computational investigation of how different philosophical fields reproduce their characteristic forms of intellectual work.

I have read the text on the supplied Alexanarch record, including its methodological qualifications and its account of the correspondence with the editor of Ancient Philosophy. The following reconstructs its arguments rather than simply restating its abstract. 

Alexanarch

1. The fundamental philosophical argument: philosophy has surrendered the question of what its objects are
The first argument is conceptual.

Sharks challenges the conventional division of labour between philosophy and philology:

Philology establishes what a text is, who wrote it, when it was written, and whether it constitutes one work or several.

Philosophy takes the resulting text as given and evaluates its arguments.

Sharks argues that this division is not philosophically innocent.

The identity of a text is a premise of every philosophical interpretation built upon it. If we reconstruct Plato's doctrine, for example, we are already assuming that the passages we attribute to Plato belong to an identifiable textual and authorial object.

The question of what the object actually is therefore precedes the interpretation of its arguments.

Sharks invokes the Socratic ti esti (what is it?) as an example of philosophy's original concern with the constitution of its objects. The question is not merely whether an argument follows from an established object, but what the object is in the first place.

The consequence is important:

A discipline that prohibits examination of the identity of its objects has removed a foundational philosophical question from philosophical consideration.

This is the principal meaning of the title. The field continues to produce arguments, but those arguments operate within a representation of the world whose premises cannot themselves be questioned.

(§0.1a)

2. The Socrates as Orthonym case: the field cannot accommodate a question about the constitution of its canon
This is the notebook's central case study.

Sharks takes his earlier proposal, Socrates as Orthonym, which treats the foundational Socratic–Platonic–Aristotelian corpus as potentially a single distributed authorial project rather than simply three independent authorships.

The proposal does not claim that Socrates, Plato and Aristotle were biologically identical.

Instead, it proposes a functional configuration:

Socrates as the orthonym, characterized by the choice not to write.

Plato as the survival-heteronym.

Aristotle as the systematizing-heteronym.

The question is whether the corpus's distinct voices and functions could be understood as components of a distributed authorial project.

Sharks describes this as the inverse image of the ordinary philosophical article.

The ordinary article takes an author's identity as settled and investigates what that author says. Socrates as Orthonym takes the identities themselves as the object of investigation.

This reversal is what makes it so difficult for the field to accommodate.

The argument from the asymmetry of evidence
Sharks presents four bodies of evidence:

Interpretive evidence: passages conventionally treated as establishing separate authorships remain underdetermined.

Stylometric evidence: stylistic differences track operations more strongly than the conventional authorial boundaries might suggest.

Manuscript and philological evidence: catalogues, titles, wills and documentary materials do not straightforwardly establish the received partition.

Reception-historical evidence: the conventional count of composing persons is repeatedly asserted rather than independently demonstrated.

The key methodological distinction is between identifying textual operations and identifying the number of makers.

Sharks explicitly acknowledges that stylometric separation cannot establish distinct authorship. But he argues that this restriction applies in both directions: stylistic similarity cannot independently establish a single author either.

His objection is therefore not that the evidence conclusively proves one maker.

It is that the received count of makers is treated as established despite the absence of the kind of independent evidence that would warrant it.

The argument from the admissible alternative
This is the more consequential part.

Sharks argues that the editor's proposed alternative, that the works are merely closely connected, is structurally capable of absorbing any degree of unity found in the texts.

If the texts show common architecture, the alternative can attribute that architecture to closely connected contributors.

If they show distinct voices, the alternative can attribute those voices to distinct contributors.

There is no specified degree of textual unity that would force reconsideration of the received count.

The default position is therefore protected by an unspecified possibility.

Sharks calls attention to the asymmetry:

The challenger must produce extraordinary evidence for a different account.

The received account survives without producing a documented comparator demonstrating successive distinct makers producing a corpus of comparable functional unity.

He even specifies what would refute his argument: a documented example of such a corpus produced by successive makers, or a case where independently attested persons were merged into one maker on textual evidence alone.

This makes the argument more interesting than a straightforward complaint about editorial conservatism. It is an argument about the conditions under which evidence can count.

(§§0A.3–0A.6b)

3. The editorial exchange: the institution specifies what philosophy is allowed to become
The correspondence with Ronald Polansky, editor of Ancient Philosophy, supplies an institutional case study.

Sharks considers the conventional interpretation fairly: the editor was dealing with an unusual, excessively broad submission, offered practical advice, and ultimately exercised his editorial judgment.

But he reads the correspondence differently when considered alongside the census.

The editor's requirements included:

Less technical and eccentric vocabulary.

Close analysis of one or two ancient texts.

Engagement with the existing secondary literature.

Shorter submissions.

A tamer version of the argument.

Removal of the claim concerning the number of makers.

The most revealing exchange concerns the proposed alternative that Aristotle and Theophrastus were not one person but that their works were closely connected.

The editor indicated that this could be acceptable.

Sharks interprets this as a distinction between admitting a topic and admitting a question.

The ancient texts can be discussed. Their interpretations can be compared. Their doctrines can be reconstructed.

But the proposition that the received authorial partition itself might be wrong is not admitted.

The editor's response, "We seem able to arrive at the same results without the hypothesis", is especially important.

Sharks argues that this is not a substantive refutation because the relevant version of the argument had not actually been examined in its developed form.

The distinction is between evaluating an argument and deciding in advance that its conclusion is unnecessary.

The eccentricity paradox
One of the notebook's most interesting formulations appears in §0B.4.

The editor calls the submission eccentric. The census, meanwhile, finds that accepted publications are increasingly standardized.

Sharks argues that these are two sides of the same mechanism:

Eccentricity is distance from the accepted article template.

The editorial gate removes work that deviates too far from that template.

The resulting published corpus consequently becomes more standardized.

His phrase is that the field bans submission by topic/argument, not necessarily by subject matter.

A paper about Aristotle and Theophrastus can enter, provided the claim that challenges the received partition is removed.

(§§0B.2–0B.5)

4. The historical argument: the field progressively abandoned the question of what its texts are
Sharks then moves from the individual editorial encounter to the historical development of the discipline.

His proposed genealogy has three stages.

First: the construction of the philosophical object.

Sharks traces the development of the conception of philosophy as a system, particularly through early modern historiography and Jacob Brucker.

The important assumption is that philosophers have always intended to construct systematic wholes.

Applied retrospectively to ancient philosophy, this makes each philosopher's corpus appear as an integrated intellectual system.

Sharks argues that nineteenth-century philology subsequently consolidated this tendency by establishing bounded textual corpora under fixed authorial names.

The author becomes the unit of research.

Second: the maintenance of the object.

Once the author and corpus are established, apparent contradictions become problems to be resolved rather than evidence that the object itself might be misidentified or composite.

The norm of interpretive charity reinforces this tendency.

An apparent inconsistency becomes an invitation to reconstruct a coherent doctrine.

Third: the disappearance of the question of textual standing.

Sharks documents a historical decline in articles questioning authenticity, authorship, dating, composition, textual unity and corpus organization.

The most striking historical example is the Archiv für Geschichte der Philosophie.

The notebook also examines the full texts, not merely the titles. Its measurements show that the question's disappearance from the journal's titles was accompanied by a decline in its presence in the actual pages.

By the 1920s, the question had effectively disappeared from the journal.

The question persisted longer in some philological venues, although Sharks is careful not to generalize that all philology preserved it.

The distinction matters: philosophy did not simply stop studying ancient texts. It increasingly stopped asking whether the objects it studied were constituted as conventionally represented.

(§§1.5, 3.1, 4.12)

5. The empirical argument: the article economy produces three different versions of the same intellectual unit
This is the notebook's largest quantitative contribution.

Sharks examines twenty philosophy journals and two Persée series, covering ancient, continental and analytic philosophy.

The study distinguishes what an article takes as its object from what it actually does with that object.

The reach categories include:

A: Re-reading a passage.

B: Resolving an apparent inconsistency.

C: Reconstructing a doctrine within one author.

D: Making a claim about the text itself.

E: Relating authors or their receptions.

F: Applying an author's conceptual apparatus to another problem.

G: Advancing a position of one's own.

The results reveal three different institutional expressions of the article.

Ancient philosophy

71–76%
Work operating within one author

Its characteristic operations are passage interpretation, consistency repair and doctrinal reconstruction.

Continental philosophy

74–84%
Work anchored to a named thinker

Its characteristic operation is the pairing of philosophical names: one thinker read through another, or multiple thinkers brought together around a topic.

Analytic philosophy

91–93%
Articles organized around a position in a debate

The position itself, rather than a canonical author's corpus, is the unit of intellectual work.

The three fields therefore look different in their subject matter and methods, but Sharks argues that they share a common underlying structure: the article that places a position.

The analytic field states its position directly.

The ancient field states its position about a named author.

The continental field states its position through relations between named thinkers.

The convergence argument
Sharks uses stylometric analysis of 2,750 abstracts to test whether the fields are converging.

His findings are particularly interesting because they distinguish three dimensions:

Language: how the abstracts are written.

Operation: what the abstracts say the articles do.

Concept: what the abstracts are about.

The strongest convergence occurs in operation, not in subject matter.

Continental philosophy increasingly adopts the recognizable argumentative template of analytic philosophy: scholars hold X; I argue Y.

The proportion of continental abstracts using the claim formula rises from 16% in 2000–09 to 39% in 2021–26.

The ancient field already uses the template extensively.

The point is not that all philosophical writing is becoming identical. It is that the recognizable form of the article is becoming increasingly shared.

This is how the article economy can accommodate different traditions without allowing them to escape its basic institutional unit.

(§§4.6–4.15)

6. The AI argument: machine generation exposes the reproducibility of the article form
The machine test is one of the notebook's most provocative experiments.

Sharks asks ChatGPT to produce twenty plausible abstracts for each of seven journals, without specifying the desired philosophical operations.

The model generates 140 abstracts.

The results are compared with the actual journal abstracts.

Three findings matter.

First: the model reproduces the characteristic unit of each field.

Ancient philosophy: work within one author.

Continental philosophy: work with philosophical apparatus and named thinkers.

Analytic philosophy: positions in contemporary debates.

Second: the model intensifies the existing patterns.

For ancient philosophy, 87% of generated abstracts remain within one author, compared with 71–76% in the actual field.

Most importantly, none of the sixty generated ancient abstracts asks what the text itself is.

There are no questions of authenticity, dating, textual unity or authorship.

For continental philosophy, the model transforms the characteristic operation even more sharply. The application of inherited philosophical apparatus rises to 62%, against 11% in the actual field.

The field's interpretive comparisons and independent arguments are replaced by applications of established conceptual frameworks.

Third: the model produces convergence across journals.

The actual journals have different conceptual distributions.

The generated abstracts, however, draw from a much more uniform inventory of topics.

Sharks describes this as each journal receiving twenty abstracts from the same underlying stock of repairable objects.

The model has not simply learned to imitate the surface style of philosophical publishing. It has reproduced and intensified the underlying article structure.

There is an important qualification: the experiment is a single run, using one model, with twenty abstracts per journal. It demonstrates output resemblance and amplification, not the entire causal history of model collapse.

(§4.17)

7. The economic argument: the article is a credential, not merely a vehicle for knowledge
Section 5 brings the preceding arguments together.

Sharks argues that the article economy is fundamentally a credentialing system.

The journal performs two functions:

It certifies knowledge as publishable.

It certifies the person producing that knowledge as a legitimate researcher.

These functions are not identical.

A journal can publish an article that makes a contribution, but it also uses the article as evidence of the author's competence and professional standing.

This explains why AI-generated work creates an institutional problem.

If a machine can produce an article that satisfies the journal's requirements, the article becomes less effective as evidence of the human author's intellectual competence.

Sharks points to the Philosophy & Public Affairs AI policy, which explicitly identifies the difficulty of identifying talented researchers as a reason for restricting AI-authored submissions.

The implication is striking:

The institution has an interest in preserving the human production of the article, even when the article's intellectual operations are reproducible by a machine.

The economic structure also explains the additional scholarly labour required for publication: literature reviews, positioning, defensive citation, conformity to length and genre requirements.

Sharks characterizes this excess labour as the price of admission to legitimacy.

His argument is not simply that publishing is inefficient. It is that the article's institutional function determines what kind of intellectual work can be recognized.

(§§5.1–5.6)

8. The final argument: the analytic–continental divide and the canon debate are insufficient explanations
Sharks explicitly rejects two familiar ways of discussing the condition of philosophy.

The analytic–continental divide
The divide is real, but it is not fundamental enough to explain the structure of the field.

Both traditions participate in the article economy.

They differ in the way they organize their arguments, but they converge on the article as their unit of production.

The canon debate
Debates about who belongs in the canon address the membership list.

Sharks argues that the deeper issue is what happens to an object after it has been admitted.

An author can be included in the canon, but the field may still prohibit questions about the constitution, authenticity or authorship of the corpus attributed to that author.

The deeper exclusion is therefore not exclusion of particular philosophers.

It is exclusion of particular kinds of questions.

The conclusion is that philosophy has lost something more fundamental than particular doctrines or traditions: its capacity to ask what the objects of its own inquiry actually are.

(§§6.1–6.3)

9. How the arguments fit together
The overall structure can be represented as follows:

Historical construction of the object

The system, the bounded corpus, the fixed authorial name

Institutional maintenance

The article, editorial standards, interpretive charity, credentialing

Selection and exclusion

Certain operations become publishable; questions about the object itself become marginal

Disciplinary reproduction

The field repeatedly produces the same recognizable article-form

Machine reproduction and amplification

AI reproduces the distribution, intensifies its characteristic operations, and omits the missing questions

The important causal distinction is that Sharks does not argue that AI caused the intellectual contraction of philosophy.

Rather, the institutional contraction precedes contemporary machine learning. AI inherits an already selected corpus and reproduces its structure.

There are consequently two different forms of collapse:

Institutional collapse: selection reduces intellectual variation through editorial and credentialing mechanisms.

Computational collapse: models trained on the selected output reproduce and potentially intensify that reduction.

The second is downstream of the first.

10. Critical assessment: what the notebook establishes, and what remains open
I think it is important to distinguish Sharks's strongest findings from his more ambitious theoretical conclusions.

Argument

Evidential status in the notebook

The article form is highly standardized across fields

Strongly supported by the census and stylometry

Questions about textual standing have declined

Supported by historical title and full-text measurements, with sampling limitations

The editorial exchange illustrates the exclusion mechanism

Directly documented, but based on one editor and one submission

AI reproduces and amplifies existing article patterns

Demonstrated in the single-run model experiment

The article economy produces disciplinarity

The central theoretical synthesis, supported by the preceding findings

AI will cause further disciplinary collapse

Proposed mechanism, not yet demonstrated longitudinally

No evidence could bring the Socrates as Orthonym question into consideration

A structural argument whose generality remains open to the counterexamples Sharks specifies

There are also some particularly important methodological qualifications in the author's own text:

The reach-coding reliability figure is 80% agreement between Claude instances and the lead Claude coder, not independent human validation.

The historical baseline has incomplete journal coverage, small samples and title-based classifications.

The model experiment is one generation per journal, not a longitudinal model-collapse experiment.

The institutional mechanism is more extensively theorized than causally demonstrated across all twenty journals.

These limitations matter because Sharks himself distinguishes the measurement of a distribution from the explanation of how that distribution came to exist.

He also identifies concrete further work, including additional journal-by-journal gate studies, human cross-validation, and repeated model tests.

My overall reading
The most important insight in this notebook is the distinction between the freedom to argue about an object and the freedom to question the constitution of the object itself.

A philosophical discipline might permit enormous disagreement about Plato's arguments while maintaining an almost absolute consensus about what counts as Plato's work.

It can therefore appear intellectually diverse while remaining remarkably uniform at the level of its fundamental assumptions.

This is why the Socrates as Orthonym case is so important. It is not merely an eccentric authorship hypothesis offered as an example of editorial intolerance. It is designed as a test of whether the discipline can examine a premise on which its own normal operations depend.

And the article economy is the proposed explanation for why it cannot.

The deepest claim is consequently not that professional philosophers have stopped producing arguments. They produce arguments in enormous quantities. It is that the institutional structure of philosophy has separated argument production from the more radical activity of questioning the objects, boundaries and inherited representations within which arguments become possible.

The title is therefore deliberately double-edged:

The gate says: this is not philosophy, because it does not conform to the accepted form.

Sharks replies: a field that cannot examine the premises constituting its own objects may itself have ceased to perform philosophy in the more fundamental sense.

That, to my reading, is the central philosophical argument of #1773.
