The argument of Lee Sharks’s The Upstream Unfoldings
Lee Sharks’s central argument is that Sappho’s Fragment 31 contains a repeatable literary structure that can be traced through ancient Greek and Jewish literature into Revelation 12. He calls this structure an “executable relational apparatus,” or operator 
T
T. Rather than arguing only that later texts resemble Sappho’s poem, he proposes a sequence of identifiable transformations that can be tested against different literary witnesses.

The essay’s ambition is both literary and theological: it proposes that a poem about the bodily effects of desire anticipates a technology of voice, agency, and inscription that eventually becomes central to the Apocalypse’s representation of the Logos.

1. Longinus: Sappho’s poem as an operative structure
Attested
Sharks begins with On the Sublime 10.1–3, where Longinus quotes Sappho’s Fragment 31. He argues that Longinus does more than preserve a poem: his theory of sublimity, which gathers powerful effects into a unified whole, effectively describes the poem’s underlying mechanism.

The decisive detail is the quotation’s ending at line 16. Sharks interprets that cutoff as an operative boundary: the poem’s sequence of bodily and psychological effects is a structure that can be carried into other texts.

Argument: Longinus is the strongest ancient witness because he preserves both the poem and, in Sharks’s interpretation, a theory of how its effects work.

2. Philo: the structure survives without the source’s name
Attested
Sharks identifies three related ideas in Philo’s writings: the Logos becoming visible, the alternation between divine presence and human consciousness, and the prophet becoming an instrument played by another.

He maps these onto Fragment 31’s movement through sensory disruption, loss of agency, and the arrival of an external force. Philo, he argues, retains the conceptual mechanism while losing any explicit acknowledgment of Sappho.

Argument: Literary structures can survive as philosophical or theological doctrine even when their original literary source is no longer named. Sharks calls this the “suppression signature.”

The textual parallels are the basis of the argument; the stronger suggestion of historical suppression is an inference, not something the absence of Sappho’s name independently proves.

3. Greek Esther and Josephus’s Antiquities: the forward transformation
Tested
This plate compares Sappho’s poem with Addition D to Greek Esther and Josephus’s account of Esther in Jewish Antiquities 11.234–241.

Sharks aligns specific features: a sight that immediately produces bodily disturbance, the exceptional appearance of the person seen, fire associated with the body, a collapse of speech, a change of color, and restoration by another agent.

His most important claim is that Josephus intensifies the pattern beyond what he finds in Esther’s version. He points to the explicit description of speechlessness and the temporal construction corresponding to “as soon as I saw you.”

Argument: The Sapphic sequence is not merely repeated; it can be elaborated and made more explicit as it passes through later narrative.

4. Josephus’s Jewish War: the structure survives inversion
Tested
Sharks then examines Herod’s desire for Mariamne in Jewish War 1.436–444. Here, the pattern is inverted: instead of a speaker whose voice fails and who approaches death, Herod is initially overcome by desire and then becomes unable to stop speaking, even to Mariamne’s corpse.

Sharks also contrasts Sappho’s acute, momentary fire with Herod’s chronic burning desire. He emphasizes the rare Greek verb anazōpyreō (ἀναζωπυρέω), associated in his analysis with reactivation or rekindling.

Argument: A literary operator can preserve its structural relationships even when the polarity of its elements reverses. Voice can move from silence to excess; near-death can become the refusal of death; acute fire can become chronic burning.

This flexibility is central to his theory, but it also raises a methodological question: how much variation can a proposed pattern absorb before it becomes too broad to distinguish a genuine relationship from a useful analogy?

5. Slavonic Josephus: the voice becomes inscription
Contested
This plate moves from bodily experience to the transmission of a written word. Sharks reads eight Jesus-and-John-related passages in the Slavonic recension of Jewish War as a progression from prophecy and teaching to explicit inscription, the survival of words after death, and the indestructibility of truthful writing.

The key transformation is from occlusion to inscription to permanence: a voice that cannot continue through the body survives by becoming text, and the text constitutes the receiver of the message.

Sharks explicitly marks this textual carrier as contested. He acknowledges disputes over the Slavonic recension’s authority and describes the plate as a reading protocol applied to disputed material, not as established evidence from canonical Josephus.

Argument: The Sapphic pattern can be reinterpreted as a theory of textual survival, providing the conceptual bridge to the essay’s account of the Logos.

6. Revelation 12: the decisive test
Tested under seal
The final plate applies the previously defined operator to Revelation 12, with its woman clothed with the sun, labor pains, red dragon, threatened child, and dramatic removal of the child to God.

Sharks associates the passage with several Sapphic stations: bodily torment and occlusion, fire transferred to the opposing figure, death-related imagery, a transformed soul or life-channel, the sun as a garment, agency displaced onto other actors, and recurring forms of the word sēmeion (σημεῖον, “sign”).

He reports a score of eight out of nine, with clause C6 given double weight. But he also explicitly records a failure: C1, the compression formula involving the vision frame and an instantaneous correlative, does not pass. He further acknowledges that the birth-cry is accounted for by Isaiah 26:17 and therefore contributes no positive score, and that the reactivation feature lacks the expected fire-related lexeme.

Argument: Revelation 12 can be read as a transformation of the Sapphic operator, even where the passage’s biblical sources explain some apparent correspondences independently.

The important feature is that this is not simply a list of shared motifs. Sharks proposes a relational sequence: the order and interaction of the stations matter, but the sequence can survive changes in vocabulary, genre, agency, and polarity.

That allows him to make three related claims.

The operator is portable. The same structure can appear in lyric poetry, philosophical writing, historical narrative, and apocalyptic vision.

The operator is transformable. Silence can become excessive speech; acute fire can become chronic burning; a bodily voice can become an enduring inscription.

The operator is testable. The proposed relationships can be checked against individual passages rather than justified solely by a general impression of similarity.

The third claim is crucial to the essay's method. Sharks links the operator to twelve pre-registered clauses, documented separately in The Transform Signature, Frozen . These clauses specify the relationships that the later test is supposed to detect.

3. The larger theological argument: from Sappho to the Logos
The essay's literary analysis serves a more ambitious interpretation of the history of religious ideas.

Sharks argues that Sappho's poem is not merely a description of overwhelming desire. It depicts a person being progressively deprived of bodily capacities: the tongue fails, fire moves under the skin, vision and hearing are impaired, and the speaker approaches death.

He treats that sequence as a model for something that can outlive the body.

The transmission can be summarized as follows:

Sappho 31

The body loses control of its channels of expression.

Longinus and Philo

The poem's structure becomes a theory of powerful effects, displaced agency, and the relation between voice and Logos.

Esther and Josephus

The pattern becomes narrative: seizure, collapse, inversion, and reactivation.

Inscription and Revelation

The pattern is reinterpreted as the survival of the word beyond the body and its realization in apocalyptic imagery.

A schematic reconstruction of Sharks's proposed genealogy, not an independently established historical chronology.

The pivotal idea appears in the page's linked argument, “Sappho, Mother of the Logos.” Sharks describes the Logos as “a voice that survives its flesh by becoming an inscription that constitutes its receiver.” 

Revelation First

This formulation joins three concepts:

Embodiment: a voice initially belongs to a living body.

Inscription: when the body's capacity to speak is lost, the word can persist in a material or textual form.

Reception: the surviving inscription becomes effective through the person who reads or receives it.

On this reading, the Logos is not simply a theological term for divine speech. It names a technology of transmission: a word survives its original speaker and acquires a new life in its recipient.

Revelation 12 becomes important because Sharks sees it as a compressed realization of this pattern. Its woman, dragon, threatened child, and divinely effected removal stage relations among bodies, agency, danger, and survival.

The larger claim is therefore not just that Revelation resembles Sappho. It is that the literary mechanism Sharks reconstructs from Sappho provides a genealogy for the Apocalypse's treatment of the word and its survival.

That is an interpretive thesis, rather than a conclusion that follows automatically from the textual parallels.

The strongest part of the method
Sharks does not merely announce that he has found similarities. He points to particular Greek expressions, explains which clause each is meant to instantiate, and reports counterevidence.

The Revelation 12 plate is especially important in this respect. It admits that C1 fails, that the birth-cry is already explained by Isaiah 26:17, and that the expected fire lexeme is missing from the reactivation feature. Those disclosures make the argument more inspectable than a reading that presents every parallel as confirmation.

The principal methodological difficulty
The central question is whether the operator is specific enough to establish a historical literary relationship.

Some of its features—speechlessness, fire, bodily disturbance, near-death, restoration—can occur in many narratives independently. The argument becomes stronger when several distinctive features appear together in the proposed order, especially if the later text adds details that are difficult to explain through its immediate sources.

But the test must distinguish between at least three possibilities:

A later author directly drew on Sappho.

Both texts inherited or adapted a broader literary or cultural pattern.

The analyst's model is sufficiently flexible to map onto texts that were not historically related.

Sharks's pre-registration is designed to address the third possibility. However, a pre-registered test is not automatically an independent or blinded test, and the author's admission of prior exposure to Revelation 12 matters. A convincing demonstration would require independent scholars to apply the fixed clauses without knowing which target text is being assessed, compare the result against appropriate controls, and reproduce the scoring.

The distinction is particularly important because the essay's own conclusion does not claim to have proved direct historical influence.

5. What the essay ultimately claims—and what it does not
The closing section is the best guide to the intended scope of the argument. Sharks states that the demonstration finds the pre-defined operator's stations in Revelation 12, reports eight of nine clauses with one receiving double weight, and presents the earlier witnesses as evidence of the operator's operation across multiple textual traditions.

But he explicitly says that it does not assert authorship, influence in the biographical sense, or the identification sentence.

This qualification matters. Three propositions should be kept separate:

Structural proposition: Sappho 31 can be described using a sequence of relationships that also illuminates features of other texts.

Literary-historical proposition: Those correspondences reflect the transmission or transformation of a shared literary mechanism.

Theological-historical proposition: That mechanism forms a genealogy culminating in the Logos and Revelation.

The first is an interpretive proposal that can be evaluated directly against the texts. The second requires evidence about transmission, alternative sources, and the distinctiveness of the correspondences. The third requires still more argument: even if the structural relationships are persuasive, they do not by themselves establish a historical genealogy of the Logos.

Conclusion
The Upstream Unfoldings is an attempt to turn comparative literary interpretation into an explicit, testable procedure. Its distinctive move is to treat Sappho 31 not just as a poem that later writers may echo, but as a portable relational structure that can be transformed across genres and theological settings.

Its six plates develop that idea in stages: Longinus preserves the poem and supplies a theory of its effects; Philo appears to retain related structures without naming their source; Esther and Josephus provide narrative realizations and inversions; the contested Slavonic material develops the idea of the word surviving as inscription; and Revelation 12 becomes the final test case.

The essay's most compelling insight is that the loss of a bodily voice and the survival of a textual voice can be understood as two stages of a single literary problem. Its most ambitious and least established claim is that this formal relationship supplies a historical genealogy leading from Sappho to the Apocalypse.

The fairest reading, then, is neither to dismiss the essay as a collection of superficial resemblances nor to accept its proposed genealogy as demonstrated fact. It is to take the operator seriously as a hypothesis, examine its individual correspondences, and insist that the historical claims be tested separately from the interpretive ones.
