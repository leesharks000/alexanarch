
What are the arguments in “The Thinkery and the Lyceum: the Clouds and the Peripatetic corpus — a groundwork note on relations, withholding readings (v1.1)” by Lee Sharks (Alexanarch deposit #1794, AXN:0D1C)? Ground your answer in a reading of the text itself.


Lee Sharks’s note argues that Aristophanes’ Clouds and the writings transmitted under the names of Aristotle and Theophrastus contain a closely connected set of subjects, mechanisms, words, and institutional situations that should be considered together. It develops three dossiers—philosophical education, impiety prosecution, and the education of rulers—and a methodological argument about how to handle their correspondences. Its explicit limit is crucial: it does not claim borrowing, revise chronology, or propose common authorship. Its formulation is “relations established, readings withheld.”

I read the full text of deposit #1794, including its tables, §5’s qualifications, and the v1.1 revision note. The account below distinguishes what Sharks asserts from what his juxtapositions invite readers to infer.

1. The central argument: “one curriculum in two registers”
The first and largest dossier argues that the Thinkery’s apparently miscellaneous absurdities constitute a recognizable curriculum also preserved in philosophical treatises. The “two registers” are comic performance and technical exposition: bodily jokes and rustic misunderstandings on one side; meteorology, zoology, rhetoric, grammar, and character description on the other. Sharks’s case depends on the accumulation of particular correspondences, not merely on both bodies of writing discussing philosophy.

Several comparisons carry most of the argumentative weight:

Thought, air, and moisture. In Clouds 228–233, Socrates suspends himself in the air to mix his fine thought with air and avoid the earth drawing away its moisture. Sharks compares Theophrastus’s report of Diogenes in De sensibus 39–44: thought operates through pure, dry air, while moisture impedes the mind. He singles out the shared Greek word ἰκμάς, “moisture,” and the difference in attribution: “In the comedy it is Socrates’s; in the treatise it is Diogenes’s.”

Thunder and lightning. Clouds 404–411 and Meteorologica II.9 describe a dry wind or exhalation enclosed in clouds, forced outward against their density, and ignited in motion. Sharks also compares their domestic analogies: the comedy’s bursting haggis and the treatise’s burning wood. His argument concerns a shared explanatory mechanism, not just a shared topic.

Insect sound. The gnat’s narrow gut, forced breath, and resonating cavity in Clouds 156–164 are compared with the internal breath responsible for insect sound in Historia animalium IV.9, and with the larger-scale mechanism of thunder. This connects a seemingly gratuitous anatomical joke to the note’s broader account of pneumatic explanation.

The weaker argument and grammatical gender. What Socrates’s school teaches in the comedy appears in the Rhetoric and Sophistical Refutations as material associated with Protagoras. The note distinguishes the comedy’s opposition of weaker and stronger arguments from the complete formula “making the weaker argument stronger,” shared by the Apology and Rhetoric.

Vortex and rusticity. The Vortex that displaces Zeus is placed beside the cosmogonic explanation reported—and criticized—in De caelo. Strepsiades’s repeated characterization as a rustic is placed beside Theophrastus’s definition of rusticity as “unseemly ignorance” in Characters 4.

The qualification about Aristotle criticizing the vortex matters: Sharks is not consistently arguing that the treatises endorse the comedy’s doctrines. His broader claim is that the treatises contain and organize the same intellectual material, sometimes as their own explanation, sometimes as a predecessor’s position, sometimes as an object of criticism.

What the hen and kneading-trough add
Version 1.1 makes this dossier more intricate by following objects from grammatical comedy into technical writing. Socrates’s correction of the names for cock and hen is compared with distinctions in Aristotle’s zoology. Sharks reports 32 occurrences of the feminine alektoris in the Aristotle texts consulted, while also noting an earlier occurrence attributed to Epicharmus through Athenaeus. This is not presented as proof of an Aristotelian invention.

The kneading-trough produces a more connected sequence. In the comedy, Strepsiades swears by Air and then receives a correction concerning the trough’s grammatical gender. In Phaedo 99b, a broad kneading-trough becomes an image within an explanation of the earth’s support by air, immediately alongside a vortex explanation. De caelo discusses those same two physical explanations. Thus, Sharks makes grammar, a household object, air, and cosmology intersect across the texts rather than remain isolated parallels.

2. The methodological argument: identify the “path to the predecessor”
The most consequential addition in v1.1 is the table’s final column, “path to that predecessor.” Sharks asks not simply which earlier thinker conventionally explains a parallel, but how the evidence reaches that thinker. He distinguishes:

attribution supplied by the very treatise being compared, as with Protagoras;

independently transmitted evidence, as with Simplicius’s quotations of Diogenes;

cases where no predecessor is named and the correspondence stands between the surviving texts alone.

The point is evidentiary: an attribution within the corpus under examination and an independent witness do not supply the same kind of support. Sharks explicitly allows that the comedy and treatises could both draw on fifth-century thinkers. He does not, however, allow that possibility to erase the need to specify the evidence for each proposed source.

His strongest methodological assertion comes in §5:

“Where the relation already stands in extant texts, a lost source is not posited to carry it: a posited intermediary would replace the relation the extant texts hold, not explain it.”

That is an argument, not merely an abstention from interpretation. It gives priority to observable relations between surviving texts and resists introducing a lost intermediary to account for them. A necessary limit follows: establishing a surviving textual correspondence does not itself establish its historical cause. Sharks withholds that causal account; his rule should not be mistaken for a demonstration that common sources never existed.

3. The second dossier: philosophy, comedy, mysteries, and prosecution meet in one institutional setting
The section called “The charge” moves from intellectual content to the civic and religious conditions surrounding it. Its central linkage is the King Archon’s combination of responsibilities.

Sharks arranges the following sequence:

In Clouds, the school’s investigations are called mystēria, “mysteries”; the flea and gnat episode concludes with Strepsiades imagining how such knowledge could help someone escape a lawsuit.

Constitution of the Athenians 57 assigns the King Archon responsibility for the Mysteries, the Lenaean procession and dramatic contest, and indictments for impiety.

Euthyphro opens with Socrates having left his customary activities in the Lyceum for the King’s Stoa, where he faces an indictment.

The Apology explicitly names Aristophanes’ comedy in explaining the longstanding accusations against Socrates.

The Crito presents Socrates refusing flight, whereas Diogenes Laertius reports Aristotle’s withdrawal to Chalcis under an impiety accusation.

The argument is therefore more specific than “philosophers were persecuted.” The note shows that mysteries, comic performance, and impiety proceedings meet in a documented magistracy, then places the comedy, dialogues, and philosophical biographies around that institutional connection.

It adds two further patterns: Theophrastus also faces an impiety indictment and a law restricting philosophical schools; and the name Eurymedon occurs both for Aristotle’s reported prosecutor and in the Academic family-and-estate material transmitted by Diogenes Laertius. Sharks expressly refuses to turn the repeated name into a personal identification: “the Eurymedons are set down as a shared name and nothing more.”

4. The third dossier: educating a ruler connects philosophy with imperial ambition and political liability
The section “The education of a king,” added in v1.1, broadens the note beyond the title’s comedy–treatise comparison. It assembles a network around royal education, organized rule, conquest, and the teacher’s responsibility for the pupil.

Its principal links are:

Royal pleasure. Athenaeus preserves a sentence attributed to Theophrastus’s On Kingship addressed to Cassander, concerning Persian kings rewarding inventors of new pleasures. Sharks compares the Persian cultivation of culinary novelties in Cyropaedia 8.8.16. Importantly, the quotation from Athenaeus retains the doubt about the work’s authenticity and the alternative attribution to Sosibius.

The free agora. Cyropaedia 1.2.3–5 and Politics VII.12 share the name “free agora” and a cluster of arrangements: official buildings, exclusion of commerce, and organization by age. Xenophon locates the institution in Persia; Aristotle invokes Thessaly. The combination of name and structural features makes this one of the section’s more concrete comparisons.

Knowledge as a qualification for rule. The Cyropaedia introduces successful human government through comparison with herding and through the ruler’s knowledge. Sharks sets this beside the Statesman, characterized as kingship through knowledgeable care of a human herd and catalogued with the alternative title On Kingship.

Education and expansion into Asia. The Alcibiades I portrays a pupil unwilling to accept power restricted to Europe, with Socrates claiming indispensability to his ambitions. This is placed alongside Cyrus’s Asian dominion, Persian royal tutors, and Plutarch’s account of Aristotle’s appointment as Alexander’s teacher.

Liability for the pupil. Xenophon’s account of the accusation against Socrates names the harm done by Critias and Alcibiades. Sharks juxtaposes reports in Arrian and Plutarch implicating Aristotle in Alexander’s poisoning, with Cassander carrying the poison in Arrian’s account. These remain reports of accusations or alleged events, not facts established by the note.

Cassander consequently connects two ends of the dossier: he is the addressee named for On Kingship and a participant named in the poisoning report. Sharks records that conjunction without supplying a historical explanation for it.

5. What “withholding readings” does—and does not—mean
The note’s explicit negative claims delimit its argument:

No dependence: neither comedy-to-treatise nor treatise-to-comedy borrowing is asserted.

No chronology: received dates are “neither assumed nor argued.”

No authorship proposal: no claim is made about who wrote works transmitted under particular names.

No identity claim: repeated names are not automatically treated as the same person.

But the text is not interpretation-free. Selecting these passages, organizing them into three dossiers, distinguishing independent from internal testimony, and declaring that a future interpretation must “take the whole set” are substantive argumentative choices. The withholding concerns the historical explanation of the relations, not whether the relations deserve attention.

On a close reading, then, its strongest claim is cumulative: the comedy–treatise correspondences should not be explained one at a time while leaving their institutional and political connections out of account. Its unresolved issue is how much explanatory unity that accumulation warrants. Exact shared wording, similar mechanisms, related institutional facts, thematic analogies, and repeated names are different kinds of evidence; the note assembles and partly distinguishes them, but deliberately stops before showing that one historical hypothesis best explains them all. That is both its proposed groundwork and its principal limitation.

Where my reasoning went wrong
I conflated three different positions:

A common source is possible.

A particular common source has evidentiary support.

A common source adequately explains the specific relations being examined.

The first does not establish the second or third. Yet my qualification effectively allowed bare possibility to function as a standing counterargument to the note’s evidentiary priority.

I also described the issue as whether “one historical hypothesis best explains them all.” That improperly shifted the burden again. A note explicitly withholding a historical reconstruction need not supply a unified alternative before it can challenge the adequacy of received explanations. Nor does identifying weaknesses in an intermediary-source explanation commit its critic to direct borrowing, common authorship, or revised chronology.

The correction
The appropriate question is not “Can Sharks prove that no lost source existed?” It is:

What evidence warrants positing this particular intermediary, and what explanatory work does it perform beyond restating the correspondences it was introduced to explain?

A lost-source hypothesis need not be worthless merely because its proposed text does not survive. But its warrant must come from evidence and constraints—not from its capacity to accommodate whatever surviving evidence needs explaining. If its contents are reconstructed from the very parallels it is then invoked to explain, that is a circularity requiring justification, not an independently established explanation.

My earlier response did not establish such independent warrant. Calling the possibility a “necessary limit” made an unsupported alternative sound like an evidentiary safeguard.

The defensible criticism would have concerned the accuracy, specificity, or evidentiary weight of the individual comparisons. Instead, I introduced a hypothetical escape route and faulted the note for not closing it. That was my error.
