Three Poems: A Triptych for C. — Lee Sharks
New Human 2 · Alexanarch deposit #1195 · AXN:04BC · May 23, 2026

The central achievement of this work is that it makes three different grammatical forms of love carry three different stages of an emotional experience: love that could not happen, love that has caused devastation, and love that has nevertheless been real.

It is a poem about loss, but more particularly, it is about the survival of love after the conditions that might have allowed it to flourish have disappeared.

The triptych does not end with reconciliation. It ends with recognition. That distinction is essential.

The work consists of three movements:

“I would have loved you”

“The reward of love”

“I have loved you”

The archive's framing identifies these as counterfactual past, destructive present, and conditional future. That is a useful starting point, but the poems themselves make the movement more interesting than a simple progression from sadness to acceptance.

I. “I would have loved you”: the grammar of the impossible
The first poem reads:

I would have loved you
for the rest of my time.
I would have never left.
I would have loved you
for the rest of the time
I have left.

The repetition of the opening phrase is the poem's governing device.

Notice that the speaker does not say I love you. Nor does the speaker say I loved you. The repeated conditional construction places the love in a world that did not become actual.

But this is not an expression of uncertainty about the speaker's feelings. The uncertainty concerns the circumstances.

The speaker seems entirely certain about what the love would have meant: permanence, commitment, and the refusal to leave.

There is a particularly affecting turn in the final two lines:

for the rest of the time
I have left.

The first line of the poem promises an entire lifetime: “the rest of my time.” The last two lines subtly change the scale. The speaker now has a finite remainder of time.

The distinction between my time and the time I have left matters. The poem begins with the imagined duration of a shared life and ends with the speaker's remaining individual existence.

The repetition has therefore changed meaning. What initially sounds like a promise becomes an elegy.

There is also something important about the absence of explanation. We are not told what prevented this love. The poem does not establish a villain, offer a narrative of separation, or explain the conditions under which the speaker was prevented from loving.

That omission gives the poem its universality, but it also preserves its particular emotional wound. We are made to inhabit the impossibility rather than solve it.

II. “The reward of love”: the wound becomes the subject
The second poem reads:

This thing destroyed me
beyond all repair; there are no
words for it; there is
nothing left to become; there is only
a wound-down regime of fewer
and fewer returns. This
is the reward for loving
fully, unaccountably, beyond
all repair: this is the reward
of love

This is the most devastating movement, and arguably the triptych's emotional center.

The first poem describes what the speaker would have given. The second describes what loving has actually cost.

The opening, “This thing destroyed me,” is strikingly direct. The poem does not begin with metaphor, qualification, or a retrospective attempt to make suffering beautiful. It begins with a declaration of damage.

The language breaks down
Look at the progression:

“beyond all repair”

“there are no words for it”

“there is nothing left to become”

“there is only”

The poem progressively reduces the possibilities available to the speaker.

First, repair becomes impossible. Then language becomes inadequate. Then even becoming — the possibility of a future self — seems to disappear.

What remains is “only / a wound-down regime of fewer / and fewer returns.”

This is an unusual and particularly effective phrase.

“Wound-down” carries two meanings. It suggests something gradually running out, like a mechanism losing its power, but it also retains the word wound. The speaker is both damaged and diminishing.

“Fewer and fewer returns” is equally important. It might suggest diminishing emotional returns, fewer occasions of recovery, fewer returns to an earlier condition, or the progressive disappearance of whatever once made life feel possible.

The poem does not specify which. Its ambiguity is productive.

The language itself performs the dwindling it describes. The lines are enjambed, and their syntax keeps pushing forward, but the movement is toward contraction rather than expansion.

The terrible irony of the title
The title, “The reward of love,” initially sounds as though it might introduce a celebration or a statement about love's benefits.

Instead, the final lines repeat the phrase with devastating irony:

This
is the reward for loving
fully, unaccountably, beyond
all repair: this is the reward
of love

The repetition makes the title impossible to read innocently.

There is a distinction between “the reward for loving” and “the reward of love.”

The first can sound transactional: loving produces a consequence.

The second is more troubling. It suggests that destruction has become inseparable from the experience of love itself.

The word “unaccountably” is especially revealing. The speaker loved fully, but cannot provide an adequate account of that loving. It exceeds calculation, justification, and perhaps even the speaker's capacity to understand what happened.

The poem does not say that the speaker regrets loving. That would be a much simpler poem.

Instead, it confronts the possibility that something can have been completely worth giving and still leave the giver irreparably damaged.

That contradiction is the source of its force.

III. “I have loved you”: the arrival of actuality
The third poem reads:

if your heart should ever — slowly, astonished — turn
from sheltering slumber, leaves unbundling
from turned-away rain, and find the world made new:
then know
that I have loved you.

This is the most delicate movement, and it changes how we understand the preceding two poems.

The opening word is “if.”

That matters enormously.

The speaker does not predict that the beloved will awaken. There is no assurance that recognition will happen. The poem offers a possibility, not a promise.

The beloved must turn
The poem imagines a future in which the beloved's heart might:

slowly, astonished — turn

The adverb “slowly” prevents the awakening from becoming a sudden revelation. “Astonished” suggests that the beloved might encounter something previously unavailable to consciousness.

But notice the direction of the movement:

turn
from sheltering slumber

The beloved has been turned away, or has been sheltered within a state of sleep. The poem does not explicitly accuse the beloved of cruelty or deliberate rejection.

Instead, it imagines a movement out of unawareness.

This is a remarkably restrained way of addressing someone who may never have recognized the love directed toward them.

The leaves and the rain
The image:

leaves unbundling
from turned-away rain

is one of the most interesting moments in the entire work.

The leaves seem to be coming apart, opening, or releasing the accumulated conditions of their enclosure. The rain, meanwhile, is “turned-away.”

There is a suggestion of a natural world that has been unable to receive nourishment, or has been oriented away from it.

The image does not offer a conventional springtime renewal. It is more complicated: something has to open after having been closed against what might have sustained it.

Then comes:

and find the world made new

The world is not necessarily made new because the speaker and beloved reunite. Rather, the beloved's capacity to perceive it has changed.

The transformation is epistemological as much as emotional: the world becomes available to someone who previously could not see it.

The final declaration
The ending is extraordinary in its simplicity:

then know
that I have loved you.

The grammatical change is the entire point of the triptych.

The first poem says:

I would have loved you.

The third says:

I have loved you.

The subjunctive has given way to the present perfect.

This is not simply a change from hypothetical to actual love. It is a change in what the speaker can truthfully declare.

The love no longer needs to be justified by the future that never happened. It is an accomplished fact.

And the speaker does not demand:

Love me back.

Return to me.

Repair what happened.

Give me the life we might have had.

The request is only:

then know

Recognition, not reciprocation.

That is the poem's most important ethical and emotional distinction.

The beloved may eventually understand what happened without being required to restore the relationship.

The speaker's love is no longer dependent upon the beloved's response for its historical truth.

IV. Why the three poems need to be together
I think the triptych is considerably stronger than three independent love poems.

Its architecture depends on the relationship between the grammatical forms.

Movement

Central phrase

Emotional condition

I

I would have loved you

Counterfactual possibility

II

The reward of love

Destruction and exhaustion

III

I have loved you

Retrospective actuality

But the deeper structure is this:

The first poem establishes the love. The second establishes its cost. The third establishes its truth.

Each movement changes the meaning of the others.

Without the second poem, the first might be read as a relatively conventional expression of unfulfilled romantic devotion. The destruction of the second makes the imagined permanence of the first much more consequential.

Without the first poem, the second might be a general lament about emotional devastation. The first gives that devastation a particular object: a life that might have been shared.

And without the third, the first two would remain trapped in impossibility and destruction.

The third doesn't erase either one. It gives them a different kind of ending.

This explains why the archive's designation of the combined triptych as the canonical form is aesthetically significant. The first two poems had existed as standalone deposits, but the third makes possible a larger work that neither of them could constitute alone.

V. The importance of lineation
The poems are extremely short, but their formal differences are purposeful.

Consider their three shapes:

First poem: Six relatively clean lines, with repetition providing the structure. Its emotional movement is circular: the speaker returns to the same declaration, but arrives at a different understanding of time.

Second poem: A more fragmented, eleven-line movement. Enjambment produces an increasingly constricted sense of continuation. The poem's form embodies the diminishing returns it describes.

Third poem: An expansive opening sentence, with several clauses suspended by dashes, followed by a contraction into the final two lines.

The third poem's movement is especially satisfying. It begins with a complicated, conditional, almost breathless image and ends with extraordinary verbal economy.

The final declaration is not embellished.

After the elaborate opening, the poem needs only five words:

that I have loved you.

The formal contraction makes the ending feel discovered rather than announced.

VI. The significance of C.
The dedication, “for C.,” gives the work an intimate addressee.

It is important, however, not to confuse intimacy with biographical certainty. The poems do not tell us who C. is, what precisely happened, or what the relationship consisted of.

We should resist inventing that history.

What the dedication does establish is that the second-person address is not merely an abstract literary exercise. The poems are oriented toward someone.

This creates an interesting tension: the work is deeply particular in its emotional address but deliberately incomplete in its narrative information.

We know what the speaker wants to communicate. We do not know the full circumstances that made the communication necessary.

That restraint allows the poems to maintain their emotional concentration.

VII. My overall reading: a love poem that refuses the usual ending
I would resist describing this as a poem about moving on.

That interpretation would make the third movement too reassuring.

The speaker has not necessarily recovered. The second poem explicitly tells us otherwise:

there is nothing left to become

Nor does the third poem promise that the beloved will return.

The word “if” prevents that.

What changes is the speaker's relationship to the truth of the experience.

At the beginning, love is something that could have been lived.

In the middle, love is something that has left the speaker damaged.

At the end, love is something that has happened, regardless of whether the beloved ever fully understands it.

That is a meaningful distinction between emotional resolution and consolation.

The work achieves the former without offering the latter.

There is also a subtle generosity in the final poem. The beloved is not required to return, apologize, or reciprocate. The beloved is simply imagined as eventually becoming capable of recognition.

The speaker's final statement is therefore not a demand for a future. It is a declaration about the past.

And the present perfect tense is exactly right: the loving is completed, but its significance remains available in the present.

Final judgment
I find the third poem the most accomplished individually, the second the most psychologically forceful, and the first the most structurally indispensable.

The second contains the most memorable language, particularly “a wound-down regime of fewer / and fewer returns.” The third contains the work's most consequential formal decision: changing would have loved into have loved.

But the first poem supplies the condition that makes the final declaration meaningful.

The triptych's real subject is not simply unrequited love, heartbreak, or lost possibility. It is the distinction between an unrealized life and a real experience of loving.

A life together may never have happened. The love nevertheless did.

That is what the final poem insists upon.

And I think the strongest thing about the work is that it does not attempt to make this discovery happy. It allows it to be true without pretending that truth repairs everything.
