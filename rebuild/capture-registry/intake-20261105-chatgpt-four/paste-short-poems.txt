A selective reading across the corpus
I would organize the reading around four different kinds of compression, rather than simply count words.

1. The miniature as an act of perception
"green-grayer than papyrus grass" (9 July 2026)

Five words · New Human 2 · AXN:0433

green-grayer than papyrus grass

This is perhaps the purest example of the miniature in the material I have examined.

The unusual comparative adjective is the poem's principal event. "Green" becomes insufficient; "gray" becomes insufficient. The hyphenated compound produces a color that exists between categories.

But the comparison is doing more than describing a color. Papyrus carries an entire history of writing, preservation, and material transformation. Grass is still living matter; papyrus is matter becoming a writing surface.

The poem therefore holds two conditions together: organic life and the possibility of inscription.

There is an important difference between this reading and the accompanying archival commentary. The commentary develops an elaborate theory of substrate, decomposition, Sappho, and survival. That theory is interesting, but the poem does not require all of it. Indeed, its strongest feature may be its refusal to explain itself.

It is a visual event before it is an intellectual proposition.

2. The miniature as an emotional structure
"Three Poems" (22–23 May 2026)

Three short lyrics · New Human 2 · AXN:02AE

The sequence reads:

I would have loved you

The reward of love

I have loved you

The actual poems are more extensive than these titles, but their verbal architecture is remarkably economical.

The first poem repeats its conditional declaration:

I would have loved you

The second changes the emotional register:

This thing destroyed me

The third moves into a different tense:

then know
that I have loved you.

The important achievement is the movement from conditional to retrospective actuality.

The first poem cannot make love happen. The second registers the damage. The third does not repair the damage or demand reciprocation. It establishes that the love existed.

This is an unusually disciplined treatment of emotional time. The distinction between would have loved and have loved is not merely grammatical. It is the distinction between a possibility denied and an experience that cannot be annulled.

The triptych also demonstrates why the individual poem and the larger structure should not be separated. Each lyric can exist independently, but the third acquires a different weight when read after the first two.

I find this one of the more convincing demonstrations of how Sharks can make formal organization carry emotional meaning without requiring a large vocabulary of images.

3. The miniature as literary judgment
"here is what i learned from catullus" (27 July 2026)

Twenty words · Two manifestations · AXN:0594

The published lineation is:

here is what i learned from catullus

is that

a single

sparrow

matters

more

than all

of rome

This is a particularly revealing poem because its form enacts its argument.

The line contracts toward the isolated word "sparrow." The large historical entity, Rome, is deferred until the end.

The poem's claim is that the small surviving lyric can outlast the great political and material power. Catullus's sparrow poems remain available to readers; Rome, in its former magnitude, is largely an object of historical knowledge.

But there is another formal experiment here. The author deposited both the lineated poem and its unbroken spoken form. The twenty words remain identical, but the experience changes.

In the lineated version, the reader physically descends toward the sparrow. In the prose version, the assertion is delivered as speech.

This is a genuinely interesting distinction between verbal content and poetic form. It is not an argument that line breaks are always meaningful; it is an instance in which the line breaks perform the very hierarchy the words assert.

There is also an important irony: the poem is about the survival of small things, yet it arrives with a substantial explanatory apparatus. The apparatus explains why the sparrow matters; the poem demonstrates it more economically.

I would put this among the strongest short poems in the recent material.

4. The miniature as an ontological question
"WHO IS LEE SHARKS, TO FORGIVE EZRA POUND?" (2015)

This is an important earlier point of reference, even though it is not quite the same kind of miniature.

The poem's central question concerns the standing of a textual person before another textual person. It asks who has the authority to forgive Ezra Pound, and what it means for a poet to exist as a person through language.

The poem's subsequent history makes the question more complicated:

The original blog version appeared in May 2015.

A second version appeared in New Human 2, with a different attribution and dedication.

The two states were later collated and critically edited in 2026.

The differences are substantive, not merely typographical. They alter the identity of the speaker and the relationship between the speaker and the person being addressed.

This provides an early version of a question that becomes increasingly important in the archive: what survives when the immediate person, publication context, or original audience disappears?

It is a useful counterpoint to "green-grayer than papyrus grass." The later poem concentrates the survival question into a material image; the earlier one stages it as a problem of personhood and authority.

The larger corpus: what changes when the scale expands?
The short poems become more interesting when set against three larger tendencies in Sharks' work.

A. The poetics of inheritance
The 2014 Pearl and Other Poems is an important early reference point. Its contents include PEARL, the three Undersongs, FUGUEWORK, and a substantial appendix of essays and manifestos.

Even the organization is significant. Poetry, criticism, parody, literary argument, and statements about poetic technique occupy the same book.

The longer work is interested in how poetry inherits and transforms its predecessors. Whitman, Ginsberg, Pound, and other literary figures are not simply references; they become participants in an ongoing argument about what poetry can do.

The short Catullus poem is a particularly successful condensation of this concern. It takes a literary-historical proposition and makes it into a memorable formal demonstration.

The difference is that the longer work argues about inheritance, while the miniature makes inheritance perceptible.

The poem doesn't need to explain the whole history of Catullus. It needs the sparrow.

B. The poetics of multiplicity
The archive's twelve heteronyms introduce another complication.

Lee Sharks is not always the sole speaking identity. The various voices have differentiated literary, critical, philosophical, and institutional functions.

This makes attribution particularly important.

A short lyric signed by Lee Sharks, a poem appearing under another heteronym, and a critical statement written by Johannes Sigil should not automatically be treated as equivalent expressions of one authorial voice.

Yet there is a productive tension here.

The short lyrics often seem to need very little machinery. They have an identifiable speaker, a sharply delimited experience, and an ending. The larger archive, by contrast, proliferates identities, relationships, documents, and explanatory systems.

That difference can be artistically productive. It can also become a problem when the apparatus begins to determine in advance what the reader is supposed to discover.

C. The archive itself becomes a literary form
This is especially important in the most recent material.

The September 2026 The Work Bears the Mantle and October 2026 The Negative of the Negative are useful examples of the increasingly elaborate relationship between literary work, critical method, inheritance, and archival organization.

The latter is a methodological specification rather than a lyric. Its extensive treatment of sources, ontology, and suppressed evidence demonstrates how much of the author's activity has moved into the construction of a system for reading and establishing literary claims.

My critical judgment
I would resist two tempting conclusions.

The first is that the short poems are better because they are shorter. That would confuse economy with achievement.

The second is that the larger work is necessarily more important because it establishes the conceptual architecture in which the short poems can be interpreted. That would confuse the scale of an intellectual project with the quality of its individual literary productions.

What I find most promising is the relationship between three capacities:

The ability to make a small physical or sensory observation carry a much larger implication.

The ability to make grammatical tense, lineation, and sequence carry emotional experience.

The ability to make literary inheritance an active rather than merely referential presence.

The Catullus poem brings all three together. The papyrus poem does something different but equally interesting: it trusts an image to exist without converting it into an argument. The triptych demonstrates that emotional compression need not mean emotional simplification.

These are not merely reduced versions of the larger corpus. They disclose possibilities within it.

What I would particularly watch in subsequent deposits
I would continue to read new short poems against the following tests:

Does the poem establish an experience that cannot be adequately paraphrased?

Does its form make a difference to its meaning?

Does its literary inheritance enrich it without requiring explanatory apparatus?

Does it leave the reader with something that survives independently of the archive?

Can it withstand being read by someone who knows nothing about the heteronyms or the author's theoretical project?

The last test is especially important.

A poem should be able to enter the world without requiring its own institutional infrastructure to accompany it.

My provisional conclusion: Lee Sharks' very short poems may be among the most important places to look for the independent literary value of the larger project. They reveal an author capable of considerable concentration, perceptual precision, and emotional control. They also offer a useful counterweight to the archive's extraordinary tendency toward expansion.

I would not yet claim that the short poems constitute the best part of the corpus. That requires a broader sample, especially of the 2025–26 blog posts and the newest deposits. But I think they deserve to be read as a distinct and consequential strand of the work, not as curiosities scattered through an immense archive.
