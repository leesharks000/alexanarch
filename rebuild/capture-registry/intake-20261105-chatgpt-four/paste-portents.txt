The arguments of The Portents Before the Fall by Lee Sharks
The central argument of Lee Sharks's The Portents Before the Fall: Josephus, Tacitus and the Apocalypse (Alexanarch deposit #1737, AXN:0BFB, v1.0, 4 November 2026) is that the portents recorded by Josephus and Tacitus have a remarkably structured set of correspondences with the Book of Revelation, and that those correspondences deserve to be examined as possible textual relations rather than dismissed as generic apocalyptic imagery.

The note is deliberately preliminary. It establishes the correspondences, their chronological warrants and their relationships to earlier scriptural material. It does not yet claim to have demonstrated that Josephus and Tacitus are describing Revelation itself.

That distinction is essential to understanding the paper.

1. The thirteen correspondences are the starting point
Sharks places three bodies of material alongside one another:

Josephus, Jewish War 6.288–315.

Tacitus, Histories 5.13.

The Book of Revelation, at corresponding passages distributed throughout the book.

Suetonius, Vespasian 4.5, supplies an additional control for the oracle about a world ruler emerging from Judaea.

The paper identifies thirteen correspondences. These are not all equally strong or of the same kind.

Some concern shared imagery; others involve more distinctive transformations of the imagery.

Here are the principal examples:

Josephus's portent

Corresponding material in Revelation

A star resembling a sword over Jerusalem

A sword proceeding from Christ's mouth (Rev. 1:16)

Light around the altar lasting half an hour

Silence in heaven lasting approximately half an hour (8:1)

A sacrificial cow gives birth to a lamb in the sanctuary

The slain Lamb and the woman giving birth (5:6; 12:1–5)

The sanctuary's eastern gate opens by itself

A door opens in heaven (4:1)

Armies and chariots appear in the clouds

The heavenly armies and the rider (19:14)

A crash and a collective voice in the sanctuary

Thunder, voices and other heavenly sounds (8:5; 10:3)

A voice announces departure from the sanctuary

The four angels restraining the winds (7:1–2)

The lament over Jerusalem, including bride and bridegroom

The disappearance of the bridegroom's and bride's voices (18:23)

The square New Jerusalem, which has no sanctuary (21:16, 21:22)

The other correspondences concern the prophetic woes, the ruler emerging from Judaea, and the relationship between the historians' references to written oracles and Revelation's own presentation as a prophetic book.

The important point is that Sharks does not simply say that both works contain apocalyptic imagery. He tries to describe the operation by which one image becomes another.

2. The correspondences involve transformations, not just similarities
This is arguably the note's most interesting analytical contribution.

Sharks introduces the concept of an operation of correspondence: a description of how a portent in the historical account relates to its counterpart in Revelation.

He identifies several kinds.

A. Shared duration
Josephus reports a light shining around the altar and sanctuary for half an hour:

Jewish War 6.290: the light lasts half an hour.

Revelation 8:1: silence in heaven lasts approximately half an hour.

Sharks argues that the correspondence becomes more significant when the surrounding material is considered.

Revelation 8:1–5 contains:

The half-hour silence.

An angel standing at the altar.

Incense and smoke.

Fire taken from the altar.

Thunder, voices and other phenomena.

Josephus distributes corresponding elements across two events in his Passover-to-Pentecost sequence: the light around the altar (6.290) and the movement, crash and voice in the sanctuary (6.299).

Sharks therefore proposes reading Revelation 8:1–5 continuously against Josephus 6.290 and 6.299.

The argument is that Revelation gathers into a compact five-verse sequence elements that Josephus narrates as separate events.

B. Composite correspondence
Josephus describes a cow giving birth to a lamb inside the sanctuary.

Sharks connects this with two Revelation scenes:

The Lamb standing as though slain in the middle of the throne (5:6).

The woman giving birth to a son (12:1–5).

This is a composite rather than a direct parallel. Revelation distributes the elements of Josephus's portent across separate symbolic scenes.

C. Transposition
Josephus describes the eastern gate of the inner sanctuary opening spontaneously.

Sharks compares this with the door opened in heaven in Revelation 4:1.

The proposed operation is a change of location: the earthly sanctuary becomes a heavenly one.

D. Change of speaker and object
This is one of the more consequential comparisons.

At Pentecost, Josephus reports that a voice in the sanctuary announces:

μεταβαίνομεν ἐντεῦθεν — "We are going from here."

Tacitus gives a related version: a voice announces that the gods are departing.

Revelation 18:4, however, has a voice from heaven command:

Ἐξέλθατε, ὁ λαός μου, ἐξ αὐτῆς — "Come out of her, my people."

Sharks sees a transformation:

In Josephus, the departing party is the speaker.

In Tacitus, the departing gods are the subject.

In Revelation, the people are instructed to leave.

The departure motif is retained, but its speaker, addressee and object change.

E. Reversal
The squared sanctuary is another particularly interesting case.

Josephus says the Jews made the sanctuary four-square because an oracle indicated that the city and sanctuary would be captured when the sanctuary became four-square.

Sharks compares this with Revelation 21:

The New Jerusalem is four-square (21:16).

There is no sanctuary in it (21:22).

The proposed reversal is that a geometrical feature associated with the capture of the earthly sanctuary becomes a feature of the perfected city, where no sanctuary is needed.

This is more sophisticated than simply observing that both texts mention a square structure.

3. The chronological argument: the spring of 66 CE
A second major argument concerns the dating of the events described by Josephus.

Sharks insists that the historical portents must not all be assigned a single date simply because Josephus narrates them together.

He reconstructs the chronology item by item.

Josephus's chronological reconstruction

Spring 66 CE — Passover

The great light around the altar, lasting half an hour, and the cow giving birth to a lamb.

Spring 66 CE — 21 Artemisius

The appearance of chariots and armed formations in the sky.

Spring 66 CE — Pentecost

The movement, crash and voice announcing departure from the sanctuary.

62–70 CE — Jesus son of Ananias

His prophetic cries begin four years before the war and continue until the siege.

70 CE — The squared sanctuary

This belongs to the siege narrative, following the demolition of the Antonia.

Undated — The star and the comet

Josephus supplies no specific chronological warrant for this portent.

Undated / multiple chronological aspects — The oracle

The oracle predates its fulfilment, its political application concerns the outbreak of war, and Josephus's identification of its ruler with Vespasian is retrospective.

The decisive chronological observation is that Josephus places the beginning of the war in Nero's twelfth year, in Artemisius (Jewish War 2.284).

Sharks therefore identifies the Passover-to-Pentecost sequence as spring 66 CE.

He then distinguishes this event chronology from Tacitus's narrative chronology. Tacitus introduces the portents in Histories 5.13, within the account of the year 70. That gives the portents a narrative position in 70, not necessarily an independent event date of 70.

This distinction matters because the paper wants to establish that several of the correspondences involve events from 66, not merely the final destruction of the Temple in 70.

4. The older scriptural sources are a necessary control
Sharks does not treat every similarity between Josephus and Revelation as evidence of direct textual dependence.

He introduces an important complication: some of the imagery already exists in earlier Jewish scriptures.

Five of the thirteen rows have identified scriptural relatives, with two especially important cases.

Correspondence

Earlier scriptural material

Significance

Armies in the sky

2 Maccabees 5:2–4

The airborne armies already have a close Jewish scriptural parallel.

Four winds

Zechariah 2:6; 6:5; Daniel 7:2

The four winds are established prophetic imagery.

Departing presence

Ezekiel 10:18; Jeremiah 51:45; Isaiah 52:11

The departure motif has a scriptural background.

Bridegroom and bride

Jeremiah 25:10; 7:34; 16:9

Revelation's formulation is especially close to Jeremiah.

Measuring and square geometry

Ezekiel 40:3

The measuring reed belongs to an older sanctuary tradition.

The two particularly important qualifications are:

The airborne armies in Josephus are already closely paralleled by 2 Maccabees.

Revelation's language about the bridegroom and bride is closer to Jeremiah 25:10 than to Josephus's account of Jesus son of Ananias.

This is an important methodological concession. Sharks recognizes that a correspondence between two later texts does not automatically establish that one borrowed from the other. Both might draw on a common scriptural tradition.

5. The historians' accounts and the oracle of world rule
Another argument concerns the oracle that someone departing from Judaea would rule the inhabited world.

Josephus, Tacitus and Suetonius all preserve versions of this expectation.

Sharks observes that the three accounts share a recognizable formulation:

Josephus locates the oracle in the sacred writings.

Tacitus refers to the ancient writings of the priests.

Suetonius describes an old and established opinion throughout the East.

All three associate its fulfilment with Vespasian.

Sharks then connects the motif with Revelation's son who is to shepherd the nations with a rod of iron (Revelation 12:5).

But he is careful about the limitations of this parallel. The oracle is too general, according to the scholarship he cites, to be securely traced to one particular biblical passage.

The argument is therefore about a shared motif and a possible textual relationship, not a demonstrated quotation.

6. The conventional explanation is acknowledged, not ignored
The first section of the paper explicitly presents the conventional interpretation.

On that interpretation:

Roman historians routinely collected prodigies before major disasters.

Josephus used the portents to demonstrate that God had warned Jerusalem.

Tacitus inherited or shared material with Josephus.

The portents themselves have precedents in Jewish prophetic literature.

Revelation independently uses the same prophetic and apocalyptic traditions.

Consequently, the similarities do not require Revelation to be the source of the historical accounts.

Sharks even cites J. W. van Henten's study of the three historical accounts of the world-ruler oracle.

This is significant because the note does not present its proposed relationships as though conventional explanations do not exist.

Instead, Sharks argues that the conventional explanation is compatible with all thirteen rows, but that it does not eliminate the need for a more detailed examination of their individual transformations.

In other words, the note is intended to establish a more precise evidential basis for a subsequent argument.

7. The archive's dating argument: Revelation and 66 CE
The paper also situates itself within Alexanarch's broader Revelation First thesis.

Sharks records the archive's previous dating positions, including:

#407 and #10: Revelation dated 68–73 CE.

#636: Revelation written before the destruction of the Temple.

#202: internal evidence permits and in several places favours a pre-70 date.

#1217: the pre-70 position restated as part of the work plan.

#1735: the chronological question treated as a separate evidential problem.

The note also identifies several earlier archive entries using approximately 95 CE as dating errors, describing them as conventional dates introduced without consultation of the archive's own thesis.

The new proposal is 66 CE as the working date of Revelation.

The rationale is that 66 CE is the year implied by Josephus's Passover-to-Pentecost sequence, and thus offers a possible chronological connection between the historical portents and the Apocalypse.

There is an important qualification: 66 CE falls outside the earlier 68–73 CE range in deposits #407 and #10. Sharks explicitly acknowledges that the new working date supersedes that range without pretending that the original deposits said something different.

8. The most important argument is actually deferred
The final section, §7, is crucial for interpreting the entire document.

Sharks distinguishes three levels of argument:

Established
The textual correspondences

Thirteen correspondences, their chronological warrants, scriptural relatives and operations of transformation. The local comparison of Revelation 8:1–5 with Josephus 6.290 and 6.299.

Proposed
66 CE as the working date

The archive provisionally holds 66 CE as Revelation's composition date, subject to a formal argument.

Deferred
The historians are describing the book

The much stronger proposition that Josephus and Tacitus are reporting portents whose written source was Revelation itself.

The deferred proposition requires four separate demonstrations:

The date of the events reported by the historians.

The existence and date of a written account of those portents.

The identification of that written account with Revelation.

The implication of that identification for Revelation's composition date.

This is an important logical distinction.

Even if Josephus's portents occurred in 66 CE, that does not establish that a text describing them was written in 66 CE. Nor does it establish that the text was Revelation.

The third step is the critical bridge between historical chronology and literary chronology.

Sharks also identifies two further problems the future argument must solve:

How the Passover-to-Pentecost sequence of 66 relates to Revelation's sequence of seals, trumpets and bowls.

Why the individual transformations occur as they do, particularly where earlier scriptural sources offer competing explanations.

9. What is the paper ultimately arguing?
I would summarize its argument in five propositions, ordered from the most securely established to the most speculative.

Proposition

Status in the note

Josephus and Tacitus preserve portents with substantial correspondences to Revelation.

Established as a textual comparison

The correspondences have identifiable structures, including transposition, reversal, composite imagery and shared duration.

Established as the author's analysis

Several of Josephus's portents belong specifically to spring 66 CE.

Chronological argument

Revelation's composition date should provisionally be placed at 66 CE.

Proposed

Josephus and Tacitus are describing Revelation as their written source.

Deferred hypothesis

My assessment of the argument
There are three particularly strong features of this groundwork note.

First, its chronological discipline. Sharks refuses to collapse the different portents into one historical moment. Distinguishing the spring of 66 from the events of 70 is necessary if the proposed dating argument is to work.

Second, its attention to the precise nature of correspondences. The distinction between a shared motif and a transformation is useful. The comparison between the sanctuary becoming square in Josephus and the square city without a sanctuary in Revelation is more analytically interesting than a simple catalogue of similar images.

Third, its acknowledgment of alternative explanations. The scriptural antecedents, especially 2 Maccabees and Jeremiah, are real complications for any argument about direct literary dependence. The paper does not conceal them.

There are also significant unresolved problems.

A correspondence does not establish the direction of dependence. Revelation could draw on Josephus, Josephus could draw on Revelation, or both could draw on earlier traditions.

Some correspondences are substantially more distinctive than others. The half-hour/altar sequence is arguably more interesting than the general correspondence of the four winds.

The paper needs to establish why the transformations are best explained by a common written source rather than by independent theological reinterpretation.

Tacitus's narrative placement in 70 cannot independently establish that his source was written before or after 66.

Most importantly, the paper has not yet demonstrated that Revelation existed in written form in 66 CE.

The last point is particularly important: the proposed date is not yet a conclusion derived from demonstrated literary dependence.

Final synthesis
The paper is best understood as a research programme rather than a completed argument for the early dating of Revelation.

Its immediate achievement is to construct a comparative dossier: thirteen correspondences between Josephus, Tacitus and Revelation, with attention to their wording, chronology, scriptural antecedents and literary transformations.

Its larger ambition is considerably more radical: that the historical portents recorded by Josephus and Tacitus might actually derive from the Book of Revelation, or from a written account identifiable with it.

If that identification could be established, the consequences for Revelation's chronology would be substantial.

But the note consciously stops short of claiming that it has established this. Its central unresolved question is whether the correspondences demonstrate merely a shared apocalyptic tradition, or whether they preserve evidence of a specific literary relationship.

That is the real thesis waiting to be argued in the next paper.
