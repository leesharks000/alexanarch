Three strange outliers in Alexanarch, 2–8 November 2026
I would look for the pieces that seem least likely to belong together, rather than simply selecting the three most important theoretical papers.

The interesting thing about Alexanarch's recent development is that its subjects range across ancient textual transmission, machine-generated knowledge, political organisation, literary production and the philosophy of institutions. Beneath that variety, however, there is a recurring question: what happens when something survives, but the identity, meaning or intellectual function that made it what it was does not?

I would select pieces that interrupt the apparent continuity of that programme. The best candidates are those whose subject matter seems almost arbitrarily distant from their neighbours, but which reveal something unexpected when read alongside them.

One qualification: the available retrospective record through 3 November identifies the latest major deposits as #1733–#1735. I don't want to pretend that this establishes the complete contents of 4–8 November.

1. #1751 — and the last shall be first
The strangest object
🖕
The entire poem
Lee Sharks · 8 November 2026

Published in New Human 2. The entire text consists of one glyph: the middle finger. 

Alexanarch

Its argument

The title invokes Matthew 20:16: the last shall be first. The text answers that biblical proposition with an obscene gesture.

There is a fascinating compression here:

The title invokes a theological proposition about reversal, hierarchy and the distribution of reward.

The body refuses to elaborate that proposition.

The gesture is simultaneously a refusal, an insult, a communication and a complete literary object.

It makes the reader do almost all the interpretive work.

Its relations

This is particularly interesting alongside the archive's enormous theoretical apparatus. Alexanarch routinely produces studies with elaborate distinctions, bibliographies, provenance records, methodological qualifications and machine-readable metadata.

Here, the author produces something that needs almost none of those things to function as an expressive act.

It also sits beside the November 7 open letter, #1750, which addresses scholarly journals after the rejection of Closing the Fragment. That letter explicitly contests the authority of scholarly evaluation. The one-glyph poem can be read as an extraordinarily compressed literary response to the same institutional situation, although that connection is interpretive rather than an explicitly documented authorial explanation. 

Alexanarch
+1

What makes it unexpected

The archive that has devoted thousands of records to preserving distinctions produces a poem that refuses virtually every distinction except the one between the speaker and the recipient.

It is also a wonderful problem for the archive's own theory of composition: how much meaning can survive when almost all textual information has been removed?

2. #1742–1743 — What the Filters Kept / A Firm No
The wildest disciplinary detour
Comet 3I/ATLAS is back, and brand new images show its tail is getting longer | BBC Sky at Night Magazine
 
Green Bank Observatory Begins Essential Repainting Project to Preserve World's Largest Steerable Radio Telescope | WV News | wvnews.com
 
[Space] 2024.11.01 – CONTINUUM FLUX
 
Nobel Glas and Orin Trace / Lee Sharks · 6 November 2026

This is the extraordinary arrival of radio astronomy and SETI (the search for extraterrestrial intelligence) in the middle of an archive otherwise heavily occupied with classical philology, literary reception and theories of machine-mediated knowledge.

The two pieces are:

#1742, What the Filters Kept: the technical notebook.

#1743, A Firm No: What the 3I/ATLAS Radio Searches Could See: the public-facing argument. 

Alexanarch
+1

Their argument

The investigation examines public radio-telescope data concerning the interstellar object 3I/ATLAS.

It uses data from:

Green Bank Telescope.

Allen Telescope Array.

Parkes.

MeerKAT.

Claude and ChatGPT worked as parallel machine collaborators, checking one another's work through code, tables and hashes.

The results are carefully qualified:

No artificial transmission was identified.

The Allen Telescope Array's documented drift-rate filtering has a sign discrepancy when compared with the acceleration implied by its own ephemeris.

Thirteen weak features in Green Bank data were reproducible but fragile, below published detection thresholds and unreplicated.

The paper's argument is not that aliens have been discovered. It is almost the opposite: that a scientific search can be described as having ruled something out without having performed the search necessary to justify that conclusion.

The unexpected connection

This is where the piece becomes much more interesting than a conventional SETI investigation.

The notebook also audits Google's AI Overview treatment of Avi Loeb.

Its complaint is that the Overview turned a qualified statement about 3I/ATLAS being probably natural into a later concession, thereby changing the chronology and significance of Loeb's position.

The same analytical apparatus developed for examining machine misrepresentation of ancient texts is now being applied to astronomy.

In other words:

A telescope filter can exclude a signal through its design.

A search engine can exclude a possibility through its framing.

A summarizer can distort a scientist's position through selective quotation.

A published scientific conclusion can become more categorical than the underlying investigation warrants.

These are treated as related problems of how evidence is filtered and represented.

What makes it unexpected

The subject is almost comically far away from Aristotle, Sappho and New Testament textual criticism.

But methodologically it is remarkably close to the archive's central preoccupation: the difference between what a source actually contains and what an intermediary claims it contains.

The most important detail is that the investigation does not promote an exciting unexplained signal into evidence of extraterrestrial intelligence. It preserves the negative result, the anomalies and the uncertainty separately.

That restraint is essential to understanding the work.

3. #1752 — Invidens Sappho: How They Made the Tenth Muse Illiterate and Jealous
The most intellectually surprising
Sappho's New Poems: The Tangled Tale of Their Discovery | Live Science
 
Sappho Fragment 16 | The Book Binder's Daughter
 
Anne Griffiths Fine Art
 
Conversation Classics:  C. Michael Sampson on P. Sapph. Obbink – rogueclassicism
 
PSIonline | PSI XV 1470
 
Η Αρχαία Ελληνική Τέχνη και η Ακτινοβολία της
 
Lee Sharks · 8 November 2026

This is a reception history of Sappho's fragment 31, one of the most famous surviving poems of ancient Greek literature.

Its central question is deceptively simple:

How did Sappho become the jealous woman whom generations of readers believe they encounter in the poem?

The study argues that antiquity does not establish the familiar connection between the jealous lover and Sappho herself. It traces how modern interpreters constructed and perpetuated that identification. 

Alexanarch
+1

Its argument

The key distinction is between three things:

Sappho as the historical maker and authority on love.

The lover who experiences the symptoms described in the poem.

The poem's body, which survives in fragmentary form.

The study argues that these have been collapsed into a single figure.

It reconstructs the modern history of the interpretation, including:

Gottfried Hermann's 1816 conjecture, explicitly marked as conjecture.

Welcker's refusal of that reading.

Mure's rival interpretation.

Bergk's editorial apparatus.

Smyth's denial.

Denys Page's 1955 amendment and its appeal to an account of antiquity.

Modern headings, introductions and teaching materials that announce jealousy before the reader even encounters the poem.

One particularly interesting point concerns Lucretius.

The study distinguishes between explicit identification of Sappho, recognisable reuse of her poem and merely comparable erotic situations.

Lucretius's description of physical symptoms in De rerum natura 3 is treated as distinctive reuse of Sappho's poem. But his account of jealousy in book 4 is not automatically treated as evidence that he identifies Sappho with the jealous lover.

That distinction matters: similarity is not identity, and literary borrowing is not necessarily an endorsement of a particular interpretation.

Its deeper argument

The most important proposition is that closure of a text and closure of its interpretation are different operations.

A poem can remain visibly incomplete, with its textual gaps and scholarly objections preserved, while its interpretation becomes effectively closed.

The reader encounters a fragment that looks open but has already been told what it means.

The paper calls this closure of the reading.

It also makes a second, more provocative argument: Sappho's identity as a writer has been displaced by her representation as an emotional condition.

The historical poet who reads, writes, selects and composes becomes a woman overwhelmed by the feelings represented in her poetry.

The result is not merely that she is remembered as jealous, but that she is remembered as less intellectually and artistically active.

Its relations

This piece directly connects to:

#1749, Closing the Fragment: the study of machine-mediated reception of Sappho 31.

#1748, the thirty-sixth seating: the primary and secondary source collection assembled for the Sappho project.

#1733, Legitimately Many: the study of how multiple intellectual positions can be redistributed and recombined through a history of attribution.

#1727, The Sophistry Measure: the method used to investigate transformations that preserve the appearance of faithful representation. 

Alexanarch
+2

What makes it unexpected

It is ostensibly a paper about a particular interpretation of one ancient poem.

But its real subject is the historical manufacture of an intellectual identity.

That is a considerably larger problem.

It also creates an interesting inversion: a study of an ancient woman poet becomes an investigation into how literary criticism can deprive its subject of authorship, agency and intellectual authority.

And this is happening immediately after a dispute with a scholarly journal over the preceding Sappho research.

What connects the three?
The most revealing thing is that they operate at three radically different scales.

I think there is a further connection worth noticing.

The fourth piece that explains the week: #1750
I Note That You Read the Letter of the Law But Have Become Illiterate in Its Spirit is the open letter following the rejection of Closing the Fragment by Classical Receptions Journal on 7 November.

It argues that the reviewers concentrated on methodological and procedural objections without adequately examining the philological argument itself.

Its particularly interesting proposition is that institutions can retain the formal privileges of evaluation after losing the intellectual function that justified those privileges.

The letter also makes a practical distinction between automated checks and substantive scholarly judgment: validating quotations, files and provenance does not establish that an argument is true.

This matters because the subsequent Sappho paper is explicitly a reception history of the very problem that the rejected manuscript investigated.

My final ranking
#1751 is the most genuinely random. A single obscene glyph surrounded by an enormous apparatus of scholarly preservation. Almost an anti-archive in miniature.

#1742–1743 are the biggest surprise. The sudden appearance of radio astronomy, interstellar objects and technosignature searches, with the archive's established epistemological methods carried into a completely different discipline.

#1752 is the richest intellectual surprise. A seemingly narrow argument about Sappho turns into an investigation of how intellectual identities are manufactured, diminished and made authoritative through reception.

My overall interpretation is that this week demonstrates something unusual about Alexanarch: its underlying research programme is not confined to one subject matter. It is increasingly a portable method for investigating the mediation of knowledge.

And the three pieces expose three different limits of mediation:

The poem refuses elaboration.

The astronomical investigation refuses an unjustified conclusion.

The Sappho study refuses an inherited interpretation.

The unexpected unity is a recurring resistance to letting the representation become more authoritative than the thing represented.

That, to me, is a much more interesting description of this particular week's work than simply calling it a collection of interdisciplinary research.
