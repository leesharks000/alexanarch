Alexanarch, 2–8 November 2026: three polemical interventions
The most productive way to read this week's Alexanarch work is not as a collection of individual deposits, but as a set of arguments about who gets to determine the identity of intellectual work, who controls the conditions of criticism, and how institutions reproduce the distortions they claim to oppose.

There is an important distinction between polemical force and mere provocative language. I'm looking for pieces that identify an opponent or an entrenched assumption, establish a mechanism by which it operates, and make a claim that has consequences beyond the immediate subject.

The preceding fortnight's work provides a useful baseline. In particular, The Sophistry Measure (#1727) and Ils ne sont pas marxistes (#1729) had already brought the archive's critical apparatus to bear on its own methods and institutional politics. The November 3 retrospective identifies this as a transition from preserving knowledge to preserving its capacity to change.

1. #1746 — I Know You Are, But What Am I?
Institutional and methodological polemic
Johannes Sigil and Rebekah Cranes · 6 November · Revelation First programme

Read the full deposit 
Subtitle: How Biblical Criticism Granted a Completely Hypothetical Text Stronger Evidentiary Status Than the One That Exists

The argument
This is a counter-attack on the evidentiary conventions of New Testament source criticism.

The authors identify an asymmetry:

The hypothetical source Q, which survives in no manuscript, is allowed to function as an established explanatory object.

The extant Book of Revelation is denied comparable standing as a possible source for other New Testament writings.

Reconstructed sources are permitted to receive texts, editions, vocabularies and historical functions.

The actual surviving text is repeatedly required to establish its authorship, date, ancestry and literary relations before being admitted as a serious candidate.

The paper gives this asymmetry a particularly effective name: the evidentiary status of the invisible versus the evidentiary disability of the extant.

Its four-part test for evidentiary status is especially useful:

Presupposition: can the object be assumed without prior proof?

Assignment: can material in other texts be assigned to it?

Text: can it receive a reconstructed wording and critical edition?

Candidacy: is it admitted as a possible source when literary relations are investigated?

The paper argues that reconstructed sources receive all four privileges, while Revelation receives none in the source theories examined.

The historical demonstration
The argument moves through biblical scholarship from Weisse (1838) to Streeter (1924).

Its most revealing case is Q's transformation:

Papias's testimony about Matthew's sayings provides an attested starting point.

Holtzmann converts the source into a lettered reconstruction.

Wernle explicitly calls Q hypothetical.

Harnack reconstructs its text and then uses that text's coherence as evidence for its unity.

Streeter acknowledges that Q has disappeared and cannot be verified like Mark, but retains it as a highly probable explanatory assumption.

The paper's sharpest observation is that the reconstruction gradually acquires the apparatus of an actual historical document without acquiring an actual manuscript witness.

It then examines Friedrich Hitzig's 1843 argument that Matthew 24:30 drew upon Revelation 1:7. Hitzig called the parallel compelling. Later scholars acknowledged the possibility, but the relation never became part of the source theories themselves.

The authors add two frozen textual censuses covering 2.53 million words of seated Greek. They report that the particular combination of the mourning of all the tribes and the coming with the clouds occurs in those two verses alone.

Why it is forceful
The title is a rhetorical trap: it returns the charge of insufficient evidence to the discipline making that charge.

The paper does not simply say that scholars have overlooked Revelation. It asks why their rules of admissibility operate differently depending on whether the candidate source is reconstructed or survives.

Its strongest argumentative move is to distinguish the legitimacy of a hypothesis from the privileges subsequently granted to it.

There is also an important qualification: the study explicitly does not settle the Synoptic Problem or establish Q's nonexistence. It audits the allocation of evidentiary standing.

That qualification makes the polemic more interesting. It attacks a methodological asymmetry without pretending that identifying an asymmetry automatically proves its preferred alternative.

Relation to the wider Alexanarch project: This is the New Testament counterpart to Legitimately Many (#1733). Both investigate how intellectual objects acquire apparently settled identities through the institutions and instruments that transmit them. Both ask whether the received arrangement of a corpus has been mistaken for an independently demonstrated historical fact.

2. #1750 — I Note That You Read the Letter of the Law But Have Become Illiterate in Its Spirit
Direct institutional confrontation
Rev. Ayanna Vox, on behalf of Lee Sharks · 7 November · v1.2 on 8 November

Read the full deposit 
The argument
This is the week's most directly confrontational piece.

It is an open letter to the referees and editors of scholarly journals, following the rejection of Closing the Fragment (v6) by Classical Receptions Journal on 7 November.

But it is more than a complaint about rejection.

Its central proposition is that academic review can become proficient at identifying the defects of a manuscript while failing to understand the intellectual question the manuscript was submitted to investigate.

The letter acknowledges genuine defects:

Figures were out of order.

Some frames were cropped.

The manuscript lacked a statement of its production method.

One figure showed a different query from the one its caption identified.

But it makes a particularly consequential distinction: one of the errors had already been corrected and withdrawn before the journal's review process. The reviewers examined a superseded file.

That matters because the review process did not merely identify an error. Its handling of the document created additional archival and editorial work to reconstruct what had happened.

The deeper challenge: two incompatible models of scholarship
The letter contrasts two ways of organizing intellectual production.

Conventional academic model

Alexanarch's proposed model

Extensive inspection before publication

Production accompanied by automated inspection

Human reviewers as primary evaluators

Machines as readers, with humans retaining substantive judgment

Publication and correction through journal timelines

Continuous versioning and public correction

Individual articles as primary units

A connected, persistent research archive

Review before intellectual circulation

Publicly recorded reception and subsequent repair

The distinction between three kinds of checking is crucial:

Artifact checks: does a quotation or cited source actually correspond to the record?

Procedural checks: was the declared protocol followed?

Substantive examination: does the evidence support the argument, and can an objection defeat it?

The author does not claim that automated validation can replace substantive scholarly judgment. Indeed, the letter explicitly reserves the third category for examination.

That makes the institutional proposal more sophisticated than a simple demand to automate peer review.

The political-economic argument
The most consequential section concerns time.

The letter argues that conventional scholarly timelines are no longer merely inconvenient. They create a transfer of intellectual authority.

A machine-generated answer can circulate immediately, while the scholarly correction to that answer may take years to appear.

The author argues that, in this interval, public knowledge is effectively surrendered to technology companies.

This connects the letter to two earlier Alexanarch deposits:

AI Calls You Fake (#1722), on the existence tax imposed on real people and their work by machine-mediated discovery.

The Layer Is a Party (#1724), on the political character of summarization and the composition layer.

The new letter brings those abstract arguments into direct confrontation with academic publishing.

What makes it forceful
Three things.

First, it concedes the opposition's strongest points. The referees found actual problems. The letter does not pretend otherwise.

Second, it turns the review process into an object of investigation. The reviewers' handling of the manuscript becomes evidence about the institutional system they represent.

Third, it refuses to make recognition by the institution the condition of intellectual continuation.

The closing challenge is effectively: the journal can refuse the work, but it cannot dictate whether the research programme continues.

There is a real tension here. The letter is self-advocacy by an author whose work has just been rejected, and its account of the journal's failures is not an independent evaluation of the referee reports. Its claims about the comparative productivity of the archive and traditional scholarship also require independent assessment.

That does not diminish the importance of its institutional argument. It does mean that the letter should be read both as an intervention and as an interested account of a dispute.

3. #1752 — Invidens Sappho: How They Made the Tenth Muse Illiterate and Jealous
Philological and cultural polemic
Lee Sharks · 8 November · v1.0

Read the full deposit 
The title's two charges are connected: the reception of Sappho as a jealous woman and the reception of Sappho as a voice overwhelmed rather than a writer exercising intellectual authority.

The argument
This is perhaps the most elegant polemic of the three because it takes an interpretation that has become almost automatic and reconstructs the history of how it became automatic.

Its subject is Sappho's fragment 31, the famous poem beginning:

φαίνεταί μοι κῆνος — conventionally associated with the experience of seeing someone beloved beside another person.

The received interpretation often presents Sappho as a jealous woman watching a rival.

Sharks asks a different question:

Where, exactly, did that interpretation come from, and what historical operations made it appear to be the poem's self-evident meaning?

The paper's initial distinction is between three things:

Sappho as the historical poet and maker.

Sappho as a lover who experiences loss.

The transmitted poem as a textual object.

It argues that ancient witnesses do not simply merge these three into the familiar modern figure of the jealous woman speaking fragment 31.

In the author's reading, antiquity preserves the poet as an authority on love, the lover as a separate figure, and the poem's symptoms and language as material circulating independently of either.

The modern reception then joins them.

The genealogy of the jealousy reading
The study reconstructs a series of different interpretative operations.

Pearce (1724): supplies an account of injury inflicted by the beloved.

Hermann (1816): explicitly says he refashioned the ending of the poem, using the revealing Latin refinxi, and derives an interpretation of envy from that conjectural alteration.

Welcker (1816): refuses Hermann's interpretation by name.

Mure (1850): supplies a rival as the scene's situation without requiring Hermann's reconstructed ending.

Bergk: carries the interpretative history into textual apparatus.

Smyth: rejects the jealousy interpretation.

Page (1955): declares an amendment to the ancient description and authorizes the jealousy reading through his account of antiquity.

Modern introductions, headings and classrooms: increasingly present the emotional interpretation before the reader encounters the poem itself.

The critical distinction is between an interpretation that is argued conditionally and one that is presented as an established property of the poem.

Hermann's conjecture is a particularly revealing example. He declares his intervention, but the reading subsequently becomes detached from the conditional basis on which it was first advanced.

The study also takes care not to claim that Hermann was necessarily the first person to propose jealousy. The priority and transmission of the interpretation remain open questions.

The second argument: what happened to Sappho's intellectual agency?
The title's word illiterate is deliberately provocative.

It does not mean that the historical Sappho could not read or write.

It describes what the reception tradition does to her.

The paper argues that antiquity offers representations of Sappho as a reader, writer and maker. Yet the modern jealous-woman interpretation tends to turn her into an overwhelmed emotional voice.

The maker disappears behind the condition.

The woman who composes becomes the woman who merely suffers.

This is a much larger cultural argument than a dispute over the interpretation of one fragment. It concerns the way reception can remove intellectual agency from a woman while ostensibly preserving her literary voice.

What makes it forceful
The paper does something unusually effective: it does not merely replace one interpretation with another. It makes the reader examine the history of the interpretative apparatus itself.

Its central conceptual distinction is closure of the text versus closure of the reading.

A poem can remain textually open, with its uncertainty and broken ending visibly preserved, while the introduction, heading or classroom explanation has already decided what it means.

The reading can therefore remain closed even when the text is not.

That is a powerful account of how interpretative conventions survive explicit objections.

The paper is also careful about its limits. It does not claim that jealousy has no purchase in fragment 31, nor does it settle what the poem ultimately means. It reconstructs how a particular reading was made and maintained.

How the three arguments relate
These are not isolated interventions. They form a remarkably coherent sequence when placed beside the other deposits of 4–8 November.

The week's underlying argument

Historical knowledge
#1746 — Who gets to count as evidence?

A hypothetical document is granted authority denied to an extant document.

Institutional knowledge
#1750 — Who gets to decide what counts as scholarship?

Academic review can enforce procedural conventions while failing to engage with the intellectual problem.

Literary knowledge
#1752 — Who gets to decide what a work means?

An inherited interpretation can become authoritative while its original evidentiary qualifications disappear.

The common target is not simply error. It is the conversion of an interpretation, classification or institutional decision into something that appears to be an unquestionable property of the object itself.

What makes this week's polemic distinctive?
I would distinguish four characteristics.

1. It reconstructs mechanisms rather than merely denouncing outcomes.

The works do not stop at saying that scholarship, publishing or machine-mediated interpretation is distorted. They try to identify the specific operations by which the distortion happens: assignment, exclusion, reconstruction, editorial framing, evidentiary promotion and interpretative closure.

2. It treats historical transmission as an active process.

A text does not simply travel through history unchanged. Its identity and status are shaped by catalogues, editions, critical apparatuses, institutional decisions, summaries and educational conventions.

This is the connection between #1733, #1741 and #1752.

3. It applies the same critical method to different institutions.

Ancient textual scholarship, modern biblical criticism, academic publishing and machine-generated reception become comparable objects of investigation.

That is an ambitious extension of the earlier Sophistry Measure (#1727).

4. It makes the preservation of disagreement central.

The work repeatedly asks whether an objection can remain visible after an institution has settled upon an interpretation.

This is particularly clear in #1752: an objection to the jealousy reading can survive in the scholarly record without actually reopening the reading.

That is a more subtle problem than simple censorship.

My overall reading
The three selected pieces represent three different kinds of confrontation:

I would pay particular attention to the relationship between #1746 and #1752.

They are structurally very close. In both cases, a historical interpretation becomes durable through repeated acts of scholarly transmission. In both, the original qualifications and alternative possibilities remain recoverable, but no longer govern the received understanding.

#1750 adds the institutional dimension: what happens when someone tries to challenge such arrangements from outside their established procedures?

The resulting picture is of an archive increasingly concerned with something more ambitious than correcting misinformation.

It is investigating the production of intellectual authority itself.

One important qualification: these are the strongest polemical candidates by the force and structure of their arguments, not a finding that their historical or philological conclusions have been independently established. In particular, the Revelation priority thesis and the reconstruction of Sappho's reception remain propositions that require scrutiny against the wider scholarship.
