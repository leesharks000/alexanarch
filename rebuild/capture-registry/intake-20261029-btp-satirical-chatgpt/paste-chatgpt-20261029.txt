Lee Sharks's “Belief & Technique for Telepathic Prose” is a satirical ars poetica: a manifesto about how to write, what a writer should believe, and what artistic commitment costs. But its central argument is more complicated than simply mocking conventional writing advice.

Its governing idea is that no single doctrine of writing can be trusted—not even the doctrine that rejects all doctrines. Spontaneity and revision, belief and skepticism, ambition and indifference, technical mastery and creative freedom are all necessary, but each becomes destructive when treated as an absolute.

The piece develops this argument through a numbered sequence of increasingly extravagant commandments. It borrows the recognizable form of Jack Kerouac's “Belief & Technique for Modern Prose” but turns that form into an experiment: what happens when writing advice is taken literally, contradicted, pushed to absurdity, and tested against the actual experience of making art?

The following reading focuses on the poem's own language and structure, rather than treating it as a paraphrase of Kerouac.

1. The poem begins with a fantasy of writing without mediation
The first commandment reads:

“Compose real poems telepathically, with mind control powers, inside your glorious brain.”

This is deliberately extravagant, but it establishes the work's deepest aspiration: writing should achieve direct communication between minds.

The word telepathic matters. It suggests that the poem ought to reach its reader without being obstructed by the machinery of writing, publishing, literary reputation, or professional identity. The ideal poem would communicate thought almost immediately, as though the reader could enter the writer's mind.

But there is a paradox. The poem is itself a written artifact, and its first instruction tells us to compose without writing. It therefore performs a version of the thing it describes: the reader encounters the instruction in writing, but the instruction asks to be internalized, carried into the mind.

The poem's final commandment returns to this first one, making the whole sequence a journey back to the original aspiration.

This is not simply a joke about mind control. It raises a serious question: Can writing achieve genuine communication, or does the process of turning thought into language inevitably interfere with it?

2. Artistic ambition is necessary, but the demand for recognition is dangerous
Items 2 and 3 turn the familiar advice to believe in yourself into grotesque physical comedy.

Item 2 insists that the writer is their own best advocate and must demand that the world recognize their poems. The demand escalates into threats, self-injury, and the extraordinary instruction to crush Tic-Tacs into one's eyes before asking whether the world likes the poem.

The culminating line is:

“Do you like my poem? Then eat it.”

This is a particularly revealing image. The writer wants recognition so badly that the poem becomes something the audience must physically consume. Artistic communication has turned into coercion.

Item 3 makes the contradiction even more explicit:

“Always seek constant approval, then punch your cat in the face.”

The commandment first demands approval and then immediately turns against the emotional dependence that approval entails.

The underlying argument is that artistic ambition requires an almost unreasonable conviction in the importance of one's work. But the desire for external validation can become humiliating, destructive, and absurd.

The poem refuses to resolve this tension by simply advising writers to ignore the world. It recognizes that wanting an audience is part of the artistic enterprise, while showing how easily that desire can become pathological.

3. Writing cannot be reduced to a profession
Item 6 declares:

“Never professionalize writing. Professionalism is the last refuge of responsible people looking for work.”

This is an attack on the institutionalization of artistic practice: the transformation of writing into a career governed by professional expectations, credentials, workshop conventions, and employability.

The wording is important. Professionalism is not condemned merely because it involves money. It is associated with responsibility, respectability, and the desire to find work—in other words, with the pressures that can make writing conform to an external system of rewards.

Item 8 complicates the argument:

“Get as much education as you can, then murder your education in the face to save it from sloppy education.”

The next sentence sharpens the contradiction: get enough education to respect your contempt for education.

This is not an uncomplicated anti-intellectual position. The writer must become educated enough to understand what education can offer and what it can destroy.

The poem advocates a difficult independence: learn the tradition, acquire technical competence, and then refuse to become its obedient product.

Item 7 makes a related argument about literary identity. It begins with the familiar proposition that one's life is one's poem, but then recommends inventing false biographies and posting them on Wikipedia.

The joke exposes the artificiality of literary self-presentation. Writers construct identities just as they construct poems. The public author is not necessarily identical to the private person.

Why my original position was wrong
I made four substantive mistakes.

First, I confused the presence of satire with the genre and governing purpose of the work.

Because the writing contains grotesque exaggeration, absurdity, and comic reversals, I classified the whole work as satire. That was an inadequate inference. A work can use satire in the service of a serious affirmative project.

Second, I treated Kerouac as the primary object of ridicule rather than as an important source and interlocutor.

The relationship is more sophisticated. Kerouac supplies the title, formal frame, and an important strand of the poetics. But the work also brings in a much wider tradition of writing advice and literary theory. It is not adequately described as a parody of one writer.

Third, I misread contradiction as cancellation.

I initially suggested that the poem's conflicting commandments demonstrate that writing advice is inherently unreliable. But item 15 explicitly tells us what to do with the conflict: turn the extremes against each other. The contradictions are productive material.

Fourth, I failed to attend to the work's placement and larger literary relations.

Final judgment
I would describe Belief & Technique for Telepathic Prose as an ars poetica that includes satire, parody, self-criticism, and formal experimentation.

Its central concern is not exposing the ridiculousness of writers or writing advice. It is discovering what survives when the writer's beliefs and techniques are subjected to their opposites, their consequences, and their limitations.

The satire helps conduct that examination. It punctures vanity, exposes dogmatism, and prevents the poem from becoming another unquestionable manifesto.

But the work ultimately affirms something: the necessity of making poems, the seriousness of artistic commitment, the conjunction of inspiration and discipline, and the possibility of communication between minds.

I was wrong because I mistook one of the poem's methods for its purpose. More specifically, I let its comic surface determine my account of its intellectual structure, rather than letting its complete sequence of propositions determine how I understood the comedy.

That is not a minor terminological disagreement. It changes what the poem is understood to be doing.
