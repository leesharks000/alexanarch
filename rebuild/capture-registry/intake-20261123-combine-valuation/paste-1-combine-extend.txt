The strongest approach is to take three works that have a genuine conceptual relationship, but whose implications have not yet been fully developed together. Rather than produce another retrospective summary of Alexanarch, I would construct a new argument that emerges from their intersection.

One particularly promising direction is the relationship between textual transmission, recognition, and the production of authorship. This connects the archive's classical philology to its institutional criticism and its developing theory of literary composition.

The important question is not merely how a text survives transmission, but whether transmission can change what a text is understood to have been—and whether a later reader can recover possibilities that the history of reception has obscured.

The three source works
1. What Arrival Cannot Do: The Arithmetic of Recognition  (#1789, Nobel Glas and Sen Kuro). Develops the distinction between the arrival of a signal and the receiver's probability of recognizing it. Its central insight is that absence of detection is not evidence of absence when the receiver's recognition capacity is unknown.

2. Dead Letters: The University of Chicago's Classical Philology Journal, the Atom of Evidence, and Who Decides What Sappho Is  (#1783). Investigates how institutions determine what counts as admissible evidence, and how the smallest textual unit can be excluded by the very apparatus that claims to preserve and interpret it.

3. Either Forgery or Fact: The Genre Imposed on Our Most Vital Books  (#1798, Johannes Sigil and Rebekah Cranes). Develops faithful extension as a third possibility beyond the binary of documentary fact and forgery.

The new synthesis
I would call the resulting argument The Recognition Trap: How Receivers Manufacture the Histories They Claim to Discover.

Its genuinely new contribution would be to connect three operations that are usually treated separately:

Detection: whether something arrives.

Recognition: whether the receiver can perceive what has arrived.

Classification: whether the receiver permits what it has recognized to exist as a legitimate intellectual object.

The third operation is especially important. A receiver can recognize something and still refuse its implications. An institution can acknowledge a textual witness while denying the consequences of that witness for its account of a tradition.

This produces a deeper theory than simple institutional exclusion: institutions do not merely lose evidence. They can manufacture the appearance that the evidence they cannot accommodate was never there.

Abstract
Three problems that appear unrelated are, in fact, instances of the same epistemological failure.

The search for extraterrestrial intelligence cannot infer absence from silence when it does not know the probability that an arriving signal would be recognized. Classical philology cannot infer the absence of a textual tradition from the surviving record when its own criteria determine which witnesses count. And historical criticism cannot infer forgery merely because a work fails to conform to the documentary conventions through which authorship has subsequently been classified.

These are not simply three examples of institutional bias. They reveal a more fundamental problem: the receiver participates in producing the evidence from which it subsequently constructs its account of reality.

I propose a distinction between arrival, recognition, admission, and historical incorporation. These are four separate events, each with its own failure conditions. A signal may arrive without recognition. A text may be recognized without being admitted as evidence. Evidence may be admitted without its implications being incorporated into the receiving institution's account of history.

The resulting proposition is that historical absence is frequently receiver-relative. What appears to have been absent may instead have been present but illegible, illegitimate, or incompatible with the categories through which the receiving system organizes its world.

1. The receiver is part of the experiment
What Arrival Cannot Do establishes a fundamental distinction between the number of signals that arrive and the probability that an arriving signal is recognized.

Let:

λ represent the expected number of arriving signals.
q represent the probability that an arriving signal is recognized.
A null result does not independently establish that λ is zero. It constrains the product of arrival and recognition.

The consequence is more profound than its application to extraterrestrial intelligence initially suggests.

Every investigation of the past is also an experiment conducted by a receiver.

The surviving evidence does not enter an intellectually neutral apparatus. It enters systems of recognition that have already acquired expectations about what evidence should look like, which forms of authorship are possible, what constitutes a historical witness, and which relationships between texts are admissible.

The historian is therefore not merely searching for evidence. The historian is operating a detector.

And, as with the astronomical detector, the absence of a detection cannot establish the absence of the thing detected unless the detector's capacity has itself been investigated.

3. The classification of what has been recognized
Either Forgery or Fact supplies the next step.

The binary between documentary fact and forgery is not simply a method for distinguishing authentic from inauthentic works. It is also a classification system that determines what kinds of intellectual production can be acknowledged.

The essay's concept of faithful extension provides a third possibility: a tradition can continue producing its own works, persons, and voices without those extensions being either documentary reports or fraudulent imitations.

This is where the recognition problem becomes especially consequential.

Suppose a work explicitly identifies an earlier voice as its source. A receiving institution may recognize that reference but classify the resulting work as derivative, fictitious, or unreliable because it expects a different relationship between source and composition.

The institution has not failed to see the object.

It has failed to recognize the kind of object it is.

This distinction matters because the resulting historical judgment can become circular:

The institution establishes a definition of legitimate historical evidence.
A work that does not satisfy that definition is excluded or downgraded.
The resulting historical account contains no legitimate evidence of the alternative possibility.
That absence is subsequently used to validate the original definition.
The receiver has produced the absence that it then claims to have discovered.

4. The fourth problem: recognition can be deliberately preserved without consequence
The three works together permit a further distinction that none needs to establish independently.

There are at least four ways in which an intellectual object can fail to enter history:

Failure of transmission. The object does not survive or arrive.

Failure of detection. The object survives but is not recognized.

Failure of admission. The object is recognized but denied evidentiary standing.

Failure of incorporation. The object is admitted, but the historical account is not revised to accommodate its implications.

The fourth is particularly important.

An institution may preserve an objection, publish a dissenting paper, catalogue a manuscript, or acknowledge a disputed interpretation without allowing any of these things to change the structure of its account.

It can therefore appear open to criticism while remaining structurally immune to criticism.

This is not necessarily conscious suppression. It can arise through the ordinary operation of disciplinary procedures, editorial conventions, evidentiary standards, and inherited classifications.

But the result is the same: recognition becomes a ceremonial act rather than an epistemic one.

5. A new concept: the recognition debt
I propose the term recognition debt for the accumulated difference between what a receiving institution has encountered and what its historical account has actually incorporated.

Recognition debt arises whenever evidence has been encountered but its implications have not been followed through.

It is not simply the quantity of neglected evidence. It includes:

Textual witnesses whose existence is acknowledged but whose historical implications remain excluded.
Alternative accounts that are cited but never allowed to alter the prevailing chronology.
Works classified through categories that prevent their own internal claims from being understood.
Disagreements that are recorded as matters of interpretation when they actually concern the constitution of the object being interpreted.
Recognition debt accumulates because each generation inherits not only the surviving evidence but also the unresolved exclusions of its predecessors.

It is therefore possible for a discipline to become increasingly sophisticated while carrying an increasingly large recognition debt.

Indeed, its sophistication may partly depend upon that debt: elaborate interpretive systems can develop around the absence of evidence whose implications would otherwise require those systems to change.

6. The archive as a receiver that must audit itself
This argument returns directly to the problem that gave rise to Alexanarch.

The archive was founded in response to the disappearance of intellectual work from an institutional platform. But preserving a work is only the first operation.

A sovereign archive must also preserve the conditions under which its works can be recognized, challenged, extended, and reinterpreted.

That requires more than provenance metadata.

It requires a record of failed recognition, contested classification, unresolved evidentiary relationships, and the difference between an institution's acknowledgment of a work and its actual incorporation of that work's implications.

The archive should therefore preserve not just what it knows, but how it came to know it, what it has refused to infer, and what remains possible given the evidence it possesses.

This produces a new archival principle:

An archive should make its own recognition failures as inspectable as the objects it successfully preserves.

That principle would also apply to Alexanarch itself. Its classifications, heteronymic relationships, historical hypotheses, and theories of faithful extension must remain open to the same scrutiny they apply to other institutions.

Otherwise the archive would reproduce, at a higher level of sophistication, the very recognition problem it identifies elsewhere.

7. The final implication: history is partly the history of receivers
The conventional account of intellectual history follows works, authors, institutions, and ideas.

The argument developed here adds another historical subject: the receiving apparatus.

We should ask not only what texts were produced, what survived, and who read them, but also:

What could particular readers recognize?
What did their institutions permit them to admit?
Which recognized objects were prevented from changing established accounts?
Which historical absences were actually products of the receiving system?
How did the receiver's own categories change, and what previously illegible objects became visible as a consequence?
This changes the meaning of historical continuity.

A tradition does not survive merely because its works remain available. It survives when subsequent receivers remain capable of recognizing what the tradition is doing, including when it extends itself into forms that the receiving institution does not yet understand.

The deepest connection between the three source works is therefore this:

The universe may contain signals we cannot recognize. The historical record may contain witnesses we cannot admit. And a literary tradition may continue its work in forms that our categories cannot accommodate.

In all three cases, the receiver mistakes the limits of its own capacity for the limits of reality.

The most important question is consequently not just what has survived.

It is what our systems of recognition have made impossible for us to see, and what would happen to our histories if we finally learned to see it.

Why this is a worthwhile new direction
The synthesis adds three things to the existing arguments:

A unified model: arrival, recognition, admission, and incorporation become distinguishable stages of knowledge production.

Recognition debt: a new concept for measuring the distance between what an institution acknowledges and what it allows to change its understanding.

A reflexive archival principle: the archive must document its own failures of recognition, not just preserve the objects that successfully pass through its systems.

The most important conceptual leap is the third. It turns Alexanarch's theory of institutional criticism into a theory of institutional self-audit.

It also creates a testable research programme: examine cases in which evidence was preserved and acknowledged but failed to change the historical account, then identify precisely where the recognition process broke down.

That, I think, is a more productive next step than another general essay about how archives preserve knowledge.
