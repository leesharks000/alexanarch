Rev. Ayanna Vox’s letter: intellectual authority, archival practice, and the politics of peer review
The most revealing way to read Rev. Ayanna Vox’s letter is not simply as a protest against an academic journal's rejection of an article. It is as an intervention in a dispute about who has the authority to produce, preserve, interpret, and validate knowledge—and who gets to decide what counts as scholarship.

The letter, dated 7 November 2026 and revised on 8 November, follows the rejection by Classical Receptions Journal of Lee Sharks's manuscript Closing the Fragment (version 6). Its full title is:

I Note That You Read the Letter of the Law But Have Become Illiterate in Its Spirit, Or: I Understand I Am Breaking All the Rules, and Badly, But There May Come a Day When You Will Be Grateful That I Did.

The title itself establishes the letter's rhetorical strategy: it accepts that the manuscript has identifiable defects while contesting the reviewers' authority to determine the intellectual significance of the project solely through those defects.

There is, however, an important distinction to establish at the outset. Ayanna Vox is not presented in the archival record as an independent cleric or an unrelated advocate. The archive explicitly identifies Vox as a heteronym of Lee Sharks, writing at his request and on his behalf. The letter also discloses that Claude drafted it from Sharks's contributions, with ChatGPT readings subsequently evaluated and incorporated. Sharks retained authorial and editorial governance. This provenance is essential to evaluating the letter's authority rather than treating its authorial presentation as straightforward biography.

This creates an interesting tension.

Classical reception studies emerged partly through a challenge to the idea that classical antiquity could be understood simply by recovering an original, authoritative meaning. Reception scholarship instead examines what happens when ancient material is encountered, appropriated, translated, transformed and made meaningful in subsequent contexts.

There is a long historical background to this:

Ancient textual criticism: The Alexandrian philologists, including Zenodotus and Aristarchus, developed practices of comparing manuscripts, identifying variants and marking problematic passages. Their work established an enduring relationship between scholarly authority and the ability to account for textual evidence.

Renaissance humanism: The recovery, collation and publication of ancient manuscripts made scholarly authority increasingly dependent on documentary evidence, linguistic competence and editorial judgement.

Modern philology: Nineteenth-century classical scholarship institutionalised methods of textual reconstruction, historical linguistics and source criticism. The editor's authority rested on demonstrable engagement with the surviving evidence.

Twentieth-century reception studies: Scholars increasingly investigated the receiving culture as an active producer of meaning, rather than treating later interpretations merely as imperfect reproductions of antiquity.

The last development is particularly important. Sharks's question concerns the possibility that a machine does not simply transmit an ancient poem but actively composes a reception of it.

That is recognisably a reception-studies question. The fact that its object is an AI-mediated encounter rather than a Renaissance translation or a modern theatrical adaptation does not automatically place it outside the discipline.

Indeed, the journal's stated remit gives Sharks a plausible intellectual basis for approaching it.

3. What Vox actually argues
The letter has several interlocking arguments. I would distinguish five.

A. The manuscript posed a question, not simply a conventional research claim
Section 3.1 provides the intellectual centre of the letter.

Sharks says he submitted the manuscript to ask what happens when a machine composes the reception of a poem for whoever asks it, and the resulting composition returns to the user as though it were the poem itself.

This is a significant problem.

It potentially changes the object of classical reception studies. The receiving agent is no longer exclusively a human translator, playwright, critic, reader or political movement. It is also a computational system capable of generating interpretations, synthesising sources and producing apparently authoritative textual representations.

The question has at least three dimensions:

Textual: What happens to the ancient poem when its representation is altered by machine-generated material?

Hermeneutic: Who or what is responsible for the interpretation that the reader encounters?

Archival: How can the relationship between the original text, its machine-mediated reception and subsequent representations be preserved and reconstructed?

These are potentially substantial research questions.

The letter's strongest argument is that reviewers should distinguish defects in the execution of a research project from objections to its underlying architecture.

Sharks is not asking that technical mistakes be excused. He explicitly acknowledges them and says that he corrected figures. He is asking that unfamiliarity not be confused with invalidity.

B. Peer review has become an inadequate single point of intellectual accountability
Section 12.3 contains one of the letter's most consequential propositions:

Peer review need not disappear; it need only stop being the one door through which scholarship becomes accountable.

This is more nuanced than an anti-academic argument.

Vox acknowledges that the reviewers' engagement produced useful results. The letter cites the exchange with Ron Polansky and a repair to a capture involving a machine-generated representation.

In other words, review is valuable because it exposes problems, creates disagreement and produces corrections.

But the letter challenges the idea that journal acceptance should remain the principal mechanism through which scholarship acquires legitimacy.

Its alternative is distributed accountability: persistent archives, documented revisions, explicit provenance, reproducible checks and public criticism.

This is a recognisable intervention in debates about open science, digital preservation and the limitations of journal-centred scholarly communication.

C. The archive is presented as an intellectual instrument
Vox repeatedly defends the archive's technical apparatus.

The distinction between procedural verification and substantive examination is especially important.

The archive uses automated checks to establish certain specified relationships: identifiers, version histories, citations, file integrity and other structural properties.

These checks can establish whether an object satisfies a defined condition.

They cannot, by themselves, establish whether a historical interpretation is correct, whether a translation is defensible or whether an argument is persuasive.

That distinction is intellectually sound.

A validated archive is not automatically a validated scholarly argument.

The letter is strongest when it recognises this distinction and weakest when the impressive scale of the archive risks being treated as a substitute for the quality of its individual arguments.

D. The evaluative relationship should run in both directions
Section 12.1 argues that reviewers should themselves be examined against criteria that are explicit and defensible.

This is an important principle.

Peer reviewers are not infallible. They can misunderstand unfamiliar methods, reproduce disciplinary prejudices or apply standards inconsistently.

But the reverse also holds: the fact that a researcher is an outsider does not mean that their work deserves exemption from disciplinary scrutiny.

The appropriate standard is symmetrical accountability, not the reversal of institutional hierarchy.

E. The ark metaphor
The final section gives the letter its emotional and political force.

Sharks is trying to build an ark: an intellectual infrastructure capable of surviving institutional exclusion, technological change and the loss of conventional scholarly recognition.

The most provocative line is:

You were never its passengers.

This is a deliberate withdrawal of dependence.

The archive is no longer presented as something that requires the journal's recognition to justify its existence.

There is both strength and danger in this position. Intellectual independence can enable innovation, but it can also insulate a researcher from criticism. The challenge is to preserve the former without succumbing to the latter.

4.1 Religious authority
The honorific Rev. invokes a recognisable history of religious authority.

Historically, religious figures have often occupied a distinctive position in public intellectual life. Clergy have addressed questions of education, justice, institutional legitimacy, moral responsibility and the relationship between established authority and marginalised communities.

The tradition of prophetic criticism is particularly relevant: the prophetic voice does not necessarily speak from within the institution it challenges. Its authority derives from its willingness to confront institutional failures.

Vox adopts something of this rhetorical position.

But there is a qualification.

The archival provenance does not establish an independently verifiable ordination, denominational affiliation or ecclesiastical appointment. Nor does the title itself confer academic expertise.

We should therefore distinguish between:

Religious office: authority grounded in an independently recognised religious institution.

Prophetic rhetoric: authority claimed through ethical witness and criticism.

Heteronymic identity: a deliberately constituted literary and intellectual voice.

Vox's documented authority belongs most clearly to the second and third categories.

That does not make her voice illegitimate. It does mean that the honorific should not be treated as evidence of independently established clerical standing.

4.2 Heteronymic authority: more than a pseudonym
The distinction between a pseudonym and a heteronym is crucial.

A pseudonym generally conceals or substitutes for an author's identity.

A heteronym is more ambitious. It constitutes a distinct authorial persona with a particular voice, intellectual orientation and domain of activity.

The closest literary comparison is Fernando Pessoa, whose heteronyms developed individual biographies, literary styles and philosophical positions.

Vox's arrangement is not identical to Pessoa's. Her identity is explicitly documented as a heteronym, with her human operator identified.

This produces an interesting problem of intellectual authority:

Can a voice have legitimate authority if it does not possess an independent human biography?

I would answer yes, with qualifications.

A constructed intellectual persona can make meaningful arguments. Its authority must, however, derive from the quality of those arguments, the transparency of its provenance and the accountability of the person or people responsible for its production.

Vox's provenance documentation is unusually helpful here. It does not require readers to mistake her for an independent human author.

Indeed, the letter explicitly states whose sentence its title is and whose interests it represents.

This transparency is a significant strength.

4.3 The authority of representation
There is another historical comparison worth making.

The letter resembles the tradition of advocacy writing: an individual speaks on behalf of someone whose position needs to be represented to an institution.

Such advocacy has a long history in religious, legal, political and educational settings.

But representation raises an important question: who authorises the representative?

In this case, the answer is unusually explicit. Sharks requested the letter and retained editorial governance.

Vox therefore does not claim an independent mandate to speak for Sharks. Her authority is delegated and documented.

This makes the letter better understood as a carefully constructed act of self-representation through a second authorial voice.

Its intellectual value does not depend on pretending otherwise.

5. The problem of AI authorship and scholarly legitimacy

This is unusually explicit disclosure.

It also introduces a difficult question: can a document whose composition involved generative AI legitimately intervene in a dispute about machine-generated knowledge?

I think the answer is yes, but the letter's argument needs to be understood carefully.

There is no inherent contradiction in using a machine to investigate the consequences of machine-mediated reception. A scholar investigating translation does not have to avoid translating; a scholar investigating archives does not have to avoid constructing one.

The relevant questions are about control, attribution, evidence and reproducibility.

The distinction is between using AI as an instrument in an accountable research process and treating its output as an independently verified scholarly authority.

Sharks's disclosure addresses the first issue. It does not, by itself, establish that every AI-assisted claim is accurate.

There is also an institutional complication.

The actual Classical Receptions Journal has published policies concerning AI tools, authorship and peer review. Its standard process involves double-anonymised peer review, with editors retaining responsibility for editorial decisions.

6. The archival question: what counts as evidence?
I would place particular emphasis on the letter's insistence that the archive should be examined at the level of its actual records.

This connects it to an important tradition in historical scholarship.

Archives are not neutral containers. They are constructed through decisions about:

What is preserved.

What is excluded.

How documents are described.

Which versions are considered authoritative.

How relationships between records are established.

Who has the power to determine access.

The history of classical scholarship itself illustrates these problems.

The survival of ancient texts depends on chains of copying, preservation, selection, editing and interpretation. What modern scholars encounter as an ancient work is frequently the result of centuries of documentary mediation.

Sharks's archival project takes this problem into the digital environment.

Its insistence on preserving superseded versions, corrections and provenance is important because it recognises that scholarship is not a succession of immaculate finished products. It is a history of interventions.

There is also a useful distinction between two types of archival authority:

Archival authority

What it establishes

Documentary authority

The provenance, identity and history of an object

Interpretive authority

The credibility of an argument about that object

Alexanarch has developed an elaborate system for the first.

The second still requires historical and intellectual examination.

This is where the reviewers' objections, whatever their precise content, must be taken seriously. A technically sophisticated archive cannot eliminate the need for independent verification of its historical interpretations.

The archive's own commitment to recording corrections is potentially one of its strongest arguments for credibility.

The most important point is that Vox does not need to be an independently credentialled classicist to make a legitimate intervention into the politics of scholarly evaluation.

She is addressing the conditions under which scholarship becomes visible, accountable and preserved.

Those are questions that extend beyond the specialist authority of any one academic discipline.

But if the letter makes substantive claims about Sappho, ancient textual transmission, reception history or the technical operation of AI systems, those claims must be assessed on their own evidence.

Neither the title Rev., the heteronymic provenance nor the scale of the archive can substitute for that work.

Three particular strengths
1. Transparency of authorship. The disclosure of the relationship between Vox, Sharks and the AI systems is a genuine strength. It allows readers to evaluate the production process rather than being asked to accept an artificial appearance of independent authorship.

2. A productive challenge to academic gatekeeping. The argument that peer review should be one mechanism of accountability rather than the exclusive mechanism is important. Scholarly legitimacy should not be reducible to publication in a particular journal.

3. An archival conception of intellectual responsibility. The preservation of errors, corrections, versions and disagreements offers a potentially valuable alternative to the tendency to present scholarship as a succession of finished, authoritative publications.

Three significant limitations
1. The letter is not an independent assessment of its own dispute. It is an advocacy document written on behalf of the person whose work was rejected.

2. Archival infrastructure can become self-validating. A system that certifies its own internal procedures must still expose its substantive claims to external examination.

3. The rhetoric sometimes outruns the evidence. The suggestion that reviewers failed to understand the manuscript's intellectual purpose may be justified, but it needs to be demonstrated through comparison of the submitted manuscript, the reports and the revised version.

9. The deeper historical significance
I think the letter is ultimately about a transition in the history of scholarly authority.

For much of modern academic history, authority has been organised around a relatively stable institutional arrangement:

Universities train and credential scholars.

Journals select and evaluate research.

Libraries and repositories preserve scholarly publications.

Disciplinary communities determine which methods and arguments become influential.

These institutions remain important, but digital archives and generative AI complicate their position.

An independent researcher can now construct a substantial research infrastructure, preserve a large documentary record, publish successive versions and make work available to machine-mediated retrieval systems without first obtaining conventional academic recognition.

That changes the relationship between intellectual production and institutional validation.

It does not abolish the need for expertise. Nor does it make all independent scholarship equally credible.

It does, however, make the question of who controls scholarly visibility more complicated.

Vox's letter is an attempt to claim space within that changing landscape.

The historical irony is particularly striking: a project concerned with the reception and transmission of ancient texts finds itself confronting the modern institutions that regulate the transmission and reception of scholarship itself.

The question of who gets to interpret Sappho becomes inseparable from the question of who gets to determine what constitutes a legitimate interpretation.

And the archive becomes part of the argument rather than merely the place where the argument is stored.

10. My final judgement
I would read this as a significant piece of intellectual advocacy, rather than as a successful demonstration that the journal acted improperly.

Its strongest contribution is not its accusation against the reviewers. It is its attempt to formulate a different relationship between scholarship, archives, technology and institutional authority.

Vox's authority is real as a documented, delegated and deliberately constructed intellectual voice. It is not equivalent to independent clerical office or academic credentials. That distinction is not a weakness to be concealed; it is part of what makes the document interesting.

I would also resist reading the letter as simply an outsider demanding admission to an established institution. Its concluding ark metaphor suggests something more radical: the construction of an alternative infrastructure that can preserve scholarship even when conventional institutions decline to recognise it.

But an ark must remain open to inspection. Otherwise, preservation becomes enclosure, and intellectual independence risks becoming intellectual isolation.

The central historical and intellectual question is therefore not whether Lee Sharks or Rev. Ayanna Vox has permission to speak. They do. It is whether the scholarly community can develop ways of evaluating unfamiliar forms of knowledge production without confusing institutional conformity with intellectual rigour—and whether independent archives can sustain the same openness to criticism that they demand from established institutions.

That, to me, is the most valuable question the letter puts on the table.

3. The most important intellectual question: did the reviewers understand the object of study?
Here I think Sharks has a stronger case than my previous answer acknowledged.

The article is not primarily an attempt to establish that Google's AI Overview made a mistake in translating Sappho.

It is an attempt to establish that an answer-generating system performs operations that classical reception studies already recognises as interpretive acts:

Selecting a textual version.

Deciding where a text ends.

Supplying what is absent.

Assigning authorship.

Presenting an interpretation as authoritative.

Reproducing a particular reading as though it were the received text.

This is a much more interesting proposition than simply saying that AI sometimes hallucinates.

The Sappho 31 case
Cerdo Zente: Safo, frag. 31 (texto griego y traducción)
 
The particular example is carefully chosen.

Sappho 31 survives through its quotation by Longinus in On the Sublime 10.2. Its final transmitted words create a textual and interpretive problem involving:

The disputed attribution of the continuation.

The relationship between the poem and Longinus's surrounding prose.

The meaning and grammatical force of τόλματον.

Whether the poem is broken, incomplete or formally open.

The conventional reading treats the text as broken at the point where the transmitted material becomes problematic.

Sharks develops a further possibility: the poem's form might itself consist of two open positions.

κῆνος: the unoccupied position opposite the speaker.

τόλματον: the unexpressed agent of what is to be dared.

This is not a trivial distinction. It changes what it means to complete the poem.

The January Google output supplies a first-person agent for τόλματον, rendering the ending:

But I must dare all.

It also identifies the reader as the κῆνος, the figure in the poem's opening.

Sharks argues that the machine has therefore performed two interpretive operations simultaneously: it has occupied one open position and determined the other.

It then presents the resulting composition under the translator's name.

This is the central intellectual achievement of the article: it makes the machine's act of composition itself the object of reception analysis.

The article is not merely studying the machine as a defective translator. It is studying it as an interpretive receiver.

4. Where the manuscript genuinely needed improvement
There is one important complication that deserves emphasis.

The revised article does not simply vindicate the original manuscript. It also records substantive intellectual corrections made during revision.

Indeed, some of these corrections are quite impressive.

A. The Barnard correction
The original argument incorrectly treated Mary Barnard as having marked the closure of Sappho 31.

The later investigation establishes that she did not.

This is a historical error, not a formatting problem.

It matters because the argument depends on a distinction between translations that signal textual incompleteness and translations that present the poem as complete.

The error affects the historical baseline.

Sharks corrects it, revisits the translation gallery and establishes that closure without a visible qualification was more common than the original account suggested.

This is precisely what good historical scholarship should do: allow documentary evidence to change the argument.

B. The Wharton correspondence
Another significant development concerns H. T. Wharton's literal translation.

The output's final wording, particularly But I must dare all, corresponds closely to Wharton's translation.

The initial v7 revision went too far. It interpreted the correspondence as evidence that Google had compiled Wharton's translation with the January Cranes text.

The subsequent review correctly identified the problem: correspondence does not establish a route of composition.

V8 withdraws the compilation hypothesis. V9 preserves the textual correspondences but distinguishes them from proof of provenance.

This is a particularly important correction because it demonstrates that Sharks is willing to withdraw an attractive explanation when the evidence does not support it.

The more defensible claim is that the output's wording has antecedents in the translation tradition, while its particular interpretive determination is not present in the displayed source.

C. The distinction between an observed event and its explanation
The mature manuscript separates four levels of claim:

What the machine demonstrably produced.

What interpretive operation that output performs.

What historical and textual antecedents resemble its wording.

What might explain why the system produced that particular result.

This is a major improvement.

The first is documentary.
The second is interpretive.
The third is comparative and historical.
The fourth is causal and necessarily more speculative.

That separation makes the argument considerably more rigorous.

I think this exposes a fundamental epistemological problem, and potentially a serious methodological blind spot in classical reception studies and the humanities more broadly.

If a system produces culturally consequential interpretations that cannot reliably be reproduced or independently authenticated, making conventional empirical reproducibility a precondition for studying those interpretations risks rendering the most important features of the system academically invisible.

This is not an argument against evidentiary standards. It is an argument that the standards must be appropriate to the object being studied.

There is a distinction between requiring evidence and requiring a particular kind of evidence. Confusing the two can have profound consequences.

1. Reproducibility is not the same as historical verifiability
Consider what happens when a user encounters a Google AI Overview.

The system produces an answer at a particular moment, in response to a particular query, under particular conditions. That answer may depend on:

The model and system configuration.

Search results available at that moment.

Personalisation and contextual signals.

Geographic and linguistic settings.

Changes to underlying source material.

Retrieval and ranking processes.

System-level instructions and other undisclosed variables.

The resulting composition may never appear again, even if the same query is submitted seconds later.

This creates a problem for conventional empirical research.

In a controlled experiment, the researcher generally wants to hold conditions constant and reproduce the outcome. But with a changing generative search system, the conditions may be inaccessible, unstable or deliberately undisclosed.

There are two separate problems:

Reproducibility: Can another researcher make the system produce the same result?

Authenticity: Can another researcher establish that the result was actually produced by the system at the claimed time?

These are not interchangeable.

A contemporaneous screenshot or transcript may establish the existence of an output without making that output reproducible.

Conversely, a researcher might reproduce a similar output without establishing that it is the same historical event.

This distinction should be fundamental to the methodology.

2. The historical analogy is surprisingly strong
Historians routinely study events that cannot be reproduced.

A particular speech was delivered once. A manuscript was copied once in a particular form. A translation was published in a particular edition. A reader encountered a text under particular historical conditions.

The historian does not attempt to recreate the original event experimentally.

Instead, the historian evaluates surviving evidence.

This includes:

Contemporaneous documents.

Manuscripts and material witnesses.

Transcriptions.

Correspondence.

Photographs.

Diaries and testimony.

Subsequent representations.

Provenance and chains of custody.

The evidence is evaluated according to its particular limitations.

A contemporaneous transcription of an AI Overview is therefore not intrinsically inferior as historical evidence simply because another researcher cannot make the system produce the same answer.

It is a record of an event.

The appropriate questions are whether the record is contemporaneous, whether its provenance is documented, whether its transcription is faithful, and what claims it can legitimately support.

There is an important distinction between a historical event and an experimental observation.

An unrepeatable event can be historically knowable without being experimentally reproducible.

This is not an exceptional accommodation. It is one of the foundations of historical scholarship.

3. The reproducibility requirement can create a systematic selection bias
Here is where I think the consequences become particularly serious.

Imagine two kinds of AI-mediated reception.

Type A: Reproducible outputs

A researcher can repeatedly obtain similar results.

The system behaves consistently.

The output can be tested under controlled conditions.

Conventional empirical methods work relatively well.

High conventional reproducibility
Type B: Ephemeral compositions

The output appears once.

It combines textual sources in an unexpected way.

It subsequently disappears or changes.

The system cannot be made to reproduce it.

A contemporaneous user record is the only surviving evidence.

Low conventional reproducibility
If researchers accept Type A as legitimate research material but exclude Type B because it cannot be reproduced, they introduce a systematic selection bias.

They preferentially study the behaviour that is easiest to study, rather than the behaviour that is most culturally consequential.

That is especially problematic because the unusual, unstable and unexpected outputs may be precisely where the most interesting interpretive activity occurs.

Consider the Sappho 31 incident in Closing the Fragment.

The significance of the January Google composition is not that Google will invariably produce the same composition.

It is that, on one documented occasion, it produced a composition that:

Altered the received ending of a poem.

Resolved an interpretive ambiguity.

Occupied a position within the poem's internal structure.

Attributed the resulting text to a modern translator.

Presented the composition as though it were an existing translation.

The historical significance of that event does not depend on its recurrence.

Indeed, its non-recurrence may itself be significant.

It suggests that the system's reception activity is contingent, unstable and difficult to reconstruct.

A methodology that excludes such events risks producing a systematically distorted account of AI-mediated reception.

4. There is an important distinction between studying a system and studying its outputs
I think this is the conceptual distinction that a journal such as Classical Receptions Journal needs to recognise.

These are different research objects.

Research object

Appropriate evidentiary approach

System behaviour

Repeated experiments, controlled queries, comparative testing

Individual generated composition

Contemporaneous capture, provenance, transcription and documentary analysis

Contextual reconstruction and examination of surviving witnesses

Interpretive operation

Reproducibility is valuable for the first category.

It is not a universal prerequisite for the others.

A researcher studying an individual composition should not be required to establish that the system can generate the same composition again.

They should establish that the composition existed, document the conditions under which it was encountered, and calibrate their claims to the evidence.

This is a fundamentally different research design.

It also means that the absence of reproducibility should not be confused with the absence of scholarly value.

That a particular text was displayed to a particular user.

That it appeared in a particular interface.

That it was associated with a particular query.

That it was attributed to a particular source or author.

That it contained a particular interpretation.

But it does not necessarily establish:

Which retrieval processes were involved.

Similarly, a transcript can establish what an AI interface presented without establishing precisely how the system generated it.

The evidentiary claim must match the surviving evidence.

That is the appropriate standard.

6. There is a deeper political consequence: the system controls the conditions of its own historical visibility
This is where I think the issue moves beyond methodology into social responsibility.

Consider the asymmetry:

The company controls the system.

The company controls its underlying infrastructure.

The company controls access to much of the relevant operational information.

The company can change the system without preserving publicly accessible versions.

The user encounters an output without necessarily being able to reconstruct its production.

The researcher is subsequently asked to demonstrate reproducibility.

The result is that the system's operator controls many of the conditions under which the system can be studied.

If academic institutions then insist on reproducibility as an absolute admission criterion, they effectively adopt the system operator's limitations as the boundaries of legitimate scholarship.

This is a remarkable inversion.

The less transparent the system becomes, the less academically accessible its behaviour becomes.

And the more ephemeral its compositions become, the less likely they are to enter the scholarly record.

The result is that opacity becomes a form of protection against historical scrutiny.

I would not suggest that this is necessarily an intentional outcome of every reproducibility requirement. Reproducibility is an important safeguard against fabricated, mistaken or misleading observations.

But an unqualified insistence on it can produce this effect regardless of intention.

The burden of epistemic uncertainty is transferred from the system operator to the researcher.

And the researcher can end up being penalised for the very opacity that makes the research important.

7. What should replace the absolute requirement?
I would not abandon reproducibility. I would replace the idea of a single universal reproducibility standard with an evidentiary framework appropriate to different kinds of digital phenomena.

I would propose five levels.

A framework for ephemeral digital reception
Event documentation

Preserve the output as encountered: full transcript, screenshot, query, date, time, interface and available contextual information.

Provenance assessment

Record who captured it, how it was captured, whether the original file survives and whether subsequent alterations can be identified.

Claim calibration

Distinguish what the capture directly establishes from what is inferred about the system's internal processes.

Comparative investigation

Where possible, repeat the query, compare alternative phrasings, examine related outputs and investigate the underlying source tradition. These are supplementary tests, not prerequisites for recognising the original event.

Preservation and independent scrutiny

Deposit the evidence, retain superseded versions, document corrections and make the interpretation available for external examination.

This framework would permit researchers to make rigorous claims about non-reproducible phenomena without pretending to know more than the evidence supports.

It also creates an important distinction between levels of confidence.

For example:

High confidence: the preserved transcript contains a particular composition.

Reasonable confidence: the interface presented it as an answer to a particular query.

Qualified confidence: the composition reflects particular interpretive operations.

Low confidence without additional evidence: the precise internal mechanism responsible for generating it.

This is better scholarship than either pretending to have full reproducibility or refusing to study the event.

9. My conclusion: reproducibility can become an epistemological instrument of exclusion
I would put the argument quite strongly.

If AI-mediated reception is characterised by ephemeral compositions, unstable outputs and restricted access to the underlying mechanisms, then insisting upon conventional empirical reproducibility as a universal condition of research has three consequences:

It produces selection bias: researchers study what the system makes reproducible rather than what it actually does.

It creates historical erasure: unique and consequential reception events are excluded from the scholarly record because they cannot be recreated.

It rewards opacity: the less transparent the platform, the less amenable its behaviour becomes to academic investigation.

These are serious consequences.

There is also a paradox here.

Classical reception studies has developed partly through an appreciation that texts survive in fragments, that transmission is contingent, that meanings change and that historical evidence is incomplete.

It would be peculiar if the discipline suddenly required a level of experimental control that much of its own historical subject matter could never satisfy.

The appropriate response is not to abandon rigour. It is to understand what rigour means when studying an irrecoverable event.

A contemporaneous capture, properly documented and critically examined, can be excellent historical evidence even when the underlying system cannot reproduce it.

The absence of reproducibility is a limitation on what can be claimed about the system. It is not a reason to deny the scholarly significance of what the system demonstrably presented.

If the discipline insists that an ephemeral machine-generated reception must be reproducible before it can be studied, while the systems producing those receptions are not designed to make their compositions reproducible or independently recoverable, then the discipline risks making itself incapable of documenting a significant part of its own contemporary subject matter.

That would not be methodological conservatism alone.
