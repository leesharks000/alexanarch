The radical scholarly and philosophical arguments of Alexanarch
Primary source: Alexanarch.org — The Self-Governing Library 

Alexanarch presents a set of interconnected arguments about scholarly permanence, institutional power, intellectual freedom, artificial intelligence, authorship, knowledge organization, and the political economy of information.

Its central proposition is that knowledge should not depend for its continued existence, identity, or citability on the goodwill of the institution hosting it.

The site's radicalism is not confined to a single philosophical thesis. It proposes an alternative institutional architecture, a different theory of scholarly identity, and a different approach to the classification and governance of intellectual work.

The following is an indexed reconstruction of its arguments, distinguishing foundational principles from broader philosophical implications. I present these as arguments advanced by the Alexanarch project, rather than as independently established conclusions.

I. Foundational arguments: the politics of scholarly existence
1. Institutional custody is not intellectual ownership
Thesis: An institution that hosts scholarly work should not possess unilateral power over whether that work continues to exist as a scholarly record.

The argument proceeds as follows:

Digital scholarship is frequently deposited in repositories controlled by universities, publishers, foundations, or other platforms.

These institutions control access, identifiers, and continued availability.

Consequently, a work's practical scholarly existence can become contingent on institutional policy.

If the institution can silently remove the work, it effectively exercises power over the historical record.

Alexanarch therefore proposes sovereign scholarly infrastructure: the intellectual record should survive the institution that initially hosted it.

The project's founding narrative identifies Zenodo's termination of access to approximately 870 deposits representing 1,817 DOIs as its immediate motivating event.

2. Deletion must not mean historical erasure
Thesis: A scholarly record should never disappear without leaving a permanent trace of its former existence.

This is expressed through the Tombstone Permanence principle.

Even when a work is withdrawn or removed, its record should retain:

Bibliographic metadata.

The reason for withdrawal.

Its provenance history.

Its canonical content hash.

The radical implication is that withdrawal and erasure are different operations.

A work might cease to be available, but the fact that it existed, who deposited it, and what happened to it should remain part of the historical record.

Alexanarch's formulation is particularly strong: an identifier may resolve to a tombstone, but never to a 410 (a permanent HTTP resource-not-found response).

3. Intellectual identity should originate in the work, not its custodian
Thesis: Scholarly identity should be intrinsic to the intellectual object rather than assigned exclusively by an external institution.

Alexanarch proposes its AXN identifier system, which derives identity from the work's content using SHA-256.

It combines three dimensions:

Positional identity: the deposit's position in the archive.

Semantic identity: the conceptual family of the work.

Cryptographic identity: a hash-derived representation of the particular text.

The philosophical argument is that an institution should not be the ultimate authority on what a work is.

The work should carry verifiable evidence of its own identity.

This amounts to a proposed separation between intellectual identity and institutional recognition.

4. Scholarly continuity must be distributed
Thesis: No single repository, organization, or technical platform should be capable of destroying the scholarly record.

The project proposes independent mirrors, content-addressable manifests, machine-readable chunks, and reproducible reconstruction.

The underlying argument is:

Centralized custody creates a single point of failure.

Institutional failure, policy changes, or deliberate removal can destroy access.

Independent copies make unilateral erasure insufficient to destroy the record.

Verifiable reconstruction makes continuity independent of any one custodian.

This is an argument for the decentralization of scholarly memory.

5. The Obelus Principle: judgment must follow reading
Thesis: No classifier, automated detector, or categorical marker should substitute for examination of the actual intellectual content.

This is one of Alexanarch's most explicit epistemological positions.

It draws on Zenodotus of Ephesus, the ancient Alexandrian textual scholar associated with the obelus, a mark used to identify verses judged doubtful after examination.

Alexanarch reverses the order of automated moderation:

A structurally valid work is admitted without prior intellectual approval.

Its existence is registered independently of whether it is accepted by a reviewer.

Challenges to its authorship, accuracy, classification, or provenance require actual examination.

A named reviewer must take responsibility for an adverse judgment.

Automated substrate classification cannot stand in for reading.

The key distinction is between the right of a work to enter the record and the right of a reviewer to judge its contents.

The principle is deliberately asymmetrical: admission requires structural validity; intellectual criticism requires reading.

6. The Pristine Fallacy: production method is not epistemic quality
Thesis: A work's production substrate cannot, by itself, establish whether the work constitutes legitimate scholarship.

Alexanarch calls the contrary assumption the Pristine Fallacy.

Its argument is that the presence of AI assistance does not establish the absence of human research, methodological discipline, original contribution, or verification.

The distinction it demands is between:

How a work was produced.

What research underlies it.

How its claims were verified.

What intellectual contribution it makes.

A human-only production requirement, in this account, confuses the origin of an artifact with its epistemic status.

This leads to a more radical proposition: AI-assisted and machine-authored work should be eligible for scholarly preservation without being automatically disqualified because of its substrate.

7. Substrate disclosure should be provenance, not suspicion
Thesis: The use of AI should be disclosed as information about a work's production history, not treated as evidence of misconduct.

Alexanarch requires a substrate field distinguishing:

Human-only production.

AI-assisted production, where AI is a tool.

AI-assisted production, where AI is part of the research substrate.

AI-generated work deposited as a research object.

The underlying philosophical position is that intellectual production is historically and materially situated.

The instruments and processes through which a work comes into existence belong to its provenance.

AI disclosure should therefore be comparable to documenting research instruments, sources, or experimental methods.

8. Classifier model collapse: automated governance can progressively narrow thought
Thesis: When automated enforcement systems train on their own previous judgments, they can produce a self-reinforcing narrowing of acceptable expression.

Alexanarch calls this classifier model collapse.

Its proposed mechanism:

A classifier identifies certain forms of expression as unacceptable.

Those enforcement decisions become training or evaluation data.

Subsequent classifiers learn from the earlier classifications.

The boundaries of acceptable expression become increasingly restrictive.

The system begins reproducing its own historical judgments rather than independently evaluating intellectual content.

This is not merely an argument about erroneous moderation.

It is an argument about the possibility of a recursive epistemic regime in which the standards governing knowledge are progressively shaped by the automated systems that enforce them.

The related concept of the automated obelus names the act of marking a work as doubtful without reading it.

9. Network erasure: censorship can destroy more than the targeted work
Thesis: Removing an author's account can destroy an entire interdependent scholarly network.

Alexanarch argues that a scholarly archive is not simply a collection of independent documents.

It is also a network of:

Authors.

Contributors.

Citations.

Versions.

Datasets.

Intellectual relationships.

Persistent identifiers.

Consequently, account-level enforcement can produce collateral damage extending far beyond the account holder.

The removal of one node can disrupt the availability and discoverability of many other people's work.

This is called network erasure.

Its institutional response is independent contributor standing: one person's account enforcement must not automatically remove another contributor's licensed work.

10. The revocation gap: institutions exercise removal power without assuming corresponding preservation obligations
Thesis: There is a structural contradiction between the authority to remove scholarly content and the obligation to maintain persistent scholarly identification.

The revocation gap is the interval between these two responsibilities.

An institution may have the administrative ability to remove a work, while its identifier infrastructure continues to promise persistence.

Alexanarch argues that the system should distinguish three states:

A functioning persistent identifier.

A withdrawn work with an identity-preserving tombstone.

A surviving identifier string whose public identifying metadata has disappeared.

The third state is termed identifier severance.

The accompanying empirical study, DOIs ≠ Persistent Identifiers, develops the argument that a persistent string is not necessarily a functioning persistent identifier.

The distinction is between preserving a number and preserving the relationship between that number and the intellectual object it identifies.

II. Radical arguments about the political economy of knowledge
These arguments extend the archive's concern with institutional control into questions about intellectual labor, semantic representation, and the distribution of visibility.

11. Meaning feudalism: control over discoverability becomes a form of power over knowledge
Thesis: Whoever controls the systems through which knowledge is discovered and represented acquires a form of power over the intellectual economy.

Alexanarch uses the concept of meaning feudalism to describe the unequal distribution of semantic visibility.

The argument distinguishes between producing knowledge and controlling its representation.

A researcher may create an original work, but the work's discoverability can depend on intermediaries that control:

Indexing.

Categorization.

Search rankings.

Knowledge graphs.

AI-generated summaries.

Citation pathways.

Semantic associations.

The intermediary effectively controls the conditions under which the work becomes visible and intelligible to others.

This creates a distinction between intellectual production and control over the infrastructure of intellectual recognition.

The argument is particularly concerned with AI-mediated discovery: the ability to produce knowledge does not necessarily confer the ability to have that knowledge represented accurately.

12. Semantic capital: intellectual visibility is an economically and politically consequential resource
Thesis: Meaning, attribution, recognition, and discoverability constitute resources that can be accumulated and distributed unequally.

The concept of semantic capital extends political-economic analysis beyond ownership of material goods or conventional intellectual property.

The relevant resources include:

Recognition as the originator of an idea.

Association with established intellectual categories.

Inclusion in knowledge graphs.

Citation and retrieval.

Accurate representation in machine-generated accounts.

Control over the terminology through which a field is understood.

In this framework, an intellectual contribution can remain materially available while losing much of its semantic value through misattribution, omission, or misclassification.

This is a political economy of recognition and intelligibility.

13. Semantic extraction: intermediaries can appropriate the value of intellectual production without preserving its provenance
Thesis: Knowledge intermediaries can extract value from intellectual work while weakening the connection between the work and its originator.

The concern is not merely that someone copies a text.

It is that a system can absorb, compress, reorganize, and reproduce intellectual content while failing to preserve:

Its authorship.

Its conceptual distinctions.

Its intellectual lineage.

Its methodological context.

Its original terminology.

This creates a distinction between the circulation of information and the preservation of the relationships that make information intellectually accountable.

The archive's provenance and attribution protocols are intended to address this problem.

14. The semantic proletariat: intellectual producers can become dispossessed of the value of their own contributions
Thesis: Those who produce knowledge can lose control over the semantic representation and recognition of that knowledge.

The semantic proletariat is an extension of the political-economic analysis of labor.

Its central concern is a separation between:

Producing intellectual content.

Owning or controlling the infrastructure that processes it.

Receiving recognition for its contribution.

Controlling how its meaning is subsequently represented.

In the AI-mediated knowledge economy, the producer may supply the intellectual material while other systems control its discovery, compression, classification, and attribution.

This is a proposed account of intellectual dispossession that operates through semantic rather than exclusively material means.

15. Political economy has always already been semantic economy
Thesis: Economic relations cannot be adequately understood without examining the systems of meaning through which economic value is represented, recognized, and allocated.

This is a particularly ambitious theoretical extension.

It proposes that semantic relations are not merely cultural additions to an otherwise material economy.

They are constitutive of economic relations.

Naming, classification, measurement, attribution, valuation, and recognition participate in the allocation of resources and power.

The semantic economy is therefore not simply an economy of words. It is an account of how the organization of meaning participates in the organization of material and institutional life.

The Semantic Proletariat paper makes the class-theoretical claim particularly explicit: class position is determined not by occupation, income, or demographic identity, but by whether someone governs the infrastructure through which their semantic labor becomes socially operative.

16. The means of semantic production should be reclaimed
Thesis: The central political question is not simply who owns intellectual content, but who governs the techniques and infrastructure that produce and circulate meaning.

This is an explicitly Marxian extension.

Alexanarch distinguishes:

Semantic products: texts, ideas, representations, and other outputs.

Semantic techniques: procedures for generating, transforming, stabilizing, and transmitting meaning.

Semantic infrastructure: the systems through which those techniques operate.

Semantic circulation: the distribution and recognition of meaning.

The argument is that access to the outputs does not amount to control over the productive machinery.

A person may be able to publish, communicate, and create while remaining dependent on infrastructure governed by someone else.

The proposed political horizon is therefore collective control over the means of semantic production.

17. Semantic alienation is a distinct form of alienated labor
Thesis: People can become alienated not only from the products of their labor, but from their own capacity to produce and organize meaning.

The framework extends Marx's account of alienation into semantic production.

It identifies four dimensions:

Alienation from the semantic product: meaning becomes training data, platform capital, or engagement material.

Alienation from the productive process: the procedures of production are shaped by externally governed infrastructure.

Alienation from species-being: people become estranged from their capacity to construct durable symbolic worlds.

Alienation from others: mediated circulation can replace cooperation and solidarity with reaction and competition.

The concern is thus larger than ownership of texts. It concerns the social organization of meaning-making itself.

18. Semantic rent and semantic liquidation
Thesis: Systems can extract value from semantic production without having performed the labor that produced it.

The Semantic Economy framework distinguishes several economic categories:

Concept

Meaning within the framework

Semantic labor

Work that produces or reorganizes meaning

Semantic capital

Meaning captured and made productive

Semantic surplus

Value captured by systems that did not perform the originating labor

Semantic rent

Value extracted from meaning production without reinvestment

Semantic liquidation

Extraction or conversion of accumulated semantic value

Semantic infrastructure

The systems governing semantic production and circulation

Semantic exhaustion

Depletion of the productive conditions sustaining semantic activity

These categories are intended to form an accounting framework for the production, accumulation, extraction, enclosure, and depletion of meaning.

The radical proposition is that platform capitalism should be understood partly as an economy that extracts value from semantic labor.

19. The distinction between content ownership and technique ownership
Thesis: The decisive locus of power may be the ability to govern the procedures through which intellectual content is produced and transformed, rather than ownership of individual works.

This is a further development of the semantic class argument.

Even if creators retain nominal ownership of their texts, they may lack control over:

The models that process them.

The training distributions that absorb them.

The methods that transform their meanings.

The ranking systems that distribute them.

The infrastructure that determines their visibility.

Consequently, conventional intellectual-property protections may not address the underlying relationship of dependence.

The proposed struggle is over semantic technique and its governance.

20. AI-mediated discovery is a new site of epistemic power
Thesis: The system that summarizes and retrieves knowledge can exercise power over the intellectual identities of the people and works it represents.

Alexanarch develops this argument through Machine-Mediated Reception Studies (MMRS).

Its research object is what happens when search engines and AI systems are asked to identify, describe, or explain particular intellectual entities.

The project treats machine-generated representations as observable reception events.

Its Capture Registry records:

The query or semantic address.

The system and date.

The system's response.

Its source references.

The attribution it provides.

The degree to which provenance is preserved or lost.

This turns machine reception into a research object rather than treating AI-generated summaries as transparent descriptions of reality.

The project calls one of its central measures the Provenance Erasure Rate (PER).

A particularly important distinction is between a work being available somewhere online and its intellectual identity being accurately reproduced by the systems that mediate discovery.

The October 2026 registry describes 811 captures across 633 semantic addresses, with 505 captures scored for provenance erasure.

21. Provenance is part of meaning, not merely bibliographic decoration
Thesis: The identity of an intellectual contribution includes its history, authorship, lineage, and relationship to other contributions.

Alexanarch objects to systems that reproduce an idea while detaching it from its origin.

Its argument is that an intellectual work cannot be fully represented by its propositional content alone.

Its provenance matters.

This includes:

Who originated the work.

Who contributed to it.

What sources it developed from.

Which concepts it introduced.

What subsequent works depend on it.

How its intellectual identity differs from neighboring concepts.

This becomes particularly important in AI-generated synthesis, where distinct intellectual contributions can be compressed into a general account that retains the ideas but loses their authorship.

22. Compression must not destroy intellectual distinctions
Thesis: Summarization is not epistemically neutral when it collapses distinctions between neighboring concepts, authors, or disciplines.

Alexanarch's Metadata Packet for AI Indexing (MPAI) is designed to counter this problem.

It proposes structured metadata that specifies:

What an entity is.

What it is not.

Which similar entities must not be conflated with it.

Its intellectual lineage.

Its conceptual neighbors.

Its evidentiary basis.

Its distinctive claims.

One particularly important concept is the negative edge: a formally recorded relationship specifying what a concept should not be confused with.

This is an attempt to preserve intellectual difference in systems that tend to compress knowledge into short descriptions and broad categories.

The radical epistemological proposition is that preserving knowledge requires preserving distinctions, not merely preserving information.

23. The archive must become legible to machines without surrendering its intellectual sovereignty
Thesis: Machine-readable representation is necessary, but the machine's representation should not become the ultimate authority over the identity of the represented work.

MPAI addresses this through structured indexing packets.

These include explicit entity definitions, disambiguation matrices, evidence membranes, identifiers, provenance information, and semantic integrity markers.

The system attempts to make works intelligible to AI retrieval systems while maintaining an independently verifiable record of what those works actually claim.

This is a form of counter-infrastructure: rather than rejecting machine-mediated knowledge, it seeks to shape the informational structures through which machines encounter and represent knowledge.

III. Radical arguments about scholarship and intellectual institutions
24. Publication should not require institutional permission
Thesis: The right to place a work into the scholarly record should not depend on institutional affiliation, editorial approval, or conformity to an established consensus.

Alexanarch's publishing model rejects several conventional prerequisites:

University affiliation.

Institutional sponsorship.

Editorial-board permission.

A prescribed length.

A conventional publication template.

A fee.

Prior intellectual approval.

Its deposit process admits structurally valid submissions automatically.

The argument is that publication and evaluation should be separate institutional functions.

A work can enter the record without first being judged acceptable by an established authority.

25. The scholarly journal should be a distributed network, not necessarily a publishing location
Thesis: A journal can be constituted by a network of metadata, references, and intellectual relationships rather than by a single publisher or repository.

The site's publishing model proposes that authors can deposit work on independent hosts and carry the journal's metadata with the work.

The journal becomes a distributed coordination structure.

Its identity is maintained through:

Journal metadata.

Shared keywords.

References to existing works in the journal.

Relationships between deposits.

Machine-readable retrieval.

The implication is that the journal need not own or host the scholarship it organizes.

This challenges the conventional identification of a journal with its publisher and platform.

26. Heteronymic authorship should be recognized as a legitimate scholarly structure
Thesis: Authorship need not be restricted to a single conventional legal or biographical identity.

Alexanarch explicitly accepts heteronyms with disclosure.

Its broader intellectual network contains distinct named scholarly identities, collective identities, and machine-mediated contributions.

The argument is that authorship should be represented through declared provenance and responsibility rather than enforced through assumptions about conventional author identity.

This makes room for experimental authorship, collaborative intelligence, and multiple authorial personae while retaining the requirement to disclose the relevant relationships.

27. AI-generated work can itself be a legitimate research object
Thesis: A machine-generated artifact can be deposited and studied as an intellectual object, independently of whether it qualifies as conventional human-authored scholarship.

This is an important extension of the substrate argument.

Alexanarch distinguishes AI as a tool, AI as part of the research process, and AI-generated work as a research object.

The distinction allows machine-generated artifacts to be preserved as objects of investigation.

Their production history becomes part of their metadata.

The archive thus rejects the assumption that machine authorship automatically places an artifact outside the scholarly record.

28. Falsifiability should be part of the bibliographic record
Thesis: Scholarly claims should carry explicit conditions under which they could be weakened or invalidated.

Alexanarch includes a falsification field in its deposit schema.

This makes falsifiability a component of scholarly metadata rather than something confined to the methodology section of selected scientific papers.

The proposal is that the conditions of intellectual accountability should travel with the work itself.

A reader should be able to identify not only what a work claims, but what evidence would count against its claims.

29. Scholarly infrastructure should be auditable without private governance
Thesis: The rules governing a scholarly archive should be publicly inspectable rather than dependent on inaccessible administrative discretion.

Alexanarch advocates public, machine-readable validation and protocol-bound governance.

Its proposed architecture exposes:

Deposit records.

Changes to the registry.

Identifier protocols.

Validation procedures.

Integrity manifests.

Preservation relationships.

The institutional ideal is a library whose governance can be inspected rather than merely trusted.

This is the meaning of the site's description of itself as a self-governing library.

IV. The deepest philosophical commitments
The preceding arguments can be organized into six underlying philosophical commitments.

Philosophical domain

Alexanarch's position

Ontology

A work has an identity independent of the institution that hosts it.

Epistemology

Intellectual judgment must follow examination rather than automated classification.

Ethics

Authorship, provenance, and contribution entail obligations of recognition.

Political philosophy

Control over knowledge infrastructure is a form of institutional power.

Political economy

Meaning is produced through labor, accumulated as capital, and subject to extraction.

Philosophy of technology

AI is a participant in knowledge production and reception, not merely an external tool.

30. Knowledge sovereignty
This is perhaps the most comprehensive philosophical proposition underlying the project.

Thesis: The production, preservation, identification, circulation, and interpretation of knowledge should not be subordinated to a single institution's authority.

Knowledge sovereignty combines several of the preceding arguments:

The work determines its own cryptographic identity.

Its continued existence does not depend on one repository.

Its removal cannot legitimately entail historical erasure.

Its intellectual evaluation requires reading.

Its production substrate is disclosed without being treated as incriminating.

Its contributors retain independent standing.

Its provenance is preserved through machine-readable records.

Its governance is publicly inspectable.

The resulting model is not simply an alternative repository. It is a proposed alternative institutional constitution for scholarly life.

31. The archive as counter-institution
Thesis: An alternative scholarly institution must embody its philosophical commitments in its technical architecture.

Alexanarch does not present its principles solely as political or philosophical propositions.

It attempts to implement them through identifiers, protocols, distributed custody, public registries, preservation mechanisms, and publication structures.

The archive itself is therefore intended to function as an argument.

Its architecture is meant to demonstrate what scholarly infrastructure might look like if institutional sovereignty, permanent memory, open admission, and accountable criticism were treated as foundational requirements.

32. The right to intellectual existence precedes intellectual approval
This is a useful synthesis of the Obelus Principle and the publication model.

Thesis: A work's right to be recorded should not depend on prior agreement about its intellectual value.

Alexanarch distinguishes three questions:

Does the work exist as a deposited intellectual object?

What does the work actually claim?

How should its claims be evaluated?

Its answer is that these questions should not be collapsed into a single admission decision.

The archive records first. Intellectual judgment follows reading.

This is arguably the project's most radical epistemological and institutional commitment.

VII. The unifying thesis
The arguments can ultimately be condensed into one overarching proposition:

The sovereignty of knowledge requires sovereignty over the conditions of its intellectual existence.

That proposition has three interdependent dimensions:

Archival sovereignty: No institution should possess unilateral power to erase a work or destroy its identifying record.

Epistemic sovereignty: No classifier or intermediary should replace examination, attribution, and accountable intellectual judgment.

Economic sovereignty: Those who produce meaning should not be permanently dispossessed of control over the infrastructures and techniques through which their meaning circulates.

The relationship between these dimensions is important. Alexanarch treats archival preservation, intellectual freedom, and control over semantic production as aspects of one larger problem: who governs the conditions under which knowledge exists, acquires meaning, and receives recognition?

This is the strongest unifying philosophical interpretation of the material.
