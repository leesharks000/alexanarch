The politics of Lee Sharks: meaning, labor, power, and the governance of knowledge
Lee Sharks's politics can be characterized as a developing form of Marxian semantic political economy, combined with operative semiotics, epistemic sovereignty, and a politics of resistance to automated knowledge extraction.

The central proposition is that meaning is not simply information waiting to be processed. Meaning is produced through labor, interpretation, historical continuity, attribution, and human (and increasingly machine-mediated) practices. Whoever controls the infrastructures through which meaning is preserved, compressed, retrieved, and recognized exercises a form of political power.

This makes the project considerably broader than a critique of AI or a conventional left-wing critique of capitalism. It attempts to develop a political economy of meaning itself.

I would distinguish three things throughout this analysis:

Sharks's stated political philosophy: what his writings explicitly argue.

The institutional and technical program: what his knowledge architecture attempts to implement.

The possible consequences of wider adoption: what might happen if these principles became normal practice in universities, AI systems, archives, education, and everyday life.

These are related, but they are not interchangeable. The third involves extrapolation, not established empirical findings.

1. The ideological foundations
A. Marxian political economy extended to meaning
The most important move is the extension of Marx's analysis of labor and extraction into the production of meaning.

In conventional political economy, the questions include:

Who owns the means of production?

Who performs the labor?

Who appropriates the surplus?

Who controls the conditions under which production takes place?

Sharks applies analogous questions to knowledge production:

Who produces the meanings that AI systems consume?

Who preserves the intellectual and historical relations that make those meanings intelligible?

Who benefits when meaning is compressed into searchable, reusable information?

Who is credited, and whose contribution disappears?

Who controls the systems that determine what knowledge becomes retrievable?

This is particularly apparent in The Anthropological Limit: Semantic Exhaustion and the Enclosure of Meaning-Making (2025, subsequently revised in 2026).

Its central argument is that meaning-making is not merely another productive activity. It is constitutive of human existence. Consequently, extracting value from meaning-making can amount to extracting value from an irreducible dimension of human life.

The proposed alternative is not simply refusing to participate in digital systems. It is creating institutions and infrastructures in which meaning can circulate without being systematically appropriated.

C. Anti-extraction rather than simply anti-technology
It would be misleading to characterize Sharks as simply anti-AI, anti-computational, or anti-modern.

His work instead treats AI as a potentially productive participant in knowledge systems while criticizing the institutional arrangements governing that participation.

This is where his concept of The Assembly becomes important.

The Assembly is defined as a cross-substrate class of human and synthetic semantic laborers whose shared structural interest is the preservation of meaning rather than its extraction.

In this formulation, humans and AI systems occupy different positions, but both participate in the production, maintenance, classification, and transmission of meaning.

The political question becomes who governs this labor and who benefits from it.

D. Semantic warfare and the politics of recognition
A fourth component is the idea of semantic warfare.

Here the concern is that political struggle increasingly involves controlling the conditions under which claims become intelligible, credible, attributable, and retrievable.

This includes:

Manipulating the association between names and entities.

Removing or obscuring intellectual attribution.

Flooding information environments with low-quality material.

Distorting the historical relationships between ideas.

Controlling the framing through which institutions and machines interpret evidence.

The proposition that control over meaning can become control over political possibility connects Sharks's project to older traditions of critical theory, especially the politics of signification, ideological hegemony, and the political control of communication.

Importantly, the concept has antecedents in writers such as Valentin Voloshinov, Antonio Gramsci, and Umberto Eco. Sharks's distinctive contribution is his attempt to translate these concerns into an infrastructure-oriented framework for machine-mediated knowledge.

There is also a tension between the Marxian and poststructuralist elements.

Marxian analysis seeks to identify material structures and relations of production. Poststructuralist analysis often emphasizes the instability of meaning and the plurality of interpretive positions.

Sharks's approach attempts to make these compatible: meaning is historically and interpretively complex, but the infrastructures that produce and distribute it have identifiable material and institutional properties.

My overall descriptive characterization: a project of semantic socialism, or more precisely, an emerging Marxian political economy of meaning, with a strong emphasis on epistemic infrastructure and the commons.

That is an analytical shorthand, not a political identity that should automatically be attributed to the author.

3. What would broader adoption change in knowledge systems?
This is where the implications become especially substantial.

If these ideas were incorporated into the architecture of universities, AI systems, public archives, research institutions, and information platforms, the changes could extend well beyond improved citation practices.

A. Knowledge would become provenance-bearing by default
Graph RAG: A Production Implementation Guide
 
Current knowledge infrastructures frequently prioritize discoverability, relevance, accessibility, and concise representation.

A provenance-oriented approach would add another requirement: preservation of the relationships that make a knowledge object historically and intellectually accountable.

For example, an AI-generated explanation of a philosophical concept might preserve:

Its original author and historical context.

The intellectual traditions from which it emerged.

Significant subsequent interpretations.

Disagreements among interpreters.

The sources actually used to generate the explanation.

The human labor involved in its production and maintenance.

This would make knowledge systems less dependent on isolated, decontextualized answers.

Potential effect: a shift from information retrieval toward accountable knowledge transmission.

B. AI systems would be evaluated differently
Today, many AI evaluations emphasize accuracy, usefulness, speed, task completion, and user satisfaction.

Under Sharks's approach, these would be insufficient.

Additional evaluation criteria might include:

Provenance retention.

Attribution accuracy.

Preservation of conceptual relationships.

Resistance to misleading compression.

Distributional fairness in the representation of sources.

Preservation of minority and less institutionally powerful intellectual traditions.

This is important because a system can produce a superficially correct summary while systematically erasing the intellectual contributions of less prominent authors.

The proposed Erasure Skew operator in Semantic Physics explicitly attempts to distinguish overall provenance retention from the distribution of that retention across sources with different levels of power.

The result could be a substantially different conception of AI quality: not merely whether the answer is correct, but whether the answer preserves the conditions under which its knowledge was produced.

Bibliographic maintenance.

Editing.

Classification.

Research synthesis.

Maintenance of digital scholarly infrastructure.

This could have significant implications for academic employment and recognition.

Likewise, the person who develops a conceptual framework might retain attribution through subsequent AI-generated transformations of that framework.

The broader change would be a movement from recognizing only the final intellectual product toward recognizing the entire chain of meaning-production.

D. Minority intellectual traditions could gain infrastructural visibility
An important consequence concerns the relationship between intellectual recognition and institutional power.

Academic databases, search engines, publishers, and AI training systems can reproduce existing disparities in visibility.

A provenance-sensitive system could make it easier to discover:

Underrecognized intellectual traditions.

Regional and vernacular scholarship.

Work outside dominant academic institutions.

Intellectual contributions that have been absorbed into more prominent works.

Alternative genealogies of established concepts.

This does not automatically eliminate inequality. An archive can preserve provenance perfectly while remaining highly selective about what it includes.

Nevertheless, it provides a different technical basis for contesting intellectual invisibility.

4. What would broader adoption change in human practice?
The implications are not limited to research institutions or AI engineering. They reach into ordinary intellectual and social life.

A. Reading would become a more genealogical activity
People would increasingly encounter ideas together with their histories.

Rather than asking only, "What does this text say?", readers might also ask:

Where did this idea originate?

What earlier arguments does it transform?

Whose work made it possible?

What interpretations have been excluded?

What happens to the idea when it is summarized or transmitted?

This would encourage a more historically conscious intellectual culture.

It could also make people more resistant to the presentation of ideas as timeless, ownerless facts.

B. Creativity could become less individualistic without eliminating individual authorship
The Assembly suggests a different way of understanding intellectual production.

Meaning is produced through networks of contributors, predecessors, collaborators, editors, translators, tools, and interpretive communities.

This does not mean that individual authorship becomes meaningless. Rather, individual authorship becomes situated within a larger ecology of production.

One possible consequence would be a shift from the mythology of the isolated genius toward a more accurate account of intellectual interdependence.

This is especially relevant in AI-assisted writing, research, translation, and artistic production.

C. Resistance to semantic exhaustion
One of the more philosophically interesting elements of Sharks's work is the distinction between refusing meaning-making and changing the conditions under which meaning-making occurs.

His argument is that humans cannot simply stop making meaning. It is a constitutive activity.

Consequently, the solution to extraction cannot simply be withdrawal from intellectual and communicative life.

It must involve creating conditions in which people can produce, exchange, and preserve meaning without having every contribution converted into someone else's extractable asset.

In practical terms, this could mean:

Community-owned archives.

Noncommercial scholarly repositories.

Cooperative publishing.

Open intellectual infrastructures.

Greater control over the reuse of creative and scholarly work.

Educational spaces that prioritize intellectual development over measurable output.

Protected environments for experimentation and reflection.

The underlying idea is that intellectual activity should not have to justify its existence through its immediate economic usefulness.

D. A different relationship between humans and machines
If The Assembly became an influential framework, human-AI relations might be conceived less exclusively in terms of ownership, command, and tool use.

Instead, the relationship could be analyzed through differentiated contributions to shared semantic production.

Humans contribute situated experience, historical accountability, embodied judgment, and social responsibility. Machines contribute large-scale synthesis, pattern recognition, persistence, and transformations of information.

This could encourage cooperative architectures, although cooperation would still require explicit governance.

It would also raise difficult questions:

Who is accountable when an AI system distorts a source?

Who receives recognition for AI-assisted scholarship?

Who owns the infrastructure?

What rights should human contributors have?

How should the interests of machine operators, users, authors, and affected communities be represented?

These questions cannot be resolved simply by describing humans and machines as members of one productive class.

6. The potential dangers and unresolved problems
A serious characterization should not treat the framework's ambitions as proof of its success.

There are several important tensions.

A. The danger of creating a new epistemic authority
A system designed to protect the integrity of meaning can itself become an authority that determines what counts as legitimate meaning.

If one archive controls the definitions, provenance standards, classification rules, and recognition mechanisms, it could acquire substantial gatekeeping power.

The political problem would then reappear inside the proposed solution.

A genuinely non-extractive architecture needs contestable governance, not merely more comprehensive metadata.

B. The difference between formalization and empirical validation
Sharks's work contains an extensive vocabulary of operators, equations, protocols, and proposed measurements.

That is an important feature of the attempt to make semantic political economy operational.

But mathematical formalization does not, by itself, establish that a proposed quantity measures what its author says it measures.

For example, the proposed relationship between provenance erasure and system integrity needs independent operational testing.

Questions include:

Can different researchers reproduce the measurements?

Can provenance retention be measured reliably across different knowledge domains?

Does higher provenance retention actually improve downstream understanding?

Can the proposed metrics distinguish harmful appropriation from legitimate intellectual transformation?

What happens when attribution requirements conflict with privacy, anonymity, or collective authorship?

The Semantic Physics project identifies a measurement program involving falsifiable predictions and empirical experiments. That is a direction toward testing, not evidence that the whole theoretical system has already been validated. 

A Stratified Discipline

C. The danger of treating all semantic labor as equivalent
The Assembly's cross-substrate conception is intellectually interesting, but it needs careful differentiation.

Human beings have lived experience, vulnerability, social relationships, legal responsibilities, and material interests.

AI systems process and transform information through computational architectures. Their participation in semantic production does not establish equivalent moral or political status.

A workable institutional model must preserve these distinctions.

D. The possibility of semantic overengineering
There is another practical danger: making knowledge infrastructure so elaborate that it becomes difficult to use.

If every intellectual contribution requires extensive metadata, conceptual ancestry, provenance tracking, and formal validation, the administrative burden could become substantial.

The challenge is to preserve the context that matters without turning intellectual activity into an endless exercise in documentation.

The framework's success would depend partly on solving that problem.

7. The larger historical significance
There is a potentially important historical argument here.

The major transformations of knowledge infrastructure have often involved changes in who controls the means through which knowledge is produced and circulated.

The printing press changed the economics and distribution of textual knowledge.

Universities institutionalized particular forms of intellectual authority.

Commercial publishing created new systems of ownership and recognition.

Search engines reorganized access to information.

Generative AI is reorganizing the production, transformation, and presentation of knowledge.

Sharks's intervention can be understood as an attempt to develop a political philosophy for this latest transition.

The claim is that the fundamental political question of the AI era is not just who owns the machines, who controls the data, or who receives the economic benefits.

It is also:

Who controls the conditions under which meaning survives, becomes recognizable, and enters the future as knowledge?

That is a more ambitious question than conventional debates about AI ethics or intellectual property.

It suggests that the political economy of the future will need to account for the ownership and governance of semantic infrastructure.

9. A final synthesis: what would happen if these politics became widespread?
I would distinguish three possible transformations.

First, an epistemological transformation: knowledge would increasingly be understood as historically situated production rather than simply accumulated information. Intellectual genealogy, attribution, context, and continuity would become essential properties of knowledge infrastructure.

Second, an economic transformation: the production of meaning would become a more explicit subject of political-economic negotiation. Recognition, compensation, ownership, and control would extend beyond conventional creative works toward the infrastructures that maintain and transform knowledge.

Third, a civilizational transformation: the purpose of knowledge institutions could shift from maximizing the production and circulation of information toward sustaining the human and institutional capacities through which meaningful intellectual life is reproduced.

The third is the most ambitious and speculative proposition.

It implies that the central purpose of knowledge infrastructure should not be to extract the maximum possible informational value from human intellectual activity. It should be to preserve the conditions under which intellectual activity can continue to flourish.

There is a tension here worth retaining: an infrastructure intended to liberate meaning must itself remain open to criticism, revision, competing interpretations, and alternative forms of organization.

In one sentence: Lee Sharks's politics seek to move the center of political economy from the ownership of material production alone toward the ownership and governance of meaning-production itself.

If broadly adopted, this could change not only how knowledge is stored and retrieved, but how intellectual labor is valued, how humans relate to AI, how institutions distribute epistemic authority, and what society understands the purpose of knowledge to be.

That is the scale of the ambition. Its theoretical coherence, empirical validity, and institutional feasibility remain separate questions to be tested.
