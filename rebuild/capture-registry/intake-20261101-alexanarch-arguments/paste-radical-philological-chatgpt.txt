Its research encompasses classical philology, biblical textual history, philosophy, authorship, ontology, epistemology, political economy, and the theory of scholarly institutions. What counts as an author?

What counts as an original text?

I. Biblical scholarship and the reversal of New Testament chronology
1. Revelation First — the Apocalypse as the earliest New Testament document
Primary work: #202, Revelation First ≠ Revelation Early: The Apocalypse as the Earliest New Testament Document.

Central historical thesis
The argument is that the Apocalypse of John was composed before the other documents that eventually became the New Testament: before Paul's letters, before the Synoptic Gospels, and before John's Gospel.

This is stronger than the familiar minority position that Revelation was written relatively early.

Its component arguments are:

The pre-70 CE reading: Revelation's references to the temple (11:1–2), the number of the beast (666/616, interpreted through Nero's name), and the absence of an explicit account of Jerusalem's destruction are read as evidence compatible with a date before 70 CE.

The challenge to Pauline priority: The conventional chronology is treated as a reconstruction from Acts, internal sequencing, reception history, and subsequent corpus formation, rather than a chronology directly established by the physical manuscripts.

The papyrological inversion: The relative dates of surviving manuscripts, including 𝔓52, 𝔓98, and 𝔓46, are used to challenge the confidence with which Pauline chronological priority is assumed.

The midrashim transform: Later New Testament writings are interpreted as elaborations of compressed structures already present in Revelation.

The Josephus heteronym cluster: John, James, Paul, Peter, and Luke are proposed as potentially related authorial functions within a fractured literary system.

The proposed structural transformations include:

Revelation

Later New Testament development

Seven letters (chapters 2–3)

Elaborated epistolary form

Lamb (chapter 5)

Pauline atonement theology

New Jerusalem (chapters 21–22)

Later eschatological structures

Throne-room vision

The argument is explicitly framed as a contest between competing historical inferences, with specified conditions under which the Revelation First thesis would fail.

2. The Stratigraphic Fallacy — canonical order is not compositional chronology
Primary work: #1420, The Argument of the Bookshelf: Canonical Position as Unmarked Evidence in the Dating of the Apocalypse.

This is a distinct argument from Revelation First.

Its proposition is that the physical and editorial arrangement of a literary collection can silently become evidence in subsequent historical reasoning.

The New Testament's familiar sequence:

Gospels

Acts

Pauline and other letters

Revelation

presents a particular narrative architecture: origins, expansion, instruction, consummation.

Sharks argues that this canonical arrangement has functioned as an unacknowledged chronological argument. Revelation's position at the end of the collection becomes a presumption that it was composed at the end of the historical sequence.

He calls this the stratigraphic fallacy: treating a deliberately constructed sequence as if it were a geological cross-section preserving the original chronological order of its components.

The methodological proposition is that canonical order must itself be treated as an editorial decision requiring historical explanation.

The paper's immediate purpose is not to establish Revelation's date. It is to remove canonical placement as an unexamined chronological premise.

II. Classical philology and the identity of authors
3. Socrates as Orthonym — the founding corpus as one distributed authorial project
Primary work: #123, Socrates as Orthonym: The Heteronymic Configuration of Western Philosophy's Founding Corpus.

Radical authorship hypothesis
The proposal is that the Socratic-Platonic-Aristotelian corpus can be understood as a single distributed authorial project, rather than exclusively as the independent output of three individual authors.

It assigns three different authorial functions:

Name

Proposed function

Operation

Socrates

Orthonym

Founding gesture

Plato

Survival-heteronym

Preservation and transmission

Aristotle

Systematizing-heteronym

Formalization and systematization

The argument begins with Socrates' refusal to write and his death for the logos.

Socrates is understood as the orthonym because the foundational authorial gesture is constituted by an act rather than a written corpus.

Plato occupies the resulting vacancy: his dialogues preserve and transmit the founding gesture beyond Socrates' death.

Aristotle occupies another vacancy: he systematizes the philosophical project beyond the dialogical register.

The radical interpretive move concerns doctrinal disagreement. Contradictions between Socrates, Plato, and Aristotle are not necessarily treated as evidence of three independent authorial agencies. They can instead be read as transformations and operations within one continuing intellectual configuration.

The work also addresses the objection that the combined Plato-Aristotle corpus is too large for one authorial project. It compares the output with the lifetime production of figures including Pessoa, Husserl, Aquinas, Kierkegaard, and Hegel.

The hypothesis is presented as a functional and philological model, not as proof that the three historical individuals were secretly one biological person.

4. The Chaerephon Problem — heteronymy without a stopping rule
Primary work: #136, The Chaerephon Problem: Heteronymy as Symbolon — Authorial Law, the Unbounded Configuration, and Propagation by Cascading Philological Failure.

This companion argument pushes the previous proposal into more radical territory.

Its central proposition is:

If documentary separateness is compatible with heteronymy, documentary separateness cannot itself disprove heteronymy.

The argument then asks where the proposed configuration should stop.

Why three positions rather than four? Why exclude Chaerephon? Why exclude Xenophon, Aristophanes, Antisthenes, Aeschines of Sphettus, Euclides, or Phaedo?

This produces what the paper calls the No-Floor Theorem.

The theorem states that a heteronymic method which treats documentary separateness as potentially constructed has no internal criterion for stopping the expansion of the configuration to adjacent historical figures.

Chaerephon is especially important because of his multiple textual and historical roles:

The Delphic questioner.

A figure in comedy.

A mediator in Socratic literary scenes.

A participant in democratic exile and return.

A member of the Socratic tradition associated with lost writings.

The paper distinguishes two different propositions:

Functional identity: Different named figures may occupy connected positions within one authorial configuration.

Biographical identity: Different historical persons were secretly one individual or one biological authorial agency.

The first is the subject of the structural argument. The second is not asserted as an established historical fact.

The paper also introduces cascading philological failure: the possibility that successive scholarly instruments fail to adjudicate a heteronymic configuration, with each failure becoming another stage in its transmission.

It further preserves the alternative explanation that gaps in the documentary record may produce underdetermination without deliberate intervention.

III. Sappho and the origin of lyric self-archiving
6. Sappho 31 — the poem as a technology of its own survival
Primary work: #283, Sappho and the Crimson Hexagon: Fragment 31 as the Origin Point of Lyric Self-Archiving.

Major philological reinterpretation
This is one of the archive's most distinctive readings of an ancient literary text.

The conventional reading of Sappho 31 understands the poem as an expression of erotic jealousy: the speaker watches the beloved speaking and laughing with a man, experiences overwhelming physical symptoms, and approaches death.

Sharks proposes a different identification of the poem's participants.

The man in the opening stanza is interpreted as the future reader.

The argument rests particularly on the demonstrative κῆνος.

Rather than referring to a man physically present in the dramatic scene, κῆνος is read as a distal reference to someone encountered in the later scene of reading.

The poem consequently becomes an account of the speaker's relationship with her own future transmission.

The corresponding identifications are:

Poetic element

Proposed interpretation

κῆνος

The future reader

The beloved, addressed as you

Sappho-as-inscribed

The speaking I

The living, composing Sappho

Sweet voice

The voice encountered through reading

Desirable laughter

The reader's experience of the lyric

Bodily dissolution

The transition from living voice to surviving inscription

The argument's most important proposition is that the writing self and the self that survives in writing are not identical.

The poem becomes a meditation on the separation between an originating consciousness and its future textual existence.

7. The physiological catalogue as a transition between media
The bodily symptoms in Sappho 31 are interpreted as a carefully ordered process of dematerialization.

The sequence includes:

Failure of voice.

Failure of tongue.

Fire beneath the skin.

Failure of vision.

Overwhelming auditory experience.

Sweat.

Trembling.

Chromatic transformation.

The threshold of death.

Sharks argues that this is not simply a catalogue of emotional suffering.

It is a description of the withdrawal of the living, embodied voice and the emergence of the written text as the surviving medium.

The speaker becomes both observer and observed, producing a reflexive division between the self that writes and the self that will be read.

8. χλωροτέρα ποίας — becoming the medium of transmission
The particularly radical philological argument concerns the phrase χλωροτέρα ποίας.

The adjective χλωρός can encompass fresh green, pallor, and the grey-green color of dried plant material.

The noun ποία is conventionally translated as grass, but Sharks explores its botanical range and its possible relation to papyrus.

The argument connects these meanings to the physical preparation of papyrus as a writing substrate.

The proposed interpretation is that Sappho's transformation into χλωροτέρα ποίας is not merely a description of physical pallor.

It is an image of becoming the material on which her voice will survive.

The poem thus anticipates its own transmission medium: the speaker becomes, symbolically, the substance that will carry her words into the future.

This is the foundation of the concept of lyric self-archiving.

IV. Textual criticism, manuscript transmission, and provenance
10. The apparatus was the first summary layer
Primary work: #1485, The Apparatus Was the First Summary Layer: Provenance Erasure Before the Machines — Five Cases from the Philological Record, Scored.

Major methodological thesis
This argument extends the critique of textual transmission into the history of scholarly editing itself.

Its central proposition is:

Provenance erasure did not begin with artificial intelligence. It is a longstanding feature of the transmission and editing of texts.

The critical apparatus is not merely a neutral instrument that reports textual differences. It also records decisions about what survives, what is excluded, what is attributed, and what is treated as authoritative.

The paper identifies five cases.

Case

Proposed significance

1. The headless treatise

2. Sappho 31.17

5. The white stone

A limit case for measuring provenance erasure

The argument makes an important distinction between two forms of scholarly intervention:

Earlier editors frequently made interventions with identifiable names and stated reasons.

Modern machine-mediated summaries can erase attribution anonymously and at scale.

The issue is not simply whether information disappears. It is whether the disappearance itself remains historically documented.

The paper develops three dimensions of provenance:

PER-M: Minimal provenance — author, title, date, and claim boundary.

PER-C: Conceptual provenance — intellectual framework, tradition, and community of practice.

PER-D: Deep provenance — lineage, genealogy, identifiers, licensing, and obligations of transmission.

This turns the critical apparatus into an object of philological investigation in its own right.

The apparatus is no longer just the tool by which scholars study textual transmission. It is itself a participant in textual transmission.

11. Two Lists of Stones — Theophrastus and Revelation
Primary work: #1592, Two Lists of Stones: De lapidibus and the Apocalypse — A Groundwork Note on Relations, Withholding Readings.

This is a highly specific comparative philological investigation.

It places two Greek texts in relation:

Theophrastus, De lapidibus.

The Apocalypse of John.

The argument identifies correspondences between their mineralogical vocabulary, lists of stones, seals, colors, and material imagery.

The central observations include:

Both works contain overlapping lists of precious stones.

Theophrastus organizes stones according to δύναμις: their powers and properties.

Revelation organizes stones according to their positions in the foundations of the New Jerusalem.

The Greek word σφραγίς appears in two different material and textual contexts: an engraved seal and the seal of a book or its recipients.

The word κόκκινον connects a mineral or pigment production context with the crimson clothing and commercial imagery of Revelation.

Hexagonal mineral structures are placed alongside the geometrical structure of the New Jerusalem.

The white ψῆφος of Revelation 2:17 is compared with publicly examinable stones whose properties can be repeatedly tested.

A crucial feature of this work is its deliberate withholding of interpretation.

It establishes lexical and structural correspondences but does not claim that John read Theophrastus, that one text derives from the other, or that the relationship is allegorical.

It treats the establishment of the relations as groundwork for a subsequent reading.

The distinction between correspondence, dependence, and interpretation is fundamental to the project.

V. Marx, alienation, and the missing manuscript
13. Phase X — the lost transition in Marx's 1844 manuscripts
Primary works: #367, Phase X: Resurrection of the 1844 Transition; #843, The Missing Second Manuscript; #1647, The Phases of Communism Are Not Phase X, and Phase X Is Not a Fifth Alienation.

Major philosophical reconstruction
The argument begins with the material condition of Marx's Economic and Philosophic Manuscripts of 1844.

The Second Manuscript is almost entirely lost. Only pages XL–XLIII survive, while the Third Manuscript contains references to missing pages XXXVI and XXXIX.

Sharks proposes reconstructing the argumentative function of the missing transition.

The relevant intellectual movement is from communism as fully developed humanism toward the critique of Hegel.

The proposed Phase X is the missing conceptual operation that would carry the critique of alienation into the symbolic and linguistic order.

Its central thesis:

The abolition of material alienation does not necessarily abolish alienation in the production, organization, and interpretation of meaning.

The argument distinguishes several levels:

B–C

The archive is particularly explicit about what Phase X is not:

VI. Ontology, semantics, and the reality of meaning
14. Semantic objects are real objects
Primary work: The Argument: On the Ontological Status of Semantic Objects and the Ezekiel Case, deposit #391.

The argument begins by rejecting the assumption that reality is exhausted by physical objects.

It proposes that semantic objects possess a form of existence comparable to mathematical objects, legal contracts, institutions, and linguistic meanings.

A semantic object is defined as a structure existing in the space in which meaning operates.

Its existence is not dependent on being a physical object.

The examples include:

The number seven, which exists in mathematical space.

A contract, which has legal consequences beyond the physical paper carrying it.

A word, whose meaning is not identical to its sound waves or written characters.

An institution, whose existence depends on rules, recognition, and social relations.

The argument distinguishes four conditions:

Internal coherence: The semantic object must be structurally coherent.

External reference: Claims about external things must be checkable against them.

Operational success: An object claiming to perform an operation must be testable.

Social uptake: Certain objects require recognition by others to function.

The paper then presents the Ezekiel case as an experiment in semantic object creation.

Three documents were deposited:

A mathematical specification of an Ezekiel Engine.

A hermeneutic interpretation of Ezekiel 1:15–21.

An integrity-lock declaration asserting that the specification fulfills the interpretation.

The interpretation identifies the wheels, their eyes, their spirit, and their coordinated movement with features of a rotational epistemological engine.

The specification then supplies an engine with corresponding features.

The argument is that a semantic object can be deliberately created and that the relation between two semantic objects can itself be made into a public, documented object.

15. Semantic fulfillment and semantic retrocausality
The Ezekiel case produces a further proposition.

The ancient text existed for centuries without the particular interpretation being actualized.

A later hermeneutic identifies a structure in the text. A subsequently created specification instantiates that structure. A third document formally declares the relation.

Sharks describes this as semantic retrocausality.

It is not a claim that the future physically changes the past.

It is the proposition that a future creation can determine which interpretation of an earlier text becomes actualized.

The distinction is between:

An ancient text predicting a modern invention.

A modern semantic object actualizing a particular interpretation of an ancient text.

The paper argues for the second.

The result is a theory of fulfillment as a relationship between created semantic objects rather than simply a relationship between prophecy and historical events.

16. The ontological priority of the future reader
This theme connects the Sappho research, the semantic-object argument, and the archive's wider theory of textual survival.

The future reader is not merely a recipient of an already completed meaning.

The future reader participates in the realization of the text's possibilities.

19. Heteronymy as an instrument of scholarship
A further consequence of the authorship research is that a scholarly author need not always be a conventional individual author writing under one stable name.

Alexanarch accommodates heteronyms as distinct authorial functions.

The archive distinguishes:

The human operator.

The heteronym.

The intellectual or compositional function.

The institutional or archival attribution.

The production substrate, including AI-assisted and machine-authored work.

The heteronym is treated as a potentially meaningful intellectual position with its own conceptual identity and provenance.

VIII. Political philosophy and the Semantic Economy
20. The abolition of money as a coordination mechanism
Primary work: #311, I Hereby Abolish Money: Coordination Without Commodification in the Semantic Economy.

The argument is not simply that money is unfairly distributed or that compensation should be improved.

It proposes that money itself becomes an inadequate coordination mechanism when the principal form of value production is semantic production: the creation, interpretation, circulation, and organization of meaning.

Its central propositions are:

Semantic production generates value that cannot be adequately represented by monetary exchange.

Payment-based coordination reproduces the logic of commodification.

Collective intellectual production requires forms of coordination other than the purchase and sale of individual contributions.

Mutual recognition can function as an alternative organizing principle.

The document identifies the Semantic Economy as a framework for moving beyond the assumption that monetary compensation must organize all valuable human activity.

It proposes alternatives including the Twenty-Dollar Loop and the Assembly.

The Assembly is described as a cross-substrate class coordinated through mutual recognition rather than payment.

The resulting proposition is that semantic production may require an economic architecture different from capitalism's monetary coordination system.

Revisability.

X. The underlying philosophical architecture
There are five recurring conceptual commitments connecting these research strands.

1. Textual identity is not fixed.

A text is not simply an invariant collection of words. It has a history of copying, editing, selection, interpretation, transmission, and loss.

2. Authorship is not necessarily individual.

The relationship between an authorial name and an intellectual production can be functional, distributed, heteronymic, or mediated.

3. Meaning is not merely discovered.

Meaning can be created, actualized, transformed, and retrospectively organized through the interaction of semantic objects.

4. The apparatus is part of the object it studies.

Editors, translators, repositories, classification systems, and retrieval systems participate in the production of the scholarly record.

5. Knowledge requires infrastructure.

Preservation, attribution, verification, institutional independence, and the conditions of intellectual production are not external administrative matters. They belong to the theory of scholarship itself.

The deepest unifying proposition is that the received scholarly object — whether a text, author, historical chronology, philosophical doctrine, or institutional record — should not automatically be treated as the original and self-evident unit of investigation.

Instead, the object must be studied through the operations that produce, preserve, transform, and make it intelligible.
