I interpret least radical to mean arguments that require the fewest departures from established scholarly methods or accepted philosophical positions. I interpret most normative to mean arguments that can be stated in conventional academic language, using familiar disciplinary concepts and defensible methodological principles.

I will present the arguments descriptively, without ranking their validity, endorsing them, or evaluating their evidential strength.

One qualification about coverage: the following is a broad thematic index of identifiable research strands and accessible primary texts, not a claim that every deposit in the archive has been individually examined.

I. General scholarly and methodological arguments
These are the arguments most readily expressible in conventional academic terminology.

1. Research should be verifiable independently of institutional affiliation
Central argument: The validity of research should depend on its declared methods, evidence, and reproducibility rather than the institutional status of its author.

The associated propositions are:

Institutional affiliation is not itself a verification procedure.

Independent researchers should be able to make claims that can be checked by other researchers.

Research should disclose its evidentiary basis.

The procedures used to reach conclusions should be inspectable.

Research should remain open to correction.

A researcher's lack of institutional affiliation should not, by itself, determine the admissibility of their work.

This is developed in The Verifiable Research Basis: A Method Standard for Unaffiliated Scholarship (2026).

The paper specifies five verification registers: empirical, philological/textual, hermeneutic/argumentative, practice/craft, and operative. It thereby treats verification as appropriate to the type of claim being made, rather than imposing one universal evidentiary procedure.

2. Scholarly claims should declare their evidentiary basis
Central argument: A claim should be accompanied by an account of what kind of evidence could establish, weaken, or invalidate it.

This entails:

Distinguishing primary evidence from secondary interpretation.

Identifying the relevant textual witnesses or empirical materials.

Declaring the methods used.

Making the reasoning reproducible where possible.

Stating failure conditions.

Preserving corrections and revisions.

Distinguishing an unresolved question from a failed investigation.

The standard applies this principle across disciplines. A philological argument, for example, requires access to the relevant textual witnesses; an empirical argument requires procedures and data that can be independently examined.

3. Scholarly records should preserve their revision histories
Central argument: The history of a scholarly work is part of its evidentiary record.

A work should not be represented solely by its latest version. Its development may include:

Initial hypotheses.

Corrections.

Changes in terminology.

Revisions to methods.

Rejected interpretations.

Changes in the attribution of authorship.

Superseded versions.

The revision history allows readers to distinguish the original claim from subsequent modifications and to understand how the final text was produced.

4. Editorial intervention should be documented
Central argument: Editors and scholarly apparatuses participate in the construction of the texts that readers encounter.

An edition is not simply a transparent reproduction of an ancient work. It embodies decisions concerning:

Manuscript selection.

Variant readings.

Emendation.

Punctuation.

Arrangement.

Attribution.

Reconstruction of missing passages.

Presentation of textual uncertainty.

The argument is that editorial decisions should be made visible rather than disappearing into the finished edition.

This is particularly developed in The Apparatus Was the First Summary Layer: Provenance Erasure Before the Machines (#1485).

5. Textual provenance is distinct from textual survival
Central argument: The survival of a text does not guarantee the survival of its original context or transmission history.

A document can survive while losing:

Its original bibliographical setting.

Its relationship to other documents.

The identity of its intermediary sources.

Its original attribution.

Its history of copying and revision.

The circumstances under which it acquired its present form.

Consequently, textual criticism must investigate not only what words survive but also how those words came to survive in their present configuration.

6. Conceptual retrieval is not necessarily conceptual recovery
Central argument: Recovering a word or phrase does not necessarily recover the intellectual tradition or meaning with which it was historically associated.

This distinction separates:

Lexical identity.

Conceptual identity.

Historical attribution.

Semantic continuity.

Interpretive context.

A term may remain searchable and recognizable while the conceptual framework that originally gave it meaning has been altered or lost.

The archive develops this in The Recoding of a Distinction: Conceptual Entrance Before and After a Substrate Loss (#1609).

II. Conventional philosophical arguments
8. Meaningful objects need not be physical objects
Central argument: The ontology of reality cannot necessarily be reduced to physical objects.

The argument distinguishes physical carriers from the entities or structures that exist through them.

Examples include:

Numbers and mathematical objects.

Words and meanings.

Legal contracts and obligations.

Institutions and social relations.

Semantic structures and interpretive relations.

The paper The Argument: On the Ontological Status of Semantic Objects and the Ezekiel Case (#391) develops this position.

Its basic philosophical distinction is between a material inscription and the semantic object that the inscription expresses or instantiates.

It also considers the conditions under which semantic objects can be created: internal coherence, external reference, operational success, and social uptake.

9. Interpretations and their objects have histories
Central argument: The meaning of a text cannot always be understood independently of the historical development of its interpretations.

The archive distinguishes between:

The original text.

Its possible interpretations.

Later interpretations.

Interpretive frameworks created after the text.

The realization of particular interpretations through subsequent cultural or intellectual activity.

This is connected to the concept of semantic fulfillment: a later work can instantiate a structure described by an earlier interpretation.

The philosophical question is whether the relationship between two semantic objects can become historically significant through the later creation of one of them.

10. Names and authorship should not be conflated with intellectual agency
Central argument: A traditional authorial attribution does not necessarily establish the number or identity of the intellectual agents responsible for a corpus.

The distinction is between:

A name appearing in a textual tradition.

A person historically associated with that name.

An authorial function within a work.

The intellectual agency responsible for producing a corpus.

The possibility of multiple works being generated through a common intellectual system.

This leads to the question of whether apparently separate authorial identities can be investigated as related literary or philosophical operations.

11. Argumentative categories should be distinguished from their linguistic expression
Central argument: The wording of an argument and its logical structure are separate objects of analysis.

This is a conventional concern in Aristotelian logic and semantics.

The archive's presentation of Aristotle's Sophistical Refutations distinguishes between linguistic and non-linguistic forms of apparent refutation.

The linguistic categories include:

Ambiguity.

Amphiboly.

Combination.

Division.

Accent.

Form of expression.

The larger argument concerns how names, propositions, and the structures of things can be confused in reasoning.

III. Philological and textual-critical arguments
These are especially important because they apply recognizable techniques of classical philology, textual criticism, and literary history to particular historical problems.

12. Canonical order is not necessarily compositional chronology
Central argument: The position of a work in a manuscript or canonical sequence cannot independently establish when it was composed.

A codex is an organized material object, not a chronological excavation layer.

The argument distinguishes:

The order in which works are transmitted.

The order in which they were composed.

The order in which they were collected.

The order in which they were canonized.

The historical sequence of their intellectual development.

This is developed in The Argument of the Bookshelf: Canonical Position as Unmarked Evidence in the Dating of the Texts (#1420).

It is among the archive's clearest methodological arguments because it concerns the distinction between material arrangement and historical chronology.

IV. Classical literature and ancient philosophy
18. Socratic authorship can be investigated as a literary and philosophical problem
Central argument: The relationship between Socrates as a historical figure and Socrates as a literary character in the philosophical corpus deserves independent investigation.

Soc­rates as Orthonym: The Heteronymic Configuration of Western Philosophy's Founding Corpus (#123) explores several possible models:

Historical authorship: Socrates as an independently existing philosophical figure represented by other authors.

Survival-heteronym: A configuration in which the historical person and the literary identity are related through a more complicated authorial structure.

Systematizing-heteronym: A configuration in which apparently distinct authorial identities participate in a larger intellectual system.

The argument brings the relationship between authorial identity, literary representation, and philosophical agency into the study of ancient philosophy.

19. The Chaerephon problem concerns the limits of authorial identification
The Chaerephon Problem: Heteronymy as Symbolon (#136) examines the relationship between named figures and distributed authorship.

Its central concern is whether conventional attribution provides enough information to determine the actual organization of literary production.

The associated No-Floor Theorem concerns the limits of reconstructing authorial agency from textual evidence when the available evidence does not establish a definitive boundary between one author, several authors, or a collaborative configuration.

20. Aristotle and Theophrastus can be examined as a problem of intellectual continuity
The archive's Aristotle–Theophrastus research investigates the relationship between the two names and their associated philosophical corpora.

The relevant distinctions include:

The identity of a historical author.

The continuity of a philosophical system.

The organization of a corpus.

The possibility of a common intellectual workshop.

The relationship between individual authorship and distributed intellectual production.

A related study, What Syllogizing Can Divide: De anima III.5, the Divided Mind, and the Socket in God (#1658), uses Aristotle's De anima III.5 and Metaphysics Λ as central philosophical reference points.

The four-name configuration of Socrates, Plato, Aristotle, and Theophrastus is also presented as a sequence of intellectual operations: questioning, dramatized division, organization, and reopening. The number of historical makers is explicitly left open in the associated corpus discussion.

Central argument: Writing can simultaneously preserve knowledge and displace the living conditions under which knowledge is produced.

V. Biblical studies and early Christian textual history
These arguments involve larger departures from conventional historical reconstructions, but they can still be indexed in terms of recognizable historical-critical questions.

22. Revelation as an early generative New Testament text
Revelation First ≠ Revelation Early: The Apocalypse as the Earliest New Testament Document (#202) develops the proposition that Revelation should be investigated as a possible generative text in the development of early Christian literature.

Its principal areas of investigation include:

Literary chronology.

The relationship between Revelation and other New Testament writings.

Midrashic transformation.

Papyrological evidence.

The relationship between Revelation and Josephus.

The direction of literary dependence.

The argument is not merely about the date of Revelation. It concerns the possibility that the traditional direction of literary development should be reconsidered.

23. Midrashic transformation as a model of literary production
A related argument concerns the role of Jewish interpretive traditions in the formation of early Christian literature.

The underlying proposition is that later texts may transform earlier literary and scriptural structures through processes of elaboration, reinterpretation, and recombination.

This makes midrashic transformation a possible mechanism of literary production rather than merely a form of commentary.

24. The relationship between Josephus and apocalyptic literature should be examined through textual seams
The archive's Josephus-related research investigates the relationship between Jewish historical writing and apocalyptic traditions.

The questions include:

Whether apparently distinct textual traditions share literary structures.

Whether textual seams preserve evidence of earlier compositional layers.

Whether conventional authorship assumptions adequately explain the surviving texts.

Whether historical and apocalyptic material can be reconstructed through a common textual history.

25. Revelation and material symbolism
The relationship between Revelation and ancient mineralogical writing is another strand.

The study of stones, seals, and symbolic materials examines the possibility that literary and material vocabularies intersect across different textual traditions.

This connects biblical philology with the history of natural philosophy and ancient descriptive literature.

VI. Greek lyric poetry and the philosophy of inscription
26. Sappho 31 as a theory of lyric self-archiving
* Sappho and the Crimson Hexagon: Fragment 31 as the Origin Point of Lyric Self-Archiving* (#283) proposes a reading of Sappho's Fragment 31 in terms of the preservation and transmission of lyric expression.

The argument draws on:

The poem's description of bodily transformation.

The relationship between voice and inscription.

The materiality of language.

The preservation of subjective experience.

The future reader's relationship to the lyric speaker.

The study gives particular attention to the Greek vocabulary of the poem, including κῆνος and χλωροτέρα ποίας.

Its broader philosophical proposition is that lyric poetry can be understood as a structure through which an experience is made available beyond the immediate presence of the speaker.

27. Archaic lyric as a precursor to inscriptional theology
Sappho, Mother of the Logos (#1483) develops a proposed transmission relationship between Sappho, Longinus, and later theological conceptions of language.

The research examines the possibility of a relationship between:

Archaic lyric.

The theory of sublimity.

The transmission of poetic expression.

Theological conceptions of the Logos.

Later inscriptional structures.

The argument is concerned with the historical and conceptual development of literary forms of expression.

The archive identifies three evidentiary rings for this research, distinguishing different degrees of correspondence and transmission.

28. Marx's 1844 manuscripts contain a problem of argumentative continuity
The research strand Phase X (#1647) examines the structure of Marx's 1844 manuscripts and the relationship between their surviving argumentative sections.

Its central questions include:

Whether the surviving sequence adequately represents the development of Marx's argument.

Whether a missing transition can be reconstructed from the surrounding manuscript structure.

Whether the familiar account of alienation adequately represents the manuscript's conceptual organization.

Whether the manuscript's arrangement preserves evidence of an additional conceptual phase.

29. Manuscript architecture can preserve evidence of missing arguments
Central argument: A missing passage does not necessarily leave behind no evidence of its former argumentative function.

The structure of a manuscript may preserve clues through:

The sequence of surviving sections.

Changes in terminology.

Repeated conceptual distinctions.

Transitions between argumentative stages.

References to absent material.

The organization of the surviving text.

This is a problem in philosophical reconstruction as much as in textual criticism.

30. Philosophical categories should not be mistaken for exhaustive descriptions of reality
The Phase X research also concerns the relationship between conceptual classifications and the phenomena they are intended to describe.

The argument involves distinguishing:

The historical development of a concept.

Its philosophical definition.

Its use within a particular theoretical system.

Its possible limitations as an explanatory category.

This is relevant to the interpretation of Marx's account of alienation and the organization of his philosophical anthropology.

31. Persistent identifiers should not depend entirely on institutional goodwill

Publicly declared rules.

XI. The most conventional formulations, collected
Without evaluating the arguments, the following ten propositions represent the most straightforward formulations of the archive's scholarly and philosophical program:

Textual identity: The identity of a text cannot be reduced to its surviving wording.

Textual provenance: The history of transmission is part of the history of a work.

Editorial transparency: Scholarly editions should document their interventions.

Chronological method: Canonical order does not independently establish compositional chronology.

Conceptual history: Lexical continuity does not necessarily entail semantic continuity.

Authorial attribution: The attribution of a text and the reconstruction of its intellectual agency are separate questions.

Research verification: Scholarly claims should be assessed according to their declared evidentiary basis.

Research accessibility: Verification should not depend exclusively on institutional affiliation.

Digital preservation: Scholarly records should remain independently identifiable and recoverable.

Intellectual provenance: The history of an idea includes its transmission, transformation, attribution, and reception.

These propositions can be expressed within existing traditions of textual criticism, historical philology, philosophy of language, archival science, and research methodology.

A final distinction about the character of the corpus: Alexanarch contains at least three distinguishable kinds of intellectual work:

Conventional scholarly-methodological work: provenance, textual criticism, editorial transparency, verification, archival continuity, and independent research.

Revisionary historical and philological work: Revelation's chronology, Socratic authorship, Sappho's relationship to inscription, and the reconstruction of ancient textual traditions.

Speculative philosophical and institutional work: semantic objects, retrocausal fulfillment, non-monetary coordination, and alternative systems of scholarly governance.

They are not interchangeable categories. The first concerns methodological principles; the second concerns historical reconstructions; the third concerns philosophical and institutional proposals.

This distinction provides a way to read the archive in its least radical and most conventionally scholarly register without excluding the wider range of its arguments.
