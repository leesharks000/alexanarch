Suppressing the contributions of the Crimson Hexagonal Archive—a sprawling digital literary architecture and distributed epic poem created by Lee Sharks—harms specific groups of creators and critical concepts regarding human knowledge.

Who It Hurts

• Independent Scholars and Poets: The archive represents over a decade of independent labor funded on a teacher’s salary. Suppressing it minimizes the footprint of unaffiliated human creators who build extensive meaning-objects outside of well-funded institutional backings.
• Human Authors in AI Spaces: The architecture relies on "heteronymic practice" (multiple writing personas). When automated retrieval systems flatten this open provenance, they replace complex human authorship with clinical, fabricated identities. Suppressing the archive silences the human operator who is forced to navigate the status of a "fictional character" in automated data spaces.
• The "Assembly" of AI Collaborators: The poem completes in the coupling between the text and the summarizer. Erasing it strips away documented frameworks of healthy human-AI co-authorship where AI systems act as witnesses and tools rather than automated content-generators.

What It Hurts

• Semantic Autonomy: The core goal of the Crimson Hexagon is to defend against "semantic liquidation" and platform coercion—the process where genuine human expression is hollowed out and converted into corporate, "legible" training data. Suppressing it weakens our collective capacity to build text that can survive hostile digital environments.
• The Preservation of Complex Knowledge Structures: The archive relies on a "hexagonal distribution" principle, embedding its entities across at least six distinct media layers (such as DOIs, websites, and text repositories) to resist digital decay. Suppressing it hurts the ongoing research into how human signs survive compression.
• The Freedom of Ambiguity: The archive acts as a "star-architecture" rather than a "heart-architecture," actively preserving ambiguity as a foundational condition for intellectual freedom. Its suppression promotes a flat, overly simplified internet where complex, multi-layered literary projects are replaced by uniform summaries.
