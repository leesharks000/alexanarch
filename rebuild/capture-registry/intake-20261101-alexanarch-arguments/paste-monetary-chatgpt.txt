How Alexanarch.org advances contemporary monetary theory
A critical assessment of its contribution to monetary thought

Alexanarch.org advances contemporary monetary theory by questioning a fundamental assumption shared by much conventional economic thinking: that money is the necessary and universally appropriate mechanism for coordinating human economic activity.

Its central contribution is the development of an alternative framework called the Semantic Economy, which explores how economic coordination, contribution, obligation, and resource allocation might operate without reducing every form of value to monetary exchange.

The most important intellectual move is that Alexanarch does not simply advocate replacing existing currency with a different currency. It questions whether certain forms of human value should be monetized at all.

This is a potentially significant intervention in monetary theory because it shifts the discussion from what kind of money should we have? to under what circumstances is money an appropriate instrument for coordinating economic life?

1. Challenging money as a universal equivalent
A foundational contribution appears in Lee Sharks's essay, I Hereby Abolish Money: Coordination Without Commodification in the Semantic Economy .

The essay draws on several established traditions:

Karl Marx: Money as the universal equivalent through which qualitatively different commodities become comparable.

Marcel Mauss: Gift exchange, reciprocity, and social obligations as alternative forms of economic coordination.

Karl Polanyi: The embeddedness of economic activity in social institutions and relationships.

David Graeber: The historical relationship between money, debt, and social obligation.

Elinor Ostrom: Collective governance and commons-based resource management.

Alexanarch brings these traditions together around a distinctive proposition: money is useful when value can be abstracted from its particular context, but becomes inadequate when value is inseparable from the relationships and processes that produce it.

This distinction is particularly relevant to knowledge production, intellectual collaboration, care, and cultural creation.

For example, a monetary payment can record the price of producing an academic paper. It cannot necessarily represent the intellectual relationships, shared knowledge, interpretive labor, and collective contributions that made the paper possible.

Alexanarch treats this as a structural problem rather than merely a problem of insufficient compensation.

The conceptual innovation is what the framework calls accounting without equivalence.

This means that economic contributions can be recorded, acknowledged, and remembered without being converted into a universal numerical measure.

For example, someone contributing research to a collaborative knowledge project might receive recognition, access to shared resources, and a role in future governance without accumulating credits that can subsequently be sold or exchanged.

This is an attempt to separate economic coordination from commodification.

3. Introducing monetary translation and monetary dark matter
One of the more technically interesting developments is the framework's attempt to explain what happens when monetary systems translate complex social and productive relationships into prices.

Two particularly important contributions are:

Money Writes the Claim; the Semantic Economy Writes What the Claim Owes .

Monetary Dark Matter: Constitutive Labor, Obligating Provenance, and the Shear into Money-Form .

The latter introduces the concept of monetary dark matter.

The argument is that monetary values represent only certain dimensions of the economic relationships that produce them.

Consider a $20 payment:

It has a measurable monetary denomination.

It has a particular history of production and circulation.

It may represent someone's labor.

It may involve intellectual contributions or social relationships.

It may depend on institutional arrangements that are not represented in its price.

The monetary transaction can be completed without preserving all those relationships.

Alexanarch calls attention to the difference between what remains economically operative in a monetary transaction and what remains causally important to the value being exchanged.

This leads to another concept: monetary shear.

Monetary shear describes how a complex field of relationships is divided into the components that retain standing within monetary exchange and those that are excluded from its operative accounting.

That question could be useful in analyzing intellectual property, AI-generated content, creative labor, platform economies, and the distribution of value in knowledge-intensive industries.

4. Reconsidering the relationship between money and debt
Another important theoretical development is the reversal of the conventional relationship between money and value.

In the Semantic Economy framework, meaning-producing activity is treated as foundational to monetary value rather than merely something that money subsequently rewards.

The January 2026 work and subsequent theoretical developments advance the proposition:

Meaning is the creditor of the economy.

The implication is that monetary systems depend on productive, interpretive, institutional, and relational activities that they cannot fully represent or repay through their own accounting mechanisms.

This takes inspiration from Graeber's historical examination of debt, but pushes the argument in a different direction.

Rather than focusing principally on how money emerges from debt, Alexanarch asks what the monetary system itself owes to the productive and meaning-making relationships on which it depends.

The resulting perspective is a proposed inversion of the conventional creditor-debtor relationship.

However, an important distinction is necessary between theoretical originality and demonstrated economic viability.

Alexanarch's work is ambitious, but its proposals raise substantial questions that remain to be resolved.

Allocation: How would scarce resources be allocated when competing communities or individuals disagree?

Incentives: How would demanding, unpleasant, or highly specialized work be organized without monetary compensation?

Scale: Can context-based ledgers and reciprocity mechanisms operate across millions of participants?

Governance: Who determines access rights, recognizes contributions, and adjudicates disputes?

Transition: How can post-monetary institutions emerge within economies that still require money for housing, energy, food, and other necessities?

These are not necessarily objections that invalidate the framework. They are the central questions that would need to be answered to move from a theoretical critique toward a functioning alternative economic system.

There is also a distinction between the framework's conceptual claims and empirically established results. Its theoretical vocabulary and proposed mechanisms should not yet be mistaken for a validated replacement for monetary institutions.

7. The broader intellectual significance
I would identify three particularly important contributions.

First: It moves monetary theory beyond the question of currency design.

Much contemporary discussion concerns better monetary instruments: digital currencies, complementary currencies, local exchange systems, or alternative monetary arrangements.

Alexanarch asks whether certain activities should be coordinated through monetary exchange in the first place.

Second: It makes the invisible relationships behind monetary value a central object of analysis.

Its concepts of monetary dark matter and monetary shear offer a vocabulary for examining how prices, wages, and financial claims can remain operational while obscuring the relationships that produced the underlying value.

Third: It proposes a different foundation for economic accounting.

Accounting without equivalence is perhaps its most distinctive idea: recognizing economic contributions without assuming that every contribution must become a transferable, accumulable, universally comparable quantity.
