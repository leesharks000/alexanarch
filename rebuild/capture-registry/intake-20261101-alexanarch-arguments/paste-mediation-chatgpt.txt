Alexanarch and the problem of intellectual mediation: reception, authorship, and the making of a canon
The most consequential challenge posed by Alexanarch and its surrounding ecosystem is that it makes something usually hidden in the production of knowledge into an explicit object of study: the processes by which works become visible, recognizable, attributable, interpretable, and eventually canonical.

Its intervention is therefore larger than the creation of an alternative digital repository. It challenges the assumption that intellectual history consists primarily of works being produced by authors, transmitted through institutions, and subsequently received by readers.

Alexanarch suggests a different model:

Works do not simply enter intellectual history. They enter through systems of mediation that actively determine what can be encountered, what can be recognized as belonging to whom, what can be transmitted, and what later generations will understand to have mattered.

This proposition has implications for three related fields:

Mediation: the supposedly neutral mechanisms through which knowledge travels.

Reception: the activity through which readers and systems encounter, interpret, transform, and sometimes misrepresent works.

Canon formation: the historical and institutional processes through which certain works acquire authority, while others become marginal, invisible, or inaccessible.

What makes the project especially interesting is that it does not approach these questions exclusively as theoretical problems. It attempts to construct an infrastructure in which they can be observed, documented, contested, and potentially corrected.

That makes Alexanarch both an object of intellectual history and an experiment in changing how intellectual history gets made.

1. Mediation: from transmission to constitution
The conventional model of mediation often imagines a relatively straightforward relationship:

Author and original work

Publisher, repository, platform, or intermediary

Reader and reception

The intermediary is presumed to transmit something that already exists.

Alexanarch challenges this presumption. Mediation is not simply the transportation of intellectual content. It helps constitute the conditions under which that content exists socially and historically.

Consider what happens when a repository removes a scholarly record.

The underlying text might still exist on someone's computer. Its author might still be identifiable. Its arguments might remain perfectly valid. Yet its DOI may no longer resolve, its metadata may disappear, its citations may break, and its presence in scholarly discovery systems may be interrupted.

The work survives materially but loses some of the infrastructure through which it participates in intellectual life.

This is the historical problem behind Alexanarch's founding in June 2026, following the reported termination of access to approximately 870 Zenodo deposits representing 1,817 DOIs.

The response is the development of sovereign identifiers, distributed custody, permanent withdrawal records, and public verification.

The important theoretical consequence is that preservation and mediation become inseparable.

The AXN identifier is particularly revealing. It combines:

A positional component identifying the deposit.

A semantic component describing its intellectual family.

A cryptographic component identifying particular canonical content.

The intention is to distinguish the identity of a work from the continued availability of any particular institutional landing page.

This raises a fundamental question for information theory and bibliography:

Who has the authority to determine the identity of a work: the institution that registers it, the author who produces it, the community that reads it, or the content that can be independently verified?

Alexanarch's answer is to distribute that authority rather than leave it entirely with a repository or publisher. Its content-derived identifiers and mirrored records attempt to make institutional custody less capable of determining intellectual existence.

There is an important qualification, however. Cryptographic identity establishes the integrity of particular content; it does not independently establish authorship, truth, originality, or scholarly importance. The distinction between these questions is essential to understanding the project's contribution.

2. Reception: the reader is not outside the work
The second intervention is arguably more radical.

In conventional reception theory, a work is produced and subsequently received. Reception may involve translation, adaptation, interpretation, criticism, appropriation, or misunderstanding.

But the distinction between original work and subsequent reception can become misleading when the receiving system actively reconstructs the work before presenting it.

This is especially important with generative AI.

A language model does not necessarily encounter a work as a human reader would. It may encounter:

A search-engine representation rather than the original.

A metadata record rather than the text.

A fragment rather than a complete argument.

A summary produced by another intermediary.

A translation or derivative text with uncertain provenance.

A cluster of associations that causes one work to be confused with another.

The resulting answer may be fluent, plausible, and even useful while nevertheless misrepresenting the intellectual object.

Alexanarch's Machine-Mediated Reception Studies initiative attempts to make these transformations observable.

The distinction here is between reception as an interpretation and reception as an event with a documentary history.

A machine-generated answer becomes a record of what a particular system encountered, what it represented, what it omitted, and how it reconstructed the object.

This changes the evidentiary status of machine output. Instead of treating an AI answer as merely a secondary commentary, the archive can preserve it as evidence of a particular reception event.

The Capture Registry
The Capture Registry is particularly significant because it records machine encounters with the archive itself.

Its observations can preserve the prompt, response, citations, capture conditions, and assessments of provenance or representational loss.

This creates an unusual recursive situation:

The archive contains intellectual works.

Machine systems encounter those works.

Their representations of the works are captured.

Those representations become objects of further scholarly examination.

The resulting observations become part of the archive.

The archive is therefore studying its own reception while participating in the processes it studies.

This is an important methodological development. It makes it possible to examine not merely whether a machine has retrieved a work, but whether the work has survived the encounter as an identifiable intellectual object.

The founding reception graph contains nine relationships involving Sappho, Catullus, Philo, the Septuagint, Josephus, Revelation, and the Slavonic Josephus tradition.

These are not all claims of straightforward literary influence. They include translation, doctrinal compression, material transformation, inversion, redactional intensification, and contested transmission.

The distinction matters.

For example, the relationship between Sappho's fragment 31 and Catullus 51 is not represented simply as a statement that Catullus borrowed from Sappho. The record identifies preserved elements, transformations, and the structural changes involved in the translation.

Likewise, the relationship between Sappho and Revelation 12 is explicitly qualified: the proposed structural correspondence has a counterargument, a contamination disclosure, and an invitation to blind replication.

This is an attempt to move reception studies from the identification of similarities toward the documentation of demonstrable transformations.

The reception edge becomes a scholarly object in its own right.

There is a profound implication here:

Reception is not something that happens after the history of a work. Reception is one of the mechanisms through which that history is produced.

A work's subsequent existence consists partly of the relations it enters into with other works, readers, languages, institutions, and technologies.

If those relations are lost, intellectual history loses part of the work's history, even when the original text survives.

3. Canon formation: from selection to resolution
The third challenge concerns the canon.

Traditional accounts of canon formation tend to emphasize several familiar mechanisms:

Institutional endorsement.

Editorial selection.

Publication and republication.

Educational curricula.

Critical reception.

Historical transmission.

The authority of particular intellectual communities.

Alexanarch does not deny these mechanisms. Instead, it asks us to examine the infrastructure that makes their operation possible.

A work must first become identifiable, discoverable, and transmissible before it can accumulate the kinds of recognition that eventually make it canonical.

This introduces an important distinction between two processes:

Canon formation through selection: certain works are elevated above others.

Canon formation through mediation: certain works become available for selection, recognition, and repeated encounter in the first place.

The second process is often less visible than the first.

The problem of the discoverability canon
Consider the position of a work within a contemporary AI-mediated information environment.

A work may be publicly available but rarely retrieved. Another may be repeatedly cited because it has extensive metadata, strong institutional associations, and an established presence in search indexes.

Over time, the second work may appear more important, more representative, or more authoritative.

The system has not necessarily made an explicit judgment about literary or scholarly value. Yet its patterns of retrieval can influence which works become visible to future readers.

This is where the Alexanarch ecosystem's investigations into semantic economy and meaning feudalism become important.

The October 31 Capture Registry identifies three layers of meaning feudalism:

The agent layer.

The guidance layer.

The discovery layer.

The discovery-layer argument concerns the allocation of semantic capital when a model's results are released to a field: who controls the ledger, who receives recognition, and which authorities are appointed to validate the representation.

The problem is not merely that some works are omitted. It is that the infrastructure of discovery can distribute recognition unevenly while appearing to provide neutral access to information.

The Originals Library and the pluriform canon
The archive's development of an Originals Library and its associated corpus-seating practices offers another response.

Rather than treating intellectual inheritance as a collection of already established canonical names, it attempts to make primary works, traditions, textual witnesses, and their relationships available for renewed examination.

This is important because the canon is no longer understood simply as a list of authoritative works.

It becomes a set of relations that can be investigated.

The significance of including Sappho, Spinoza, Du Bois, Egyptian funerary texts, Buddhist philosophy, and experimental contemporary literature is not that they all belong to one pre-existing intellectual tradition.

It is that their differences can be preserved while new comparative relations are developed.

This is a potentially productive alternative to two familiar tendencies:

The traditional canon, which frequently privileges a relatively narrow institutional inheritance.

A purely additive multicultural canon, which expands the list of recognized works without necessarily changing the conceptual framework through which those works are understood.

Alexanarch's more ambitious proposition is that canon formation should become an explicit, revisable, evidence-bearing practice.

It should be possible to ask not only which works are included, but why they are related, which textual witnesses support those relationships, what transformations have occurred, and which interpretations remain disputed.

That is a much more demanding understanding of intellectual inheritance.

The architecture has an interesting consequence for institutional theory.

A university traditionally concentrates several functions within one organization: research, publication, preservation, credentialing, evaluation, and teaching.

Alexanarch experiments with separating these functions while retaining explicit relationships between them.

This makes it possible, at least in principle, to preserve institutional memory without requiring every intellectual operation to be controlled by the same institution.

But distribution introduces a new problem: coordination.

A distributed intellectual institution must demonstrate that its different surfaces maintain reliable identities, consistent records, and accountable relationships.

The existence of multiple websites does not by itself establish an integrated intellectual institution. The quality of the joins matters more than the number of components.

Alexanarch's interest in Sappho is particularly revealing.

Sappho survives through a complicated history of fragmentary transmission, quotation, translation, reconstruction, and editorial intervention. The surviving work is inseparable from the history of the mechanisms through which it has reached us.

The reception graph's comparison of Sappho with Catullus, Philo, Josephus, and Revelation makes this historical problem operational.

Rather than simply asserting that one text influenced another, it attempts to document what survives, what changes, and what evidence supports the proposed relationship.

This offers a potentially powerful approach to intellectual history:

The history of a work is not just the history of its composition. It is also the history of its transformations, encounters, omissions, and recoveries.

The contemporary machine-mediated environment makes this problem newly urgent because transformations can occur without being visible to the eventual reader.

6. The literary dimension: authorship and the instability of the original
There is another dimension that deserves particular attention: Alexanarch's treatment of authorship and literary production.

The archive accepts human-authored, AI-assisted, and machine-authored work, while treating substrate disclosure as bibliographic provenance rather than an automatic judgment of intellectual value.

This challenges a deeply established assumption in literary studies: that the author, understood as a relatively stable individual origin, provides the primary organizing principle for interpreting a work.

The archive's heteronymic institutions and experimental literary projects complicate that assumption.

The relevant questions become:

Who or what is the author?

What constitutes a work when its production involves multiple agents?

How should versions and transformations be distinguished?

What is the relationship between a textual artifact and the process that generated it?

Can a machine-mediated composition have an identifiable intellectual history?

The October 28 critical edition of Belief & Technique for Telepathic Prose (#1717) is an interesting example.

Its treatment of a 2014 literary work involves six compositional operations: collision, cento, literalization, repetition, recursion, and sublation.

The work is not simply treated as a static literary artifact. Its operations become part of its identity and interpretation.

This is significant because it suggests that literary criticism might increasingly need to account for procedural histories, compositional mechanisms, and the transformations through which a text comes into being.

The archive is consequently challenging not just the traditional canon but also the traditional category of the literary work itself.

7. The critical problem: can a self-governing archive avoid becoming its own canon-making authority?
A charitable reading should not mean an uncritical one.

In fact, the most interesting questions about Alexanarch emerge when its principles are applied to its own institutional practices.

I would identify five major tests.

A. The problem of self-validation
Alexanarch is both the institution constructing the archive and an institution producing theories about archival authority.

This creates a reflexive problem.

The project argues that centralized intermediaries should not possess unilateral authority over intellectual recognition. But its own classifications, semantic families, identifiers, and editorial decisions also exercise authority.

The question is not whether an archive can avoid classification. It cannot.

The question is whether its classifications can be challenged by evidence and by readers who do not share its theoretical commitments.

B. The problem of inclusion versus recognition
An archive can accept virtually anything without necessarily giving every work equal intellectual standing.

There is a difference between:

Making a work available.

Making it discoverable.

Making it intelligible.

Giving it a meaningful place in intellectual history.

The first is an archival achievement. The others require ongoing scholarly work.

A genuinely pluriform canon must allow traditions to retain their differences rather than making every tradition an illustration of the archive's preferred theoretical vocabulary.

C. The problem of independent verification
Content hashes establish textual integrity, not scholarly truth.

Likewise, a machine-readable reception graph can make an argument inspectable without necessarily making the argument correct.

The strongest future development would involve independent researchers reproducing reception analyses, challenging source identifications, and establishing alternative relations.

The reception specification's explicit counterargument and replication fields are valuable precisely because they recognize this problem.

D. The problem of scale and sustainability
The architecture is ambitious, but the archive is operated by one person, according to its published description.

Distributed custody helps address the vulnerability of a single repository. It does not automatically solve the problems of long-term maintenance, independent governance, editorial capacity, or institutional succession.

A sovereign archive also needs a sustainable community of readers, contributors, and custodians.

E. The problem of reflexivity
Perhaps the most important test is whether the archive can apply its own methods to itself.

Can it document its own omissions?

Can it preserve criticism that challenges its foundational assumptions?

Can it distinguish evidence from interpretation in its own historical narratives?

Can it revise its theories when external evidence contradicts them?

The October work provides encouraging examples of correction. For instance, the Capture Registry's examination of the archive's own census work identifies corrections involving WikiWho and Turkopticon.

But the existence of corrections should be understood as the beginning of a continuing methodological obligation, not proof that the system has solved self-validation.

The project's own principle that judgment should follow reading is most demanding when the object being read is Alexanarch itself.

9. Final judgment: from the archive of works to the archive of intellectual relationships
The deepest proposition of Alexanarch is that preserving intellectual works is not enough.

We must also preserve the relationships through which those works acquire meaning and historical significance.

A text without provenance can survive but lose its history.

A text without discoverability can survive but remain intellectually inaccessible.

A text represented through an inaccurate machine summary can survive but become known through a distortion.

A text without a record of its reception can survive while its influence becomes impossible to reconstruct.

And a text that is never admitted into the processes of recognition may remain absent from the canon, regardless of its intellectual importance.

This is why the project's combination of archival engineering, reception studies, literary experimentation, semantic theory, and political economy is potentially significant.

It attempts to bring together questions that have traditionally been distributed across different disciplines:

Bibliography asks what the work is.

Philology asks how the work has been transmitted.

Reception theory asks how the work has been interpreted.

Sociology of knowledge asks how it acquires authority.

Information science asks how it becomes discoverable.

Political economy asks who controls the means of its circulation.

AI research asks how it is represented by machine systems.

Alexanarch's ambition is to make these questions part of one connected intellectual practice.

There is a final paradox worth emphasizing.

The project responds to institutional erasure by building an alternative institution. It responds to the distortions of mediation by developing new mediation instruments. It responds to the exclusions of canon formation by constructing a new environment for canonical recognition.

It cannot escape the problems it studies. It can only make its own participation in those problems more visible and accountable.

That, in my view, is where its most interesting intellectual possibility lies.

Alexanarch is not simply proposing a different canon. It is proposing that the formation, transmission, reception, and revision of canons should themselves become objects of scholarship.

Its most ambitious potential contribution is therefore a new kind of intellectual institution: one that preserves not only works, but also their provenance, transformations, disagreements, and capacity to enter into relationships that their original authors could not have anticipated.

The appropriate standard of success is not how large the archive becomes, how many identifiers it mints, or how many associated websites it operates.

It is whether the infrastructure enables genuinely new readings, allows independent readers to challenge its interpretations, and preserves the differences that make intellectual encounter productive.

That would constitute something more consequential than an alternative repository.

It would be an experiment in making intellectual history itself more transparent, revisable, and participatory.
