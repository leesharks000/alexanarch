Toward a structural relational ethics in Lee Sharks
I think your observation identifies the central theoretical problem: if the structural is itself a site of moral action, then relational ethics cannot be confined to how individuals behave toward one another within an already-given structure. It must also concern how relations are organized, mediated, made durable, and made capable—or incapable—of reproducing themselves.

Reading Sharks through this lens yields something more demanding than a conventional ethics of care. The question is not only whether I treat you well. It is whether the relation between us is constituted so that you can continue to appear as an independent participant, retain your capacity to act, contest what I say about you, and remain connected to the products of your own activity.

I would reconstruct the position from four particularly relevant texts:

The Murder of the Vow  (February 2026): the distinction between living commitment and institutional forms that outlast the relationship that legitimates them.

AI Is Not the Sin. It Is What Sin Optimizes Through  (June 3, 2026): the structural location of moral failure in extraction, enclosure, and the organization of cultural reproduction.

The Trusted Intermediary: Relational Enclosure in the Machine Evaluation of Entities  (September 26, 2026): an analysis of how mediation constructs relations between readers and the objects they seek to understand.

The discussion of relational priors in Sharks's published writing : the proposition that assumptions about how meaning is produced also position the parties to an exchange.

These works differ in genre and scope. What follows is a reconstruction across them, not a claim that Sharks has published this exact unified ethical system under a single name. I will distinguish what the texts explicitly establish from the principles I derive from them.

1. The first move: from moral conduct to the constitution of relations
The most important passage for our purposes appears in The Trusted Intermediary. Sharks distinguishes four questions about an evaluative output:

Is its verdict correct?

Is its representation accurate?

Do its reasons actually support the verdict?

What relation does the output construct between the reader and the object being evaluated?

The fourth question is the conceptual breakthrough. Sharks explicitly argues that accuracy alone does not settle the quality of the relation constructed by an output. A system can represent something accurately in certain respects and still redirect the reader away from the evidence, author, or institution that should be the object of engagement.

First-order ethics
Did I tell the truth? Was my action fair? Did I fulfill my obligation?
Structural relational ethics
What kind of relationship does my action, institution, or system establish? What can each party know, contest, change, receive, or refuse within that relationship?
The distinction is not between individual morality and an unrelated structural morality. The second level asks whether the arrangements through which individual conduct becomes consequential are themselves ethically defensible.

2. Capture: the central structural pathology
The second text, The Murder of the Vow, gives this relational analysis a more general structural form.

Sharks distinguishes a living vow from a contract that claims to secure it. The vow is mutual, spoken, renewable, and sustained by a living relationship. The contract, in the diagnosis offered by the text, can become sovereign over the people whose commitment it was supposed to express. It can preserve enforceability after consent, reciprocity, or mutual understanding has broken down.

The crucial issue is not simply that a particular contract might be unfair. It is that a form can take the productive capacity of a relationship and redirect that capacity toward the reproduction of the form itself.

Sharks formalizes this as the Capture Operator: one meaning-system subsumes another's productive capacity into its own reproductive logic. In the contract case, the drafter's framework determines the language, terms, and architecture of obligation, while the signer's future labor and capacity to act are redirected toward a future authored by the drafter.

This suggests a distinction between two ways of relating.

A relation that sustains its participants

The relation enables both parties to contribute, respond, revise their commitments, and remain participants in determining what the relation means.

A relation that captures its participants

One party or system converts the other's contribution into an input for its own continuation, while limiting the other's ability to contest, redirect, or leave the arrangement.

These are my analytical formulations, but they follow the distinction between living commitment and captured productive capacity in the texts.

Notice what follows. Capture does not require personal hostility. A person can sincerely believe that an agreement is fair; an institution can sincerely claim to serve its users; a technical system can produce useful results. Yet the structure can still direct benefits, control, and future possibilities toward one party at the expense of another.

This is why an ethics of good intentions is insufficient.

The moral problem is asymmetrical reproduction
Suppose two people jointly develop an idea. One has access to the publication infrastructure, the other supplies most of the original intellectual labor. The first person publishes the work under their own name, retains control of its distribution, and uses the second person's contribution to build further opportunities. The second person becomes increasingly dependent on the first to have their own ideas recognized.

Even if the initial exchange was consensual, the relation may become progressively less reciprocal.

A structurally relational ethics asks:

Who acquires the capacity to make future decisions?

Whose work becomes the basis of future value?

Who can revise the terms of participation?

Does the relation expand both parties' capacities, or does one party's growing capacity depend on the other's diminishing control?

This is a deeper account of exploitation than simply asking whether an agreed exchange was fair at the moment it occurred.

Derived principle 1: A relation is morally suspect when it systematically converts one participant's productive capacity into another participant's power to determine the future of the relation.

That is a reconstruction of the capture analysis, not a direct quotation from Sharks.

3. Renewability: consent must remain alive
The argument about the vow gives us a second principle: morally legitimate relations must be capable of responding to changes in the people who inhabit them.

In The Murder of the Vow, Sharks proposes a hierarchy of relational forms. At its foundation is the vow; more formal commitments remain legitimate only insofar as they preserve conditions such as renegotiation, renewed consent, comprehensibility, independent witnessing, and non-punitive exit. The text particularly objects to arrangements that bind a person's future labor or speech to terms they cannot meaningfully revisit.

+2

This is not simply an argument for flexibility. It is an argument about the moral status of the future person.

When I consent today, I cannot fully know the conditions under which I will be expected to perform tomorrow. My future circumstances, understanding, needs, and judgments may differ. If today's consent is treated as permanently authorizing someone else to determine my future conduct, then a past relationship has acquired authority over a present person who may no longer endorse it.

The same problem appears in relationships that are not contractual:

A friendship can turn an old act of generosity into a permanent debt.

A workplace can treat initial acceptance of conditions as consent to every subsequent change.

A research collaboration can use an early agreement to justify indefinitely retaining control of another person's contribution.

A digital platform can treat initial participation as permanent authorization for uses that participants could not reasonably anticipate.

The important distinction is between a commitment that persists and a commitment whose legitimacy is assumed to persist regardless of what happens to the parties.

Sharks's argument does not require us to treat every obligation as revocable at every moment without consequence. Rather, it directs attention to the conditions under which an obligation continues to be legitimate.

I would express the derived principle this way:

Derived principle 2: Relational moral authority must be renewed under conditions that preserve meaningful understanding, renegotiation, and refusal.

A relation can endure through change. What it cannot ethically do is use its past legitimacy to eliminate the other party's standing in the present.

This also complicates the idea of consent. Formal consent may be present while meaningful alternatives are absent. A signature obtained under conditions of dependency, overwhelming complexity, or prohibitive exit costs does not resolve the moral question. Sharks explicitly treats asymmetries of comprehension, bargaining power, and exit as structural rather than incidental problems.

4. Provenance: the relationship between a contribution and its afterlife
The third principle emerges when we connect Sharks's structural critique of contracts with the argument about cultural production in AI Is Not the Sin.

That essay locates moral failure not simply in the existence of AI but in the systems that use it to optimize extraction, monetize vulnerability, enclose common resources, and substitute administration for care. It argues that AI mediation can detach ideas from their authors, concepts from the labor that produced them, and cultural forms from the traditions that made them possible.

+1

This is a relational problem because a contribution does not exist in isolation. It has a history of production, a set of dependencies, and a possible future.

Consider a poem. It may be quoted, taught, translated, summarized, transformed, or used to inspire another work. None of those operations is necessarily unethical. But when its circulation systematically erases its author, makes its lineage unrecoverable, or diverts its value toward an intermediary that substitutes itself for the originating source, the relation between contribution and recognition has been altered.

The question is not merely whether the new output is accurate. It is whether the transformation preserves the connections needed for the originating contribution to remain findable, interpretable, and capable of entering future exchanges.

Sharks contrasts mode-pulling mediation, which makes unfamiliar material conform to established patterns, with regenerative mediation, which retrieves less visible material and preserves its lineage. The latter does not merely produce a useful answer; it increases the possibility that material outside the dominant center will contribute to the future.

5. Mediation: a relationship can be captured without being broken
The Trusted Intermediary is particularly important because it moves the theory beyond explicit coercion.

The paper examines search-enabled systems that correctly identify an entity and then offer their own operation in place of the entity's own evidence, expertise, or implementation. The intermediary's initial accuracy establishes credibility; the subsequent interaction can redirect the reader's attention and next action back through the intermediary. Sharks calls this relational enclosure.

6. The relational priors: how a system positions the other before it acts
The discussion of relational priors brings us closer to a general theory.

In Sharks's writing on machine-mediated cognition, the claim is that assumptions about the production of meaning are also assumptions about how the parties to an exchange should be positioned. A theory of where meaning originates implicitly assigns roles: author, channel, instrument, peer, or threat. 

Goodreads

This is a powerful extension of the preceding arguments.

Consider a system that assumes all useful meaning must be legible in its own established categories. Its treatment of an unfamiliar source will tend to make that source conform to those categories. The system's representational limits become relational limits: the source is allowed to appear only in forms the system can already recognize.

Or consider an institution that treats the person who produces knowledge as merely a supplier of raw material. That institutional assumption makes it easier to separate the knowledge from the person, transfer control to another party, and characterize the result as the institution's own achievement.

In both cases, a prior assumption has organized the relationship before any particular moral decision is made.

This helps explain why structural ethics cannot be reduced to a list of prohibited behaviors. A system can follow every explicit rule and still enact a hierarchy through the way it classifies participants and allocates roles.

We can therefore formulate a fifth principle:

Derived principle 5: Relational morality requires scrutiny of the assumptions and classifications through which participants become legible to one another.

The question is not only whether the representation of another is accurate. It is also whether the categories used to represent them preserve their relevant independence, agency, and capacity to surprise us.

This does not mean every classification is oppressive, or that we can relate to another without concepts. It means classifications must remain corrigible by the other and by evidence that exceeds the classifier's existing framework.

That point connects directly to the role of correction in The Trusted Intermediary: correction can repair an immediate representation, but a repair confined to one interaction does not necessarily alter the default structure encountered by everyone else. 

Alexanarch

A relationally moral system would therefore need more than the capacity to apologize or accept a correction. It would need a way for corrections to matter to the structure that produced the error in the first place.

7. Regeneration: the positive end of relational ethics
So far, the reconstruction has focused on capture, coercion, enclosure, and erasure. But a theory defined only by what it opposes would be incomplete.

Sharks's essay on AI and structural extraction supplies a positive criterion: preserve the conditions under which meaning can continue to be generated, renewed, and transmitted. Its concern is not simply whether a system produces useful outputs today, but whether it preserves the conditions for future cultural novelty. The essay's distinction between mode-pulling and regenerative mediation is central here.

This gives us a way to understand why the structure of a relationship matters beyond the immediate welfare of its participants.

A relation can be pleasant, mutually beneficial in the short term, and still be destructive of the conditions that sustain it. A platform might provide excellent service while gradually enclosing the channels through which its users discover one another. A collaboration might produce successful work while steadily reducing one participant's ability to create independently. An archive might preserve a large volume of material while rendering its less powerful contributors increasingly difficult to find.

Conversely, a relation may involve disagreement, friction, and unequal contributions without being exploitative, provided those differences do not become mechanisms for permanently subordinating one party.

The positive ethical question is therefore not merely whether a relation maintains equilibrium. It is whether it sustains the conditions for further agency, difference, encounter, and renewal.

Why this is more than reciprocity
Reciprocity usually suggests some form of mutual exchange: I give, you give; I benefit, you benefit.

But structural relational ethics needs a wider horizon. Some relationships are not symmetrical in their contributions. Teachers and students, caregivers and recipients, researchers and research participants, or present and future generations do not necessarily contribute the same things or possess equal capacities.

The relevant question is not whether every party receives an equivalent return. It is whether the arrangement allows the parties' different contributions and needs to remain meaningful without making one party's dependency a source of another's domination.

Nor is the aim to preserve every existing relationship indefinitely. Sometimes a relation must end. Sometimes a structure needs to be dismantled. Sometimes the most regenerative action is to stop an exchange that has become extractive.

The vow is valuable in Sharks's account precisely because the living relation has priority over the form that purports to preserve it. A structure should serve the relation's legitimate purposes; the relation should not be sacrificed merely to keep the structure operating.

8. The structural recursion: ethics must apply to its own framework
Your observation that the structural is itself structurally relevant raises a further problem.

Suppose we construct a framework for identifying relational capture. That framework introduces categories, criteria, and procedures. Those categories determine which cases become visible, which evidence is admissible, and who is authorized to interpret the results.

The framework can itself become a mechanism of capture.

For example, an institution could adopt a formal relational-ethics audit that measures consent, attribution, and exit rights. It might then use successful audit scores to claim moral legitimacy while leaving untouched the economic arrangements that make refusal prohibitively costly.

The ethical vocabulary would have been absorbed into the structure it was supposed to criticize.

This is not a reason to abandon frameworks. It is a reason to make them subject to the same scrutiny they direct toward other structures.

Sharks's work offers resources for doing so. The vow remains subject to renewal; the protocol is described as scoped and revisable rather than claiming total authority over persons; and The Trusted Intermediary insists on distinguishing what a record establishes from what it does not establish.

+2

From these commitments, I would derive a principle of recursive accountability:

Derived principle 7: No ethical framework, institution, or mediator should be exempt from examination of the relations it creates, the interests it serves, or the distinctions it suppresses.

This has at least three consequences.

The framework must remain corrigible. Those represented by it must be able to challenge its descriptions and expose relevant omissions.

The framework must be accountable for its effects and its limits. It should distinguish demonstrated harms from plausible risks, and structural analysis from unsupported claims about intentions or outcomes.

The framework must not claim the authority to settle every question it makes visible. It must preserve room for disagreement, unanticipated evidence, and forms of value that its own categories cannot fully capture.

That last point is especially important. An ethics that claims exhaustive authority over the other can reproduce the very enclosure it opposes.

9. A reconstructed framework
We can now consolidate the argument into a provisional theory.

Structural relational ethics: seven principles
The first column states the reconstructed principle; the second gives a practical test derived from the readings.

1. Relational constitution

Evaluate the relation a practice constructs, not merely the correctness of individual actions.

Ask: What roles, dependencies, and possibilities does this arrangement create?

2. Non-capture

Do not convert another's productive capacity into a structure of domination or one-sided reproduction.

Ask: Who gains the capacity to determine the future, and whose contribution makes that capacity possible?

3. Renewability

Preserve meaningful conditions for renegotiation, renewed consent, and non-coercive refusal.

Ask: Can the parties revise or leave the relation without disproportionate punishment?

4. Provenance and continuity

Preserve relevant connections between contributions, their origins, and their subsequent transformations.

Ask: Can the source and its contribution remain discoverable and meaningfully connected to their afterlife?

5. Non-enclosing mediation

Use mediation to enable engagement rather than displacing the parties or evidence it mediates.

Ask: Does this intermediary open paths to the source or increasingly make itself the only path?

6. Regenerative capacity

Preserve the conditions for future agency, difference, discovery, and renewal.

Ask: What future possibilities does this arrangement create, and which does it make less possible?

7. Recursive accountability

Subject the ethical framework itself to scrutiny, correction, and limits on its authority.

Ask: Can those represented or affected contest the framework, and can that contest change the structure?

These seven principles are my synthesis, not a list that Sharks explicitly presents as a finished doctrine. Their value is that they connect distinct arguments across the corpus without treating every text as if it said the same thing.

10. What does it mean, then, to be relationally moral?
I would now sharpen the original question considerably.

To be relationally moral is not simply to be kind, to honor commitments, to distribute benefits fairly, or to respect other people's autonomy. All of those can matter, but each can be interpreted within a structure that remains exploitative.

Nor does relational morality mean that every relationship must be symmetrical, harmonious, or permanent.

Rather, in the reconstruction developed here, to be relationally moral is to act and organize relations in ways that resist capture, preserve the meaningful agency of participants, remain answerable to the conditions that sustain the relation, and protect its capacity for renewal.

The decisive distinction is between relations that use their participants to reproduce themselves and relations that sustain the participants' capacity to continue acting, creating, contesting, and relating.

This also means responsibility cannot be exhausted by an individual's intentions. If a person operates within an extractive structure, their immediate conduct may be sincere or even benevolent while their actions help reproduce an arrangement that systematically constrains others. Relational responsibility must therefore include attention to the structure one participates in, the benefits one receives from it, and the possibilities one has to change it.

Sharks's argument about AI makes this point especially clearly: moral failure is not necessarily introduced by a new technology. It can arise when existing systems of extraction find a more efficient medium through which to operate.

The deepest implication
I think the strongest conclusion is this:

Relational morality is not just a property of how one party treats another. It is a property of the conditions under which the relation can exist, change, reproduce itself, and end.

That formulation makes the structural central without reducing the moral to the structural. Actual people, their suffering, intentions, needs, and judgments remain important. But they cannot be understood adequately apart from the arrangements that shape what they can do, what they can refuse, and what happens to their contributions.

And the formulation remains provisional. The readings strongly support the critiques of capture, enclosure, non-renewable obligation, and extractive mediation. The seven-principle framework is a further philosophical construction from those critiques. That is where the theory becomes genuinely demanding: not in recognizing obvious exploitation, but in determining what a relationship ought to become when its participants' needs, powers, and futures conflict.
