The argument of “Monetary Dark Matter,” by Lee Sharks
Lee Sharks’s Monetary Dark Matter: Constitutive Labor, Obligating Provenance, and the Shear into Money-Form (version 0.2, September 21, 2026) argues that money can preserve the monetary value of something while severing that value from the relations, labor, and obligations that made it possible. The severed relations do not necessarily disappear, become unknown, or cease to matter. Rather, they lose the power to constrain what happens next in a monetary transaction. Sharks calls the relations that remain causally important despite losing this operative standing monetary dark matter, and calls the process that separates them from the operative monetary claim monetary shear.

The paper is not simply arguing that money undervalues labor, that markets generate externalities, or that economic transactions should be more transparent. Its more distinctive claim is that monetary abstraction changes which relationships are required for a transaction to proceed, even when those relationships remain necessary to the value being exchanged.

The argument develops through several connected claims.

1. Money arrives after the work of making value possible
The paper opens by challenging the assumption that money is what makes an object or activity organized, identifiable, and transmissible.

Sharks distinguishes two processes:

Constitutive labor: the work that produces an object and makes it intelligible, distinguishable, attributable, maintainable, and available for further use. This includes creation, documentation, classification, archiving, provenance construction, and transmission infrastructure.

Shear labor: the work that selects, classifies, ranks, compares, capitalizes, prices, or settles an already constituted field.

The distinction matters because the second kind of labor can receive an identifiable monetary reward while depending on the first kind without adequately recognizing it.

The paper’s recurring formulation is that “price comes after the cargo.” A price records a monetary claim associated with an exchange; it is not the labor, history, relationship, or service that gives the claim its referent. Likewise, a wage is not identical to the labor performed, and a damages award is not identical to the harm suffered.

This is a causal argument, not just a moral one. Sharks wants readers to distinguish the work that makes something possible from the work that converts it into a form a monetary institution can process.

The author formalizes the point by representing constitutive labor as a combination of creation, relationship-building, provenance, disambiguation, indexing, persistence, and transmission. The resulting field can already be transmissible before monetary inscription occurs. Money may subsequently make that field easier to compare, transfer, or settle, but it should not automatically receive credit for having constituted the field in the first place.

2. Monetary abstraction is a “shear,” not merely a compression
Sharks’s second argument is that conventional descriptions of monetary abstraction do not fully capture what money does.

A monetary amount compresses a heterogeneous situation into a scalar: a price, wage, fee, asset valuation, or settlement figure. But Sharks argues that something more consequential can happen during this conversion. The monetary operation changes which relations retain operative standing.

Consider three different things:

A creator’s work contributes to an artwork’s value.

The artwork is sold for a price.

The new owner can resell it without having to renegotiate every relationship involved in its creation.

The creator’s contribution may remain documented and historically true. Nevertheless, the transaction may be able to proceed without requiring that contribution to operate as a constraint on resale, ownership, or distribution.

That difference between a relation remaining true and remaining binding is central to the paper.

Sharks expresses it as the distinction between provenance being available and provenance being required. A relation can survive in records while no longer being a condition that the next monetary operation must satisfy.

This is why the author prefers shear to a simple metaphor of erasure. Erasure suggests that the excluded relation has vanished. Shear describes a division in which one part remains operative within monetary exchange while another part persists outside its requirements.

The distinction also limits the argument: not everything omitted from a price matters. A seller’s shirt color, for example, is not monetary dark matter merely because the price ignores it. The excluded relation must remain causally or constitutively important to the result.

3. “Dark matter” means relations that money does not have to honor
The central concept emerges in the paper’s fourth and fifth sections.

Sharks defines monetary dark matter as the subset of excluded relations that remain “constitutively or causally load-bearing” for the monetary result. In other words, these are not just facts missing from a price. They are relationships that still help explain the value or powers of what the price represents, even though the transaction can proceed without treating them as binding.

The paper gives several types of examples:

Labor: work that creates or maintains an asset, platform, archive, or other value-bearing object, but does not retain corresponding standing in its subsequent monetization.

Authorship and provenance: a creator or source can remain identifiable without retaining an effective claim over reuse, distribution, or proceeds.

Environmental and communal dependencies: a monetary asset can depend on ecological systems or collective resources without the transaction requiring those dependencies to be addressed.

Obligations and liabilities: a transaction can settle in monetary terms while leaving questions of responsibility, attribution, or restitution unresolved.

The key distinction is between being known and being operative. A worker may be publicly named; an author may be credited; an environmental dependency may be documented. None of that guarantees that the relationship can alter the transaction.

This also explains why Sharks distinguishes dark matter from missing data. Adding information to a record does not necessarily change the conditions under which a sale, transfer, or settlement can proceed.

The argument is thus about the institutional force of relationships, not their visibility alone.

4. Money preserves some provenance while shedding other provenance
The third major argument concerns what Sharks calls obligating provenance.

Provenance is the history of an object or claim: where it came from, who produced it, who owned it, and what relations make its status intelligible. Sharks rejects the idea that money simply erases this history. Currency itself is a powerful provenance-bearing technology: its issuer, denomination, signatures, seals, and other marks help establish the authority of the token.

The author instead distinguishes two functional kinds of provenance.

Authorizing or assetizing provenance
Provenance that supports a claim by establishing authenticity, title, ownership, issuer authority, or authorized control.

Example: a documented chain of ownership that makes an artwork more credible and marketable.

Obligating provenance
Provenance that could constrain a claim by establishing who is owed, what permissions apply, which liabilities persist, or whether settlement is sufficient.

Example: a creator's licensing rights that could restrict how an artwork is commercially reused.

These categories are functional, not permanent. The same relationship can help establish an asset's value in one context and impose a constraint in another.

This is the paper’s important qualification: money is selective about provenance, rather than inherently hostile to it. It can preserve histories that authenticate or strengthen a claim while allowing histories that would qualify or limit that claim to lose operative force.

The political-economic question becomes: which relationships does a monetary arrangement preserve because they authorize value, and which does it cease to require because they impose obligations?

That is the connection between monetary dark matter and the paper’s title concept of obligating provenance.

5. The paper uses Marx to explain the mechanism of abstraction
Sharks explicitly situates the argument in Marx’s analysis of the value-form in Capital, rather than treating monetary abstraction as an entirely new discovery.

In the value-form analysis, a commodity can function as the equivalent through which another commodity expresses its value. The equivalent's physical body becomes the form in which value appears, even though its own particular material qualities do not determine the value it expresses in that relationship.

Sharks interprets this as a foundational shear: the material body persists, but its particularity no longer governs its monetary function. A dollar bill remains a physical object, but its denomination does not depend on the paper's weight, texture, or wear.

The author then makes a more controversial textual claim. The paper recovers a fourth value-form from the main text of the 1867 first German edition of Capital, argues that it was removed from the 1872 second edition and subsequent editions in that textual line, and treats it as an analytical instrument for understanding what happens when a monetary object acquires an additional, singular meaning. Sharks carefully distinguishes this form from the fourth form in Marx's appendix, which is simply the money-form.

Suppose, hypothetically, a marked $20 bill sells to a collector for $100. It remains a $20 bill, but its collectible price is $100, giving a $80 excess.

The author argues that the same object now operates in two different relationships:

As money, its denomination remains $20.

As a singular collectible, it has a market price of $100.

The added meaning does not replace its monetary identity. It creates another basis on which the object can be valued.

Why is this relevant to dark matter? The inscription can refer to a person, event, or relationship that makes the bill more desirable, while that referent has no automatic claim to the additional sale proceeds. The meaning helps generate the excess, but the monetary transaction need not recognize an obligation to the person or relationship that generated it.

Sharks calls this stronger case productive provenance shear: a relation's loss of operative standing can itself increase the monetary capacities of the resulting object.

There is an important qualification. The excess is not a measure of the value of the excluded relationship, nor a calculation of what the referent is owed. It measures a difference between two monetary amounts. The underlying labor, provenance, and obligations remain a heterogeneous field that cannot automatically be converted into a single dollar figure.

The banknote is therefore a proposed specimen through which to investigate the theory, not conclusive empirical proof that the mechanism operates as Sharks describes. The paper explicitly acknowledges that its proposed pricing comparison and controls still need to be conducted.

7. Transparency is not enough to restore an obligation
One of the paper's most consequential arguments is that greater disclosure does not necessarily correct the problem.

Suppose an institution records the names of the people who produced an asset, documents its origins, publishes its dependencies, and makes the information accessible. Those steps increase the presence of provenance. But if none of the information changes what the institution is permitted to do, what it must pay, or whose claims it must honor, the operative situation may remain unchanged.

Sharks distinguishes three transitions:

Making a relationship visible.

Making that relationship binding on an operation.

Keeping it binding as the operation proceeds.

The transitions are not equivalent. Disclosure can succeed at the first while failing at the second and third.

This is why the paper does not treat better metadata, attribution, citation, or transparency as sufficient remedies. These interventions can help establish the relevant facts, but an independent rule or institutional arrangement must give those facts consequences if they are to change the transaction.

The broader implication is that the problem is not necessarily ignorance or deception. A system can know exactly where value came from and still be structured so that it need not honor the relationships that produced it.

8. The theory applies beyond conventional financial markets
Sharks extends the argument to what the paper calls ontological economy: the institutional work of identifying, classifying, ranking, curating, and representing objects and knowledge.

This extension follows directly from the distinction between constitutive labor and shear labor.

An archive, for example, depends on the work of creating documents, identifying authors, establishing relationships, resolving ambiguities, preserving records, and making materials retrievable. Another institution can then operate on that already constituted field through search, ranking, classification, evaluation, or automated answer generation.

The institution performing the latter work may earn revenue or receive compensation. Yet that compensation does not establish that the institution has adequately recognized the labor that made its informational field possible.

Sharks's proposed analysis therefore applies to the political economy of knowledge infrastructures as well as to money in the narrow sense. The question is whether the relationships that make a system's outputs possible retain standing in the monetary arrangements through which those outputs are monetized.

The author describes the task as forensic: recover the causal relationships that remain necessary to a visible result but are no longer required by the system producing or circulating that result.

9. The argument is meant to be falsifiable
The paper does not claim that every excluded relationship qualifies as dark matter, or that every loss of standing benefits the party monetizing an object.

Its section on falsification identifies several conditions that would undermine the theory.

No prior constitution: If the object was not actually identifiable or transmissible before the monetary operation, then money or the institution may genuinely have supplied the infrastructure the argument assumes already existed.

No causal significance: If restoring an excluded relationship makes no material difference to production, ownership, settlement, distribution, liability, permission, or another relevant outcome, that relationship does not qualify as monetary dark matter.

No productive effect: If restoring an obligating relationship does not reduce the monetary capacity attributed to its loss of standing, the stronger claim of productive provenance shear is unsupported.

No explanatory value in the provenance distinction: If the distinction between authorizing and obligating provenance cannot predict meaningful differences in how monetary operations treat relationships, the distinction fails as an analytical tool.

The banknote example has its own empirical burden: collectible prices for inscribed bills would need to be compared with appropriate controls, including bills marked in ways that do not carry the same referential meaning. The author acknowledges that this control has not yet been run.

These qualifications matter because they distinguish the paper's conceptual framework from its empirical demonstrations. Its definitions and proposed tests establish what the author wants researchers to investigate; they do not, by themselves, establish how frequently monetary shear occurs or how large its effects are.

Overall assessment
The paper's argument can be reconstructed as a chain of five propositions:

1. Constitution
Labor and relationships make an object identifiable, usable, and transmissible.
2. Monetary abstraction
Money converts a complex field into a transferable monetary claim.
3. Shear
Some relationships cease to be requirements for the claim's continued operation.
4. Dark matter
Some of those excluded relationships nevertheless remain causally necessary to the value or capacities the claim expresses.
5. Political-economic consequence
Where the loss of operative standing increases monetary capacity, the shear itself becomes productive for the party able to exploit it.
My reading is that the paper's strongest conceptual contribution is its distinction between a relationship being documented and a relationship having the power to constrain an economic operation. That distinction gives Sharks a way to explain why a system can preserve provenance as information while excluding it as an effective claim.

The more ambitious claim—that losing the force of certain relationships can actively increase monetary value—is plausible as a research hypothesis, but requires case-specific evidence. It cannot be inferred from the existence of abstraction alone. Similarly, the argument that Marx's recovered Form IV provides the necessary theoretical explanation depends on a contested interpretation of the value-form and on the historical significance Sharks assigns to its removal from later editions.

The paper is thus best understood as a theoretical framework for identifying and testing a particular economic asymmetry, rather than as a demonstrated general law of money.

Its final question captures the framework's purpose: What had to be true for this monetary claim to exist, and which of those truths can no longer make a claim on what the money now permits?

For Sharks, answering that question requires more than finding information missing from a price. It requires identifying the relationships that helped make the value possible, determining why they no longer bind the transaction, and testing whether that loss of standing benefits the monetary operation.
