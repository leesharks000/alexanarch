The argument of AI Mediation of Economic Decisions (EA-MEDIATED-SHARE-01 v0.3)
Lee Sharks’s paper, issued on September 20, 2026, argues that AI-mediated economic value is potentially measurable and worth valuing, but current market statistics do not establish either how accurately AI systems identify entities or how much that accuracy changes commercial outcomes. The paper’s central methodological move is to separate what has been measured, what is estimated, what is assumed, and what remains unknown.

The text is not simply a forecast that AI commerce will grow. It is also an argument about how forecasts should be constructed, how AI visibility should be measured, and who bears the burden of proving that improved representation of a business produces economic value.

The analysis below follows the paper’s own claims, qualifications, and proposed tests, rather than treating its conclusions as independently verified facts.

1. The paper separates three kinds of AI-mediated commerce
In section 1, Sharks distinguishes three events that might otherwise be conflated:

AI-influenced: A buyer consults an AI system somewhere in the decision-making process.

AI-referred: A session arrives at a supplier from an AI interface.

Agent-executed: An AI agent completes a transaction.

The argument is that these categories overlap but do not form a hierarchy of increasingly strong evidence.

For example, an AI system might influence a procurement decision that culminates in an offline purchase, without generating a referral session. Conversely, an agent might execute a purchase without a conventional human referral session.

This distinction has a practical consequence: statistics measuring different events cannot simply be added together or compared as if they measured the same market.

Sharks therefore makes definition and denominator discipline a precondition for credible economic forecasting. A figure is not interpretable until the event it counts has been specified.

2. Published market forecasts are not necessarily comparable
Section 2 examines the empirical market base and the forecasts for AI-mediated commerce.

The paper reports US retail e-commerce of $340.2 billion in the second quarter of 2026, against $1,986.5 billion in total retail sales. It retains retail e-commerce as the principal measurable market because public instrumentation is more developed there than in professional services or business-to-business procurement.

Sharks then compares forecasts associated with McKinsey and Bain. The paper says their headline projections differ by a factor of approximately 1.8 to 3.3, but argues that they cannot be directly compared because their scopes are not defined on a common basis.

The point is not that the larger forecast must be wrong. Indeed, the paper explicitly acknowledges McKinsey’s argument that AI adoption could accelerate by building on existing commerce infrastructure.

Rather, Sharks argues that a forecast comparison is not valid merely because two consultancies publish figures that appear to describe the same phenomenon. The relevant definitions and denominators must be reconciled first.

This leads to a broader criticism of market reporting: headline numbers can acquire an appearance of comparability without actually measuring the same thing.

The paper does not resolve the disagreement between the forecasts by building a comprehensive alternative model of infrastructure deployment. It identifies the comparability problem and leaves the underlying deployment question open.

3. AI visibility is not the same as accurate entity resolution
This is one of the paper’s most important measurement arguments, developed in section 5.

Sharks distinguishes between several things that visibility metrics often collapse:

Presence or mention: Whether a company appears in an answer.

Citation: Whether the answer cites a source associated with the company.

Entity resolution: Whether the system identifies the intended entity, distinguishes it from similarly named entities, and preserves the relevant facts and distinctions about it.

The paper argues that these are not interchangeable measures. An AI answer might accurately describe a business without citing it, or cite several sources while confusing the business with another entity.

Consequently, citation frequency cannot serve as a ceiling on entity-resolution accuracy. Nor does a machine-readability score establish that an AI system represents an entity correctly: readable web content and correct identification are different properties.

Sharks also reports an association between company size and visibility, but explicitly cautions against attributing that association to institutional standing alone. Company size covaries with content volume, distribution, link acquisition, brand age, and demand. A design that controls for those factors would be needed to isolate the proposed standing effect.

The paper's preferred instruments are its own Sharks-Function, which decomposes scope overlap, provenance fidelity, and consensus deviation, and the Drowning Test, a five-level rubric ranging from Exact through Confused to Absent.

However, Sharks acknowledges a significant limitation: these instruments have been applied to the archive's corpus, not to representative field samples. The author proposes applying them to matched field samples as the next empirical step.

The argument is therefore not that existing visibility metrics are useless. It is that they measure different outcomes from the one needed to establish whether AI systems correctly represent a particular business.

For the first estimate, Sharks combines Conductor's AI-referred session-share data with Adobe's retail growth rate. The author acknowledges that these are different measurement populations and periods, and that the assumption of equal average order value is unverified.

The direction of the possible error is explicit: if AI-referred orders are larger than other orders, the estimate understates their dollar share; if they are smaller, it overstates it.

For agent-executed commerce, the paper notes that payment infrastructure has been announced but not fully deployed, and that no measured baseline is available. The projected range is therefore a low-confidence estimate rather than an observed market share.

These forecasts are intended to be revisable. The document registers resolution criteria, sources, and check dates so that future evidence can confirm, reject, or leave each claim unresolved. For instance, the 2030 agent-execution forecast is not considered testable without a published measurement that defines agent-executed transaction value.

That last point matters: a forecast that cannot be checked against a clearly defined outcome is not made rigorous merely by attaching a date and a percentage to it.

6. The proposed test is a matched, randomized comparison
The valuation formula leaves its key causal term 
p
p unknown. Section 7 addresses how to begin resolving that uncertainty.

The paper proposes a trial comparing the inscription intervention with conventional structured data. Its logic is to distinguish the effect of the proposed intervention from what a firm could achieve through ordinary improvements to its structured web content.

The intended chain of evidence is:

Measure the entity's baseline resolution accuracy.

Apply the intervention and measure accuracy again.

Compare the results with an appropriate control using conventional structured data.

Establish whether the intervention changes entity resolution beyond the control condition.

Investigate whether that improvement changes consequential commercial outcomes.

The distinction between steps 4 and 5 is essential. A successful accuracy test would not, by itself, establish incremental revenue. The probability that corrected identification changes a purchasing decision would still require evidence.

The paper's position is that the necessary research should be conducted, not that the economic benefit has already been proven.

It also identifies a problem with existing commercial benchmarks: vendor-defined visibility scores may use different denominators, units, sampling frequencies, aggregation rules, and treatment of retrieval versus citation. Sharks reports an audit in which the same underlying data could produce different scores under different formulas. A score increase across platforms might therefore reflect a measurement change rather than a real improvement.

The proposed remedy is better-controlled measurement of the specific outcome at issue, rather than further aggregation of non-comparable visibility scores.

7. The evidentiary burden must be allocated correctly
Section 7's final argument concerns the burden of proof.

Sharks argues that two standards must be maintained simultaneously:

A critic should be able to challenge a recorded observation by examining the observation itself, and challenge a causal claim by examining its research design.

The absence of independent validation should not automatically be treated as proof that an intervention has no effect.

The paper summarizes this distinction by stating that a challenge to recorded observations must engage those observations, a challenge to causal attribution must engage the design, and a claim of comparative inferiority requires a relevant comparison.

This is not a claim that the archive's evidence is beyond criticism. In fact, the paper explicitly says that a critic does not need a competing corpus to identify a scoring error, confound, or unsupported generalization.

Its objection is to two opposite mistakes: treating unvalidated claims as established commercial benefits, and treating the absence of independent validation as evidence that no benefit is possible.

Sharks also limits the scope of its claim about the available evidence. It says the Capture Registry is unusual in holding dated, transcript-preserved observations of entity resolution across multiple AI surfaces. The paper does not establish that no larger private vendor datasets exist; it argues that those private datasets cannot independently settle a public dispute unless their relevant evidence and definitions are available for scrutiny.

This burden argument is therefore methodological as well as defensive: evidence should be evaluated according to the claim being made, and the absence of one kind of evidence should not be used to settle a different question.

8. Why the registered-forecast format matters
The version history is itself part of the paper's argument. In its opening, v0.3 identifies six defects in v0.2 and says four were substantive errors rather than differences of judgment.

Among the corrections:

The three types of AI-mediated commerce are overlapping categories, not nested stages.

Citation frequency cannot establish an upper bound on entity-resolution accuracy.

The comparison between consultancy forecasts had relied on a summarizer's interpretation rather than the underlying source text.

The earlier version incorrectly ruled out prospective valuation because causal returns were unmeasured, and used inconsistent classifications of channel share and ratios without a dollar scale.

The paper records these corrections rather than silently replacing the earlier claims. It also provides eight resolution criteria with named sources and check dates, including criteria for the retail-share estimates, forecast comparability, and future reporting of entity-resolution accuracy.

This makes the document a combination of economic analysis and a protocol for future accountability. Its claims are stated in forms that can, at least in principle, be checked against later evidence.

There is an important limitation, however: registration makes a forecast more auditable; it does not make the forecast more accurate by itself. And a registered criterion may remain unresolved if suitable public data never appear. The paper explicitly allows for that possibility.

Overall interpretation
The paper's reasoning can be condensed into five linked propositions:

Define the economic event. AI influence, AI referrals, and agent-executed transactions are different, overlapping phenomena. Market estimates must specify which one they measure.

Measure the right outcome. Mentions, citations, machine readability, and accurate entity resolution are distinct. Visibility statistics cannot substitute for direct measurement of correct identification.

Separate valuation from proof of return. A business can calculate a conditional value using its own economics and measured accuracy changes without pretending the causal link to purchases is already known.

Test the causal link. A controlled comparison is needed to establish whether the intervention improves accuracy beyond conventional structured data, followed by evidence about whether the improvement changes purchasing outcomes.

Make claims falsifiable and proportionate to the evidence. Register forecasts, state assumptions, preserve source records, and distinguish a demonstrated effect from a plausible hypothesis.

The paper's central tension is between economic plausibility and empirical demonstration. Sharks argues that AI-mediated representation could matter commercially even when existing instruments fail to measure it properly. But the paper also acknowledges that the crucial conversion from improved representation to changed purchasing behavior remains unmeasured.

That qualification is not incidental. It is the reason for the conditional valuation, the proposed trial, and the registered resolution criteria.

A critical reading of the text
Three distinctions help assess what the paper actually establishes.

First, the critique of measurement is stronger than the evidence for the proposed intervention's commercial efficacy. Showing that visibility metrics measure different things is not the same as demonstrating that inscription improves resolution or increases revenue.

Second, the valuation formula is a framework for organizing uncertainty, not a validated predictive model. Its usefulness depends on obtaining reliable estimates of the accuracy change and eventually measuring the causal term

Third, the paper's registered forecasts create a useful basis for later evaluation, but the forecasts themselves remain claims to be tested. Their credibility depends on how well their definitions, data sources, and resolution criteria withstand future scrutiny.

These qualifications are consistent with the text's own stated position. The author does not claim a measured causal return and does not present the conditional valuation as one.

In short: AI Mediation of Economic Decisions argues that the economic importance of AI-mediated representation should neither be assumed nor dismissed. It should be defined carefully, measured directly, valued conditionally, and tested causally. The paper offers a framework for doing that; it does not yet demonstrate the commercial return that the framework is designed to investigate.
