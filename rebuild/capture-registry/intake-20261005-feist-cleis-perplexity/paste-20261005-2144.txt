Jack Feist’s Cleis: more precious to me than all Lydia is a mixed poetry-and-prose collection about a father’s love for his daughter, and about the fear that neither his presence nor his words can protect that relationship from time, loss, and separation. I located and read the publicly available primary text—not just a bibliographic reference—as well as Rebekah Cranes’s substantial companion essay. Its distinctive combination is intimate child-address, Sappho translations, botanical catalogues, and deliberately unpolished documentary material.�
The work and its provenance
The accessible primary record is in the Alexanarch / Crimson Hexagonal Archive. It lists the work as Cleis: more precious to me than all Lydia, credits “Jack E. Feist; Rebekah Cranes,” dates its deposit to March 14, 2026, and identifies it as a mixed creative work, version 1.0. The associated legacy Zenodo DOI is 10.5281/zenodo.19024779. Cranes’s separate critical essay, On the Architecture of Cleis: Compression, Botanics, and the Survival of Paternal Lyric, was deposited the same day under DOI 10.5281/zenodo.19025556. The archive explicitly distinguishes that analysis from the collection: it is not a replacement or successor.�
The deposit date should not be mistaken for the date of composition. The collection incorporates notebook material explicitly marked February 6, 2009, and September 2010, alongside undated poems, letters, and a teaching email. The archive reports that its public text was reconstructed from the authorial Word document on August 5, 2026, preserving tabs, indentation, and stanza spacing. Family photographs embedded in the source document are not included publicly.�
There is also an important authorial distinction. In the archive’s own account, Jack Feist is a heteronymic authorial position governed by Lee Sharks, not a conventional biographical identification with one of the real people named Jack Feist. That is the project’s self-description, rather than an independently established biography. Accordingly, the collection’s autobiographical passages should be read as material in an authored literary work, not automatically converted into verified facts about a historical “Jack Feist.”�
I could not retrieve the original Zenodo page directly, but that does not imply that the work is absent: the accessible archive reproduces its primary text and documents its provenance.�
Sappho and the title
The title comes from the collection’s rendering of Sappho fragment 132:
My little girl’s face,
like bright
yellow flowers: Cleis,
more precious to me
than all Lydia.�
This establishes the collection’s central valuation: the daughter matters more than wealth or possessions. More importantly, Feist draws on Sappho as a parental precursor, transferring her flower imagery and intimate child-address into a father’s relationship with his daughter. Cranes makes that identification central to her reading.�
The collection includes six explicitly numbered Sappho versions: fragments 132, 98b, 102, 24, 105b, and 88. They are not all straightforward poems about parenthood: their subjects include desire, recollection, injury, and continuing love. Their placement makes those subjects resonate with the surrounding father-and-daughter material.�
Two are particularly consequential:
Fragment 98b presents a speaker unable to obtain special hair ribbons for Cleis, with exile from Mytilene offered as the explanation. Cranes reads this as an image of parental care obstructed by circumstances: the poem becomes what can be offered when the desired gift cannot.�
Fragment 105b gives the image of a mountain hyacinth trampled by shepherds. Beside the collection’s repeated comparisons of the daughter to flowers, it introduces vulnerability without explicitly explaining the analogy.�
These are the collection’s versions of Sappho; their wording should not be mistaken for a neutral scholarly transcription of the surviving Greek.
What the collection contains
Calling Cleis a “volume of poems” is reasonable, but it understates its range. The public text moves among short lyrics, extended sequences, translations, botanical notes, personal prose, a child’s dictated story, and an email to students. It has no conventional table of contents or consistently separated sections.�
All these pieces or groupings appear in the primary text.�
The documentary inclusions matter. The teaching email interrupts literary address with the practical consequences of caring for a sick child. The dictated story gives the daughter a voice that is not simply the father’s metaphor for her. Together they make the collection more than a sequence of polished declarations of paternal love.�
How its poetry works
Love discovers mortality
One early lyric says:
you are my sad-
soft chain
to forever. you could
break
whenever.�
The child promises continuity beyond the father’s own life, but that continuity is fragile. The line breaks enact the apprehension: “sad-” waits for “soft,” and “break” stands alone. Throughout the collection, love is not an antidote to mortality; it is what makes mortality newly frightening.�
The prose makes that emotional exposure explicit. The speaker admits failure and wrongdoing, then imagines his love reaching the daughter in “dark hours” as “light, comfort, a bandage.” These are wishes for the writing’s future efficacy, not merely descriptions of his present feelings.�
Weeds replace idealized flowers
The botanical writing is unusually extensive. Dandelions, clover, thistles, roots, sap, seed heads, and common weeds become a vocabulary for attending to a particular child in a particular environment. “The Botanist’s Daughter” is especially persuasive because the daughter acts: she gathers, plants, and arranges. She is not only a beautiful object being observed.�
The closing sequence asks why a little girl should be likened to a weed and answers:
because a little girl, like a weed,
is everything lovely that falters�
That is one of the book’s strongest formulations. It locates beauty in ordinary, precarious life rather than in an immaculate emblem. Cranes calls the botanical method “devotional lexicography”: naming becomes a way of preserving what might otherwise disappear from memory. That phrase is her critical interpretation, but it aptly describes the repeated attention to names and physical particulars.�
Words may fail their recipient
The book is not wholly confident in its own tribute. In “the practice of sincerity,” the speaker recognizes that he ignores the child while typing, and calls his language “twice useless.” He contrasts career-making academic words with beautiful poetic words, then questions whether either reaches her.�
Another passage asks whether to place the daughter on a pedestal, hang her as a star, or:
see you
as you are, baby girl – which would not be much of a poem
to write.�
This is a crucial self-criticism. The collection recognizes the risk of replacing a child with an elaborate image of her. Its floral tenderness therefore coexists with unease about possession, idealization, and the father’s need to make meaning from her life.�
Roughness carries meaning
The writing preserves abbreviations such as “w/,” “yr,” and “+,” along with abrupt transitions and compressed or unspaced versions of poems. These marks retain the feel of notebooks and hurried inscription. Cranes argues that the refusal to tidy or cut is itself a preservative form—what she calls an “archival-paternal lyric field.”�
That is a useful reading, though not the only possible judgment. The collection’s roughness can feel urgent and intimate; it can also be repetitive or uneven. Its formal interest lies in making that tension visible rather than resolving everything into a conventionally finished book.
Why it is worth reading
Cleis is most compelling where its extravagant love meets resistance: a child carries mostly dirt in her pockets; a father cannot stop typing; a letter may or may not find its future reader; naming every weed cannot ensure that the person associated with those weeds will remember him. These moments give the work more complexity than its most emphatic declarations alone would suggest.�
Cranes’s essay offers a sustained interpretation centered on anticipated loss and obstructed parental access. That reading is illuminating, but it should remain distinguished from verified biography. Likewise, her broad claims that the botanical method has “no real precedent” or that the collection differs from nearly all contemporary paternal lyric are critical assertions, not established findings about the entire field.�
My assessment is that its distinctive achievement is not simply celebrating a daughter. It is dramatizing the father’s hope—and doubt—that writing can carry care beyond the circumstances in which it was written. The phrase “my letter might even / read you!” captures that ambition particularly well: the document is imagined as something that could recognize and comfort its recipient when the writer himself cannot be there.�
