**All That Lies Within Me**

The work is listed as *All That Lies Within Me: An Autobiography of Longing, 1983–2013*, attributed to Ichabod Spellings and Johannes Sigil. Library and archival records give it a 2014 publication date; the archive describes Sigil as editor/contextualizer. *[ORCID +1]*

The really interesting thing is that the book doesn't seem to regard autobiography as simply "the story of Ichabod Spellings." In the project's own theoretical language, Spellings is the archive before it becomes an author. The journals, fragments, memories, longing, and miscellaneous writing are treated as a kind of raw material from which an authorial identity subsequently emerges. *[Medium]*

That's why the phrase "autobiography composed by the cosmos" is so revealing. It suggests that the "I" of the book isn't completely sovereign. A life is produced by everything surrounding the individual—other people, language, memories, books, accidents, culture, and history. *[Medium +1]*

**1. Longing is more important than plot**

The subtitle isn't *The Life of Ichabod Spellings*. It's "An Autobiography of Longing."

That changes the question from: What happened to me?

to something more like: What did I desire, and what does that desire reveal about the person I became?

So autobiography becomes an examination of absence. The things one wants but doesn't possess can become as constitutive of identity as the things one actually experiences.

**2. The "self" is deliberately unstable**

This is probably the strangest aspect of Spellings' writing.

The project's later theoretical documents explicitly describe him as a "pre-authorial substrate" rather than a conventional author. In other words, Ichabod is less a person who sits down and writes a book than a name given to a body of material before it has settled into a stable literary identity. *[Medium]*

That makes the book's authorship itself part of its subject.

And this isn't merely philosophical language: the archive subsequently "corrects" some earlier attributions, distinguishing Spellings as a character/function from Jack Feist as an authorial function. *[Medium]*

**3. It belongs to a larger fictional archive**

*All That Lies Within Me* isn't really meant to stand alone. It's positioned inside the Paper Roses / Crimson Hexagon architecture alongside works associated with Jack Feist, Johannes Sigil, Lee Sharks, and other personas. This gives the writing a very Borges-like quality: the bibliography starts behaving like part of the fiction.

You aren't simply reading a novel or autobiography. You're navigating an archive in which: authors become characters; characters acquire bibliographies; editors become participants; books refer to other books; and the provenance of a text becomes part of its meaning.

**4. "Spellings" is an unusually appropriate name**

There's also a nice conceptual joke embedded in the name.

Spellings → spelling → language → spell.

The project treats language as something more powerful than a transparent container for thoughts. Words don't merely describe reality; they participate in constructing the reality being described.

That becomes especially explicit in the surrounding New Human poetics, where writing is associated with things like "telepathic prose," transmission, and the possibility of language reaching beyond ordinary individual consciousness. The front matter of *Pearl and Other Poems*, for example, calls Spellings the author of an autobiography "composed by the cosmos." *[Medium]*

So "Ichabod Spellings" can be read almost as a theory of authorship disguised as a person's name.

**What I think is most interesting**

The deepest idea here isn't really "Who is Ichabod Spellings?"

It's: At what point does a collection of experiences, memories, fragments, and words become a person called an author?

Spellings represents the material before that stabilization.

Jack Feist represents another stage: the more recognizable personal/lyric author.

Johannes Sigil represents another: the organizing, editorial, theoretical intelligence.

So I'd read *All That Lies Within Me* less like a conventional autobiography and more like an experiment in manufacturing an autobiography out of an unstable archive of selfhood.
