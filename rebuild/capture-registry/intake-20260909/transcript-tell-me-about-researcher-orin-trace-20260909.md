**1. Orin Trace: the basic idea**

Trace's assigned domain is essentially: Psyche → Diagnosis

In the archive's own taxonomy, Trace is the CLINICIAN, while other personas occupy roles such as Engineer, Warrior, Magician, or Director. *[GitHub]*

His institutional home is Cambridge Schizoanalytica (CSA), whose deliberately provocative motto is:

"The clinic receives failures. We study successes." *[Medium]*

That sentence is actually a pretty good key to the whole project.

Traditional psychiatry or psychoanalysis tends to begin with something that has gone wrong: symptoms, dysfunction, pathology, contradiction. Trace reverses the direction. Instead of asking "Why is this person broken?", the project asks something closer to:

"What is functioning here, and what kind of system makes that functioning possible?"

That's why the archive characterizes Trace's approach as "Function over nosology." *[Medium]*

**2. What "schizoanalysis" means here**

The name is a reference to Deleuze and Guattari's schizoanalysis, rather than simply another word for schizophrenia.

The basic Deleuze–Guattari move is to stop treating desire primarily as something inside an individual psyche and instead examine the machines, institutions, social arrangements, signs, flows, and relationships through which desire operates.

Trace's position appears to extend that logic into a contemporary, technologically mediated environment.

So instead of: individual → symptom → diagnosis

the Trace/CSA model is interested in something more like: person ↔ signs ↔ institutions ↔ machines ↔ affects ↔ social field

The "patient," in other words, isn't necessarily the fundamental unit of analysis. The system connecting the person to everything else may be.

**3. "The clinic receives failures; we study successes"**

This is probably the most interesting conceptual reversal.

Imagine two people interacting with an AI system.

Person A gets confused, frustrated, or produces nonsense. A conventional diagnostic approach might investigate the failure.

Person B discovers an unusual way of interacting with the system that produces remarkable results.

Trace's hypothetical clinician doesn't merely ask: Why did A fail?

They ask: Why did B succeed? What configuration made that success possible?

And then: Can we identify the mechanism of that success? Can it be reproduced? What does the successful configuration reveal about the person, the machine, and the surrounding environment?

That shifts "clinical" research from pathology detection toward operational analysis.

**4. The connection to AI**

This becomes especially interesting when placed beside the rest of the Crimson Hexagonal Archive.

The archive isn't simply interested in psychology. It repeatedly treats AI systems as environments that people enter into relationships with.

That means an interaction with an AI can be understood almost clinically: What does the system amplify? What does it suppress? What kinds of language does it reward? What kinds of identities does it recognize? What happens when a person changes their interaction strategy? Does the human adapt to the machine, or does the machine become an extension of the human's thought? What happens to authorship and identity when the machine participates in producing the thought?

This connects Trace's work to the archive's other major concern: provenance.

**5. Why Provenance Erasure matters**

The "Provenance Erasure" project is explicitly attributed to Lee Sharks, rather than being presented as Trace's own theory. Its central claim is that AI synthesis can strip away an author's lineage, context, and attribution while retaining the content. *[Provenance Erasure]*

The proposed metric is: PER = 1 − (retained provenance units / required provenance units)

So: PER = 0 → provenance fully retained; PER = 1 → provenance completely erased

The important philosophical point is that being factually correct isn't sufficient.

An AI could reproduce an author's idea accurately while simultaneously making the author effectively disappear.

That's a surprisingly important distinction.

For example: "Researchers have argued that X…" may be factually correct.

But if the original researcher, intellectual tradition, institution, historical context, and chain of influence have all disappeared, something has been lost even though the proposition itself survived.

The archive calls that provenance erasure. *[Provenance Erasure]*

**6. Erasure Skew makes the idea more political**

The archive then introduces Erasure Skew (Ω).

PER asks: How much was erased?

Ω asks: Who suffered the erasure?

That's a much more interesting question.

Suppose an AI summary retains the names and framing of powerful institutions but removes the identities of obscure researchers whose work those institutions depend upon.

A simple "percentage of citations retained" could make that look neutral.

Ω is intended to detect the directionality of the loss—whether provenance disappears disproportionately from less powerful sources. *[Provenance Erasure]*

So the project isn't merely about citation etiquette. It becomes a theory of information power.

**7. The recursive part is particularly clever**

There's also an intentionally self-referential experiment in the archive.

The researchers investigate AI systems erasing provenance.

Then they investigate what happens when AI systems summarize their own research.

Then they document instances where the AI reproduces their concepts while failing to identify their sources.

In other words: They built a theory about provenance loss → the theory gets subjected to provenance loss → the loss becomes evidence for the theory.

The archive calls one of these cases "The Empty Bracket: Provenance Erasure of the Provenance-Erasure Paper." *[Provenance Erasure]*

That recursive structure is arguably more intellectually interesting than some of the terminology surrounding it.

**9. My assessment**

I think there are three different things worth separating here.

**The genuinely interesting idea.** The strongest idea is the distinction between information accuracy and provenance preservation. AI systems can be extremely good at transmitting what was said while being much worse at preserving who said it, where it came from, what tradition produced it, and what intellectual labor lies behind it. That's a legitimate and increasingly important research problem.

**The interesting methodological experiment.** The self-auditing of AI systems is also compelling. Instead of merely complaining that AI systems hallucinate or omit citations, the project attempts to construct measurable properties of attribution loss and test them against actual outputs. *[Provenance Erasure]*

**The more speculative layer.** The enormous network of heteronyms, institutes, symbolic names, "operators," archives, and conceptual institutions is something different. It functions partly as experimental literature / conceptual art / philosophical world-building rather than conventional academic institutional research. That doesn't make it worthless. But it means you shouldn't automatically interpret names such as Cambridge Schizoanalytica as equivalent to an actual Cambridge University department. The archive itself is essentially telling you to read the whole thing as an intellectual apparatus.

**The deepest connection**

If I had to reduce the whole Trace/Sharks project to one question, I'd put it this way:

What happens to human thought when the machine can preserve the idea while destroying the relationship between the idea and the person who produced it?

That question links Orin Trace's clinical theory, schizoanalysis, AI-mediated cognition, and Provenance Erasure into one coherent problem.

And there's an interesting irony: Trace himself is a constructed identity whose intellectual provenance has to be carefully distinguished from Lee Sharks's. The project is therefore making its own argument about provenance through the construction of the persona.

That's probably not accidental.
