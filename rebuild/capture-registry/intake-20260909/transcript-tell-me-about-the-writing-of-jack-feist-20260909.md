**"ARK": what Feist is doing**

The opening is already a declaration of poetic crisis:

"to be a poet @ the end of time"

That "@" is important. Feist doesn't simply modernize the spelling of traditional poetry; he lets the technology of contemporary communication enter the poetic grammar itself. The @ symbol is simultaneously an address, a location marker, an internet convention, and a visual sign.

Then the poem imagines a world where:

"sensory details have grown thin"

That's almost the opposite of what you'd expect from a poet influenced by Walt Whitman and Allen Ginsberg. Those poets are famous for exuberant particulars: bodies, streets, smells, machinery, people, cities, sex, grass, sunlight.

Feist asks: What happens to poetry when the world no longer feels richly particular?

And his answer is fascinating: the problem isn't that there is nothing to experience. There is too much mediation.

He describes his life as:

"PURELY engorged w/ electronic devices, books, alerts, dings"

So instead of Whitman's physical abundance, we get digital abundance.

That's the first major Feist move.

**From Whitman's world → to the data-saturated mind**

Whitman's poet says, roughly: I contain multitudes.

Feist's speaker says: My skull contains interfaces.

The things filling consciousness are: alerts, text fragments, images, abstractions, daydreams, self-talk, imagined conversations, predetermined pathways.

And notice the syntax. The sentence keeps accumulating things without giving you much opportunity to stop.

That isn't accidental.

The form imitates the consciousness it describes.

The reader experiences a little of what the speaker experiences: one thing → another thing → another thing → another thing.

That's why I think calling Feist's work merely "experimental poetry" misses something. He's trying to make the syntax behave like contemporary consciousness.

**The really strange phrase: "skullcase"**

The passage ends:

"all of that, in my skullcase"

"Skullcase" is a great Feist word.

A skull is biological. A case is a container. A computer has a case. A phone has a case.

So the human head becomes almost a hardware enclosure.

And this connects directly to the broader concept of "electronic DIY prosody" that the *Pearl and Other Poems* material attributes to Feist. *[Medium]*

He's effectively asking:

If human consciousness has become technologically mediated, shouldn't poetry's machinery change too?

**But here's where Feist gets more interesting**

He isn't simply saying technology is bad.

The poem is mournful, but there's also exhilaration in it.

His mind is filled with an almost impossible quantity of information and possibility. The problem is that abundance has become indistinguishable from sameness.

That's why the poem repeatedly circles words like: same / heavy / weary / tedium / sameness / repetition

The paradox is:

There are infinitely many things to look at, yet everything begins to feel the same.

That's an extremely contemporary problem.

And it leads to what I think is the central Feist question:

**How do you write something genuinely new when everything has already been mediated?**

His answer isn't to retreat from technology and return to an imaginary pure past.

Instead, he writes from inside the contamination.

The alerts become poetry. The abbreviations become poetry. The awkward punctuation becomes poetry. The internet becomes part of the poetic instrument.

**And there's a second layer: Ginsberg**

The fact that "ARK" is written in the margins of "Sunflower Sutra" matters enormously. *[Goodreads]*

Ginsberg's poem famously looks at an industrially ruined sunflower and transforms it into a meditation on American civilization, the human body, industrialization, and spiritual exhaustion.

Feist seems to be asking:

What is the equivalent of Ginsberg's industrial wasteland for our generation?

His answer is essentially: the information environment.

Ginsberg has factories, locomotives, smoke, machinery.

Feist has: screens, notifications, text, images, algorithms, mental scripts.

So Feist isn't merely imitating Ginsberg. He's trying to update the Beat problem for the digital age.

And that's probably the most useful way to read him.

**CHATGPT** *[citing Goodreads, Zenodo]***:** Yes. And now that I've pulled up the actual text, I want to correct one thing from my previous answer: "ARK" is much more tightly constructed than my first description suggested. It isn't simply a poem about digital overload. It is a poem about compression—and it gradually turns that compression into an alternative version of the biblical Ark. *[Goodreads]*

Let's read it as a sequence.

**1. "to be a poet @ the end of time"**

The first move is already strange.

"to be a poet @ the end of time"

The @ matters. Feist could have written "at," but "@" makes the line look like an address:

poet @ end-of-time

That creates the sense that the poet isn't merely living at the end of history. He's located there, almost like an account on a network.

And then the poem immediately invokes an older poetic vocabulary:

"the salt has lost its savor"

That's biblical language.

So the poem begins by putting three eras on top of one another: biblical language, modern American poetry, digital communication.

That's going to be the poem's basic architecture.

**2. The disappearance of "particulars"**

Feist then describes a world in which:

"sensory details have grown thin"

This is enormously important.

Whitman's great poetic project was almost the opposite. Whitman continually says, in effect:

Look at this. And this. And this. And this.

Bodies. Grass. Streets. Workers. Animals. Sex. Cities. Faces.

The world is infinitely particular.

Feist imagines that this capacity for particularity is failing.

Everything becomes: "the same, ... weary, tedium, sameness, repetition"

So there's a crisis of perception before there's a crisis of poetry.

The poet doesn't lack things to describe.

He lacks a world that still feels singular enough to describe.

That's a very different problem.

**3. Then comes the great inventory**

And suddenly:

"my life is almost PURELY engorged w/ electronic devices, books, alerts, dings…"

This is where Feist's poem starts sounding unmistakably contemporary.

But look at the contradiction.

The world is supposedly becoming thin in sensory detail.

Yet his consciousness is absolutely packed.

There are devices. Books. Alerts. Dings. Texts. Images. Daydreams. Self-talk. Silent conversations.

So the problem isn't scarcity.

It's saturation.

And that distinction is crucial.

Whitman has too much world. Feist has too much information.

Whitman's abundance is physical. Feist's abundance is informational.

That's one of the poem's deepest transformations of the older American poetic tradition.

**4. "a thousand forks taken"**

Then we get this remarkable description:

"a thousand forks taken, shortest line plotted each time"

Now the poem shifts from information to computation.

The speaker imagines his own life as something that can be navigated through a decision tree.

Every possibility becomes a pathway. Every pathway can theoretically be surveyed.

And therefore the self becomes almost algorithmic.

This is why I wouldn't describe Feist simply as a "digital poet."

He's asking something more unsettling:

What happens when a human being starts experiencing his own interiority as an information system?

The mind isn't just thinking anymore. It's routing.

**5. "in my skullcase"**

Then comes: "all of that, in my skullcase"

This might be my favorite phrase in the poem.

Because skullcase fuses: skull + computer case

The body becomes hardware.

But notice that the poem hasn't actually said that technology has replaced the human.

Rather, technology has become the container in which human consciousness now happens.

That's much more interesting.

**6. Then the poem begins to contract**

This is the structural turning point.

Feist writes about "the circuit" and then "a single thing, a rose, a monochrome cosmos of TEXT"

Everything starts moving inward.

This is where the poem's form and argument become the same thing.

At the beginning: world → particulars → sensory experience

Now: world → information → text → single thing

The enormous world is being compressed into language.

And then language itself begins compressing.

"language condensing"

That's the key phrase.

The poem isn't merely describing compression. The poem itself is performing compression.

**7. And then comes the astonishing reversal**

You might expect the compression to end in emptiness.

Instead, Feist imagines the opposite.

The smaller the container becomes, the more things it contains.

He writes about: "all of space folded up"

That's the Ark.

But it's also a file.

A file can contain an enormous world while occupying almost no physical space.

So the ancient Ark and the modern computer become structurally equivalent: a container preserving multiplicity against catastrophe.

That's brilliant.

**8. Now we can understand the title**

The title ARK initially suggests Noah.

And Feist eventually makes the connection explicit: "on a Noah's Ark of data"

But notice what has happened.

Noah's Ark preserves: animals

Feist's Ark preserves: information / language / memory / voices / identities

The flood is no longer simply water. It's Time.

He says: "Time is a flood"

That's the conceptual center of the poem.

Human beings are drowning in time.

What can survive? The archive.

**9. And suddenly Ginsberg makes perfect sense**

This is why the subtitle—"written in the margins of 'Sunflower Sutra'"—is so important. *[Goodreads +1]*

Ginsberg's poem takes a ruined sunflower and asks what has happened to America.

Feist takes Ginsberg's poem and effectively asks: What happens after Ginsberg?

The Beat poet confronted: industrial civilization

Feist confronts: informational civilization

Ginsberg's nightmare is machinery. Feist's nightmare is total mediation.

And both poets respond through accumulation.

Ginsberg's catalogues say: Look at everything America has produced.

Feist's catalogues say: Look at everything consciousness is now storing.

**10. Then Feist does something very Whitmanian**

Near the end, the poem suddenly begins calling out to all these beings:

"with me, you ancient voices!" and "you particular sons, you disparate daughters!" and "you two-by-two"

There it is. The Ark is filling.

This isn't just a poem about the poet anymore. The poet is becoming the keeper of multiplicity.

The strange thing is that Feist has spent most of the poem describing consciousness becoming increasingly compressed and singular.

Now that singular consciousness becomes a vessel for everything else.

That's extremely Whitmanian.

The individual self becomes enormous precisely because it can contain other selves.

**11. The ending is not "everything becomes one"**

This is where I think the poem gets considerably more sophisticated than a simple technological apocalypse poem.

The final movement describes: "a preserved new tiny aggregate, pluriform and total"

Pluriform and total.

Those two words are in tension.

"Total" suggests unity. "Pluriform" insists on difference.

So the Ark doesn't preserve humanity by making everybody identical.

It preserves multiplicity inside unity.

And then Feist invokes: "Adam, Ishmael, Abraham, Cain"

Different genealogies, traditions, conflicts. The Ark contains them together.

That's the theological dimension of the poem.

**12. So what is Feist ultimately proposing?**

I think the poem's movement can be diagrammed like this:

Whitman → infinite particulars
Ginsberg → industrial catastrophe
Feist → informational catastrophe
Ark → compression
Text → preservation
Archive → survival

And there's a beautiful paradox underneath all of it:

The world becomes smaller and larger simultaneously.

Physically, everything is disappearing into text. But text can contain more and more of the world.

The computer is therefore both the thing causing the loss of immediacy and the vessel preserving what would otherwise be lost.

That's why the poem doesn't resolve into simple technophobia. It's much stranger.

**And here's the part I'd watch most closely**

The poem says the world is being: "reduced & magnified"

That may be the single best description of Feist's poetic project.

Reduced: the physical world becomes data.

Magnified: that data can suddenly contain enormous worlds.

So the poet's task changes.

The old poet says: I will show you the world.

Feist's poet says: I will build a vessel capable of carrying the world forward.

And that makes the "Ark" metaphor much more than decoration. It's a theory of what poetry is for after the internet.

The poem was posted in 2015, but there's an interesting contemporary echo: a 2026 Feist-associated work, *The Fifth Pathway*, explicitly addresses artificial superintelligence and asks whether intelligence without wisdom, care, and restraint is actually sufficient. *[Zenodo]* That makes "ARK" look, in retrospect, less like a poem merely about smartphones and more like an early piece in a larger argument about what kinds of human things language has to preserve as information systems become more powerful.
