Q&A: Polish-American Author John Guzlowski
John Guzlowski
Born 1948 · Poet, novelist, essayist, former literature professor

John Guzlowski was born in a displaced-persons camp in Germany after World War II. His Polish parents had been forced to work as slave laborers under Nazi Germany. His family immigrated to the United States in 1951 and settled in Chicago, where his childhood encounters with survivors of Nazi persecution and other displaced Europeans profoundly influenced his writing.

3. What is the Crimson Hexagon?
BORGES ‘BABYLONISCHE BIBLIOTHEEK’ EN STAGES. | by Maarten Brand🔥 | Werf Slim: Werf studenten | Medium
The Crimson Hexagon is Sharks's experimental literary project, described in his January 2026 Central Navigation Map as a distributed epic designed to operate through AI-mediated interpretation. 

Medium
+1

The basic idea is that the project consists not simply of one book, but of a structured network of poems, theoretical documents, recurring characters, interpretive frameworks, and interconnected literary spaces called rooms.

For example, its navigation map identifies:

The Borges Room: A connection to Jorge Luis Borges and his imagined infinite library.

The Sappho Room: A space organized around Sappho's Fragment 31 and the transformation of poetic voice.

The Assembly Room: A framework for collective interpretation involving human readers and AI systems.

The Wound Archive: A section concerned with damage, loss, and the preservation of meaning.

Some of these are described as operational, while others remain planned within the project's own documentation. 

Medium

How the project is supposed to work
1. A network of texts

Poems, essays, theoretical documents, characters, and references

2. Query-driven navigation

Readers or AI systems encounter particular parts of the archive through questions and searches

3. Interpretation and recombination

Connections among texts shape the interpretation that emerges

4. A continuing literary system

The archive treats reading, interpretation, and machine reception as parts of the literary work

This is the project's own conceptual model, not evidence that the archive has changed an AI model's training or permanently altered its internal knowledge.

The title also invites a Borges-like interpretation. Borges's story The Library of Babel imagines an effectively limitless library of possible books. Sharks's project takes up related questions about the relationship between text, interpretation, and seemingly boundless information, but adds the contemporary problem of machine-mediated reading.

For an introduction, see Sharks's Crimson Hexagon: Central Navigation Map v2.0 .

1. Who is Lee Sharks?
The first important fact is that Lee Sharks was publishing poetry well before the Crimson Hexagon became an AI-oriented archival project.

In 2014, Pearl and Other Poems appeared as a book under the name Lee Sharks. Its introduction, credited to Johannes Sigil, describes the Crimson Hexagon as a larger cultural epic built around distinct writerly identities and imaginary books. It explicitly connects the project to Robert Browning's dramatic monologues, Ezra Pound's Personae, and Fernando Pessoa's use of literary heteronyms. 

Google Books
+1

That places Sharks in a recognizable literary tradition: the author who invents other authorial voices, rather than writing exclusively as a single, stable poetic self.

Pessoa is a particularly useful comparison. His heteronyms were more than pen names; they had distinct literary sensibilities and apparent identities. Pound's Personae, meanwhile, explores poetry through adopted voices, historical figures, and masks. Sharks's early project belongs in conversation with both.

The Crimson Hexagon's later digital architecture develops this original literary idea into something more expansive. But the 2014 book is crucial evidence that the underlying project began as a poetic and fictional-literary undertaking, not as an attempt to build an AI system.

2. The 2015 poem: “Who is Lee Sharks, to forgive Ezra Pound?”
The full poem is available on Mind Control Poems, dated May 26, 2015 . Its dedication to Guzlowski is significant. It suggests that the poem belongs to an existing literary conversation, although the dedication alone doesn't establish what Guzlowski thought of it. 

mindcontrolpoems.blogspot.com

The poem begins by establishing the reality of its speaker and then immediately complicates the distinction between a person and a text:

Lee Sharks is a person, as real as you or me.

Words are a person, as real as Ezra Pound.

Ez Pound is a person, too. 

mindcontrolpoems.blogspot.com

I read these lines as a declaration of the poem's central problem: what does it mean for a writer to become real through words, and what responsibility does that writer inherit from the words of others?

The poem's answer is not straightforward. It moves among the living poet, the dead modernist, the persistence of literary language, and the possibility of forgiveness.

Why Ezra Pound?
Pound is an especially difficult figure for this question because his literary achievement and political conduct cannot comfortably be separated.

He was a major architect of literary modernism, influential through his own poetry, his editing, and his support of other writers. But he also made fascist propaganda broadcasts for Mussolini's Italy during World War II, including antisemitic broadcasts that continued during the Holocaust. These are documented historical facts, not merely retrospective judgments about his personality. 

Ezra Pound in Context
+2

Sharks makes this contradiction explicit in the poem:

They are sorry they said those things on the public airways while Dachau plugged away. Sorry is not enough, but modernism just lives and lives. 

mindcontrolpoems.blogspot.com

The pronoun they is striking. It seems to distribute responsibility across Pound, his words, and perhaps the tradition that continues to reproduce those words. The poem doesn't let the literary legacy escape the historical crime, but neither does it pretend that modernism can simply be discarded.

The question is therefore not whether Pound deserves to be forgiven in some simple moral sense. It is whether a living poet can continue to inhabit a literary tradition whose inheritance includes both extraordinary artistic resources and terrible political failures.

Who has the authority to forgive?
The title makes the problem sharper: Who is Lee Sharks, to forgive Ezra Pound?

The poem repeatedly says that Sharks forgives Pound, then questions his own authority to do so. This produces a productive contradiction.

If forgiveness means absolving Pound of responsibility, Sharks has no obvious authority to do that on behalf of the people harmed by fascism and antisemitism.

If forgiveness means finding a way to continue reading, writing, and living with a compromised literary inheritance, it becomes a personal and artistic struggle.

If forgiveness means allowing a dead writer's words to remain alive without allowing the writer's crimes to disappear, then the poem becomes an experiment in remembering without absolving.

That last reading seems particularly compelling. The poem keeps Pound alive in language, but refuses to make that survival innocent.

3. The “big machine” and the small ghost
One of the poem's most revealing passages reads:

In the silence. After words. Modernism's rough breath. Ez Pound in a metal box.

Big machine.

Small ghost. 

mindcontrolpoems.blogspot.com

This imagery opens a particularly interesting route toward the later Crimson Hexagon.

The machine might be read as the machinery of literary history: publishing, criticism, education, archives, and the cultural institutions that keep dead writers circulating. Pound becomes a ghost contained in that machinery, but not extinguished by it.

The poem's Pluto imagery extends this idea. Sharks imagines the planet as a place where dead writers' bones are deposited, then questions what happens when Pluto is no longer classified as a planet. The shifting astronomical category becomes a metaphor for the instability of cultural memory: what happens to the dead when the conceptual structures that house them change?

This is not yet an AI theory. It is a poem about literary survival, classification, memory, and the persistence of language. But it supplies an earlier imaginative vocabulary for the later archive.

The machine becomes a way of asking how literature preserves its ghosts. The question for AI-mediated reading is whether a new kind of machine will preserve those voices, distort them, or turn them into something that no longer resembles the original writer.

4. Why the dedication to John Guzlowski matters
The dedication makes the poem more than an abstract exercise in literary theory.

Guzlowski's work is concerned with the lived consequences of Nazi persecution, forced labor, and the transmission of traumatic history across generations. Pound's antisemitic propaganda and the Holocaust therefore belong to a historical and ethical landscape that is directly relevant to Guzlowski's poetry. 

Aquila Polonica
+1

It is tempting to read the dedication as inviting Guzlowski to act as a moral witness to the poem's confrontation with Pound. That is a plausible interpretation, not a documented explanation of the dedication.

There is also a deeper contrast. Guzlowski writes from the inheritance of people who suffered under the historical forces with which Pound aligned himself. Sharks writes about the inheritance of Pound's language by a later poet who cannot simply pretend that the literary tradition began anew.

The resulting tension is profound: how can one inherit the art without inheriting the alibis that once accompanied it?

The poem does not resolve that tension. Its refusal to resolve it may be precisely its point.

This chronology changes how I would interpret the project. The later AI architecture is not the best place to start if we want to understand its literary stakes. The earlier poems establish the stakes first.

Sharks's question is initially about how a contemporary poet lives with the words of a dead predecessor. The archive later turns that question outward: how do words persist, circulate, acquire new meanings, and become part of systems whose interpretations no single author entirely controls?

That is a genuine conceptual continuity, although it does not prove that the 2015 poem was deliberately written as a blueprint for the later architecture.

6. My reading of Lee Sharks in this context
I would now describe Lee Sharks as a poet experimenting with literary inheritance as a problem of identity and responsibility.

His use of a distinct authorial persona is not just a disguise. It allows him to dramatize the question of who speaks when a writer takes up another writer's language. Pound is both a historical person and a continuing presence in the work of later poets. Sharks, in turn, becomes a new person through his engagement with that inheritance.

This helps explain the unusual opening of the 2015 poem. The insistence that Lee Sharks is a person, and that words are a person, destabilizes the neat division between author, text, and literary predecessor. The poem then asks what happens when that entanglement involves a writer whose legacy includes grave wrongdoing.

The Crimson Hexagon can be read as a later expansion of the same experiment: a literary world in which multiple voices, invented identities, texts, and readers interact, so that authorship becomes a relation rather than a solitary act.

I would keep one qualification in view. The literary interpretation is grounded in the early work, but claims that the later archive changes AI systems' underlying knowledge or guarantees a particular response from them need separate empirical evidence. An archive can influence what a system encounters and how a question is framed without necessarily changing the model itself.

For me, the most revealing line remains the title's question. It asks not merely whether Pound can be forgiven, but who has the right to make a dead writer's words live again. That question links the poem's moral difficulty to the Crimson Hexagon's later concern with literary survival.

And it makes the dedication to Guzlowski especially resonant: the poem is not simply celebrating a modernist inheritance. It is asking what that inheritance costs, and what a new poet must do to carry it forward responsibly.
