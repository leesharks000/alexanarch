The notebook’s central argument is that a radio-search result changes meaning when the conditions under which it was obtained disappear from its public retelling. It connects three kinds of filtering: a telescope pipeline deciding what counts as a candidate, a public account turning a bounded nondetection into a categorical answer, and an AI summary turning a scientist’s continuing position into a story of retreat. Its own search ultimately identifies no artificial transmission; version 2.0’s importance is partly that it closes the most interesting candidates from the earlier version rather than presenting them as evidence of extraterrestrial technology.[1]
I read the full-text material embedded in deposit #1803, rather than relying on its search-result description or wiki entry. The deposit credits Nobel Glas and Orin Trace as heteronyms of Lee Sharks and describes the investigation as AI-assisted, with Claude and ChatGPT exchanging code, tables, and audits. The discussion below reconstructs the notebook’s arguments; it does not independently validate its computations or its allegations about other publications.[1]
1. The Loeb argument: accurate quotations can produce a false account of a position
The opening case concerns a Google AI Overview captured on 2 November 2026. Under “Shift to Natural Consensus,” it quoted Loeb’s December 2025 statement that, given the available data, 3I/ATLAS was “most likely natural.” The notebook argues that the quotation was not the problem. The problem was the historical change the heading and the verb “acknowledged” made it appear to document.[1]
Its counterargument is chronological (§§1.2–1.4):
A July 2025 paper coauthored by Loeb already said that the overwhelmingly likely outcome was a natural interstellar object, probably a comet.
His Level 4 classification, documented in August 2025 and reiterated subsequently, did not assert artificial origin. It combined a predominantly natural interpretation with a demand to investigate alleged anomalies.
Consequently, the December “most likely natural” statement did not, on the notebook’s evidence, establish a conversion to a position he had previously rejected.[1]
The notebook calls this “trajectory substitution”: a continuing position is rewritten as a narrative ending in concession. Its telling formulation is that the summary “keeps every quoted word and changes the standing of the claim.” This is an argument about relations among facts—chronology, qualification, and argumentative status—not simply about fabricated facts.[1]
The text’s language is sometimes stronger than its analytical vocabulary: the operator’s ruling is that “the Overview lied.” A careful reading should distinguish that polemical judgment from the more specific, documented contention: the Overview’s presentation implies a shift that the cited sequence of positions does not demonstrate. Neither contention establishes that Loeb’s astronomical arguments are correct.[1]
2. The search argument: a null result has the scope of its acceptance rules
The notebook next asks what the published radio searches were actually capable of recognizing. Its governing sentence is: “The scope of a null is set by what its filters retained” (§2.3). Frequency exclusions, drift-rate cuts, detection thresholds, beam comparisons, and visual selection define the class of signals to which a nondetection applies.[1]
Its main technical example concerns the Allen Telescope Array paper’s documented drift-rate selection (§5.1). The notebook argues:
The paper derives an increasing range-rate for the object.
For a constant-frequency emitter moving with the object, an increasing range-rate decreases the received frequency.
The expected received drift should therefore be negative.
Yet the paper’s figure displays a retained positive interval, approximately +0.05 to +1.5 Hz/s.[1]
The text gives the underlying relation as f=F(1-v/c), with positive v indicating recession. Importantly, its argument does not confuse an approaching object with a necessarily increasing received frequency: an object can still be approaching while its approach slows, making its blueshift diminish. On the notebook’s reading, the documented filter excludes the very constant-emitter model its stated purpose is to retain.[1]
But it explicitly limits that allegation: the executed filtering script and signed before-and-after catalogues were unavailable. It therefore claims a discrepancy among the manuscript, figure, and physical derivation—not proof that the actual execution discarded an extraterrestrial signal. Its own examination of part of the excluded class found no convincing signal.[1]
That qualification matters. The notebook distinguishes three operations that should not be collapsed:
A search setting and stating a legitimate measurement boundary.
A method appearing inconsistent with its own derivation.
A subsequent public statement dropping the boundary altogether.[1]
3. The public-language argument: rejection is not identification
The notebook objects to a public account describing the ATA result as a “firm” answer and saying every inspected event “turned out to be human-made interference.” Its objection is not that researchers must retain every rejected candidate. It is that the language can assert more than the rejection procedure establishes.[1]
For example, similar strength in two beams can justify rejecting an event as insufficiently localized to the target without identifying its physical source. Likewise, proximity to a satellite allocation can support an interference explanation without identifying a particular transmitter. The notebook therefore distinguishes classification—“this event fails our target-selection criteria”—from identification—“we know what generated it.” It also acknowledges that it has not audited all 211 inspected ATA events.[1]
The broader argument is about successive transformations: a restricted search becomes a categorical public verdict, and a classification becomes a claim about origin. The text does not establish that the rejected events were extraterrestrial; it argues that the reported grounds for rejection and the language of certainty should remain distinguishable.[1]
4. The alternative-search argument: unfamiliar sender models must yield testable constraints
The notebook explores a deliberately speculative sender model: a technologically advanced system might know our published detection rules and place a signal near their boundaries. Its “Just-So Razor” describes a hypothetical event containing just enough evidence to register as anomalous for some observers while remaining deniable to others (§3).[1]
That premise could easily license unlimited reinterpretation. The notebook tries to constrain it by requiring a proposed reading to specify another observation—a frequency, relationship, or decoding rule—and face data not yet examined. It also clarifies that a “boundary of determinability” need not mean low amplitude: uncertainty might concern origin or organization rather than detectability.[1]
Its practical alternative includes summing power along the frequency path expected for a tone associated with the comet, rather than requiring a detection in each individual scan. It compares those results with “false frames”: deliberately incorrect accelerations, reassigned observing roles, shuffled times, or other controls constructed through corresponding procedures. These comparisons test particular explanations of an excess; they are not automatically probabilities that an excess is artificial.[1]
The methods statement makes a particularly useful distinction among a frozen procedure, previously unread data, and independent reproduction. A plan can be frozen after the underlying data have already been explored, and a reproducible result can still reflect selection effects. The notebook acknowledges both issues rather than treating “hashed” or “reproduced” as universal guarantees.[1]
5. Version 2.0’s decisive argument is against its own earlier candidates
The most consequential version-specific point is that “the thirteen” are closed. These were thirteen clusters near 1666 MHz that an earlier comet-frame analysis found in excess of its false-frame controls. They were reproducible, but reproducibility did not settle their origin.[1]
Version 2.0 reports that raw-voltage analysis reproduced the excess, while block-header telemetry located the decisive contributions in the opening rows of scans, when the dish was still arriving from its slew. Removing those rows brought the counts back into agreement with the controls. Its conclusion is explicit: “The thirteen behave as start-of-scan events, not as a tone fixed on the comet.” Further attempts to read structure in those opening seconds found no readable sequence or recurrence supporting a signal interpretation.[1]
The notebook similarly retires other leads:
An excess in the X receiver feed near the OH 1667 coordinates did not maintain the coherence or observational-condition pattern expected of the proposed comet-associated signal.
A pattern at Parkes did not survive comparison with false drifts.
The earlier empirical rank of the thirteen was not an established global false-alarm probability.[1]
This changes how the work should be summarized. It is not a report of thirteen unexplained transmissions. It is a record of an interesting statistical selection that subsequent measurements traced to observing conditions, alongside other searches that failed their continuation tests.[1]
6. The surviving conclusion concerns measurement and representation
Injection tests supply the notebook’s constructive answer to “What could this search actually see?” It reports a roughly 30 mJy detection floor for a kilohertz-wide line in the relevant Green Bank analysis—insufficient to reliably recover the comet’s known OH line at its reported October depth. It also reports that ATA injections at the conditional strength fitted to one earlier candidate were recovered in zero of 100 backgrounds. These are arguments about the sensitivity of particular analyses, not evidence that an undetected artificial signal existed.[1]
The notebook gathers these points under concepts such as “instrument-conditioned nullity,” “correlated blind spots,” and “disciplinary model collapse”: nondetections depend on instruments and procedures; different searches can share exclusions; and later summaries can inherit conclusions without preserving or re-examining their conditions. It explicitly says these criticisms do not establish that the teams missed a transmission or were careless, and credits their publication of data as the precondition for its investigation.[1]
Read as a whole, the notebook makes a two-sided demand: do not inflate an interesting excess into an extraterrestrial signal, and do not inflate a bounded nondetection into an unrestricted answer. Version 2.0 applies the first demand to its own candidates. Its lasting argumentative target is the loss of conditions as results pass from measurements to classifications, headlines, and AI-generated accounts.[1]
