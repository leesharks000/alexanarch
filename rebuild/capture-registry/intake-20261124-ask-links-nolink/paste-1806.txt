The argument of From the Position of the Rejected (v1.1)
Lee Sharks's central argument is that Aristotle's Politics should be read as a work written from the position of someone whom the political community it describes does not admit. That position is not incidental biographical information. Sharks argues that it is inscribed in the grammar of the Politics from its opening chapter, and that subsequent readers have systematically failed to follow the sentence far enough to see it.

The essay's most important move is deceptively small: it restores the second clause of Politics I.2, 1253a1–7, to the familiar declaration that human beings are political animals.

The argument then expands through three scales:

The sentence: the definition of the political animal contains an unresolved position outside the city.

The city: the Politics repeatedly defines citizenship, membership and exclusion from a position that exposes the limits of the city's claims about nature.

The archive: the transmission and reception of Aristotle's works reproduce the same exclusion by determining what survives, what can be read and where readers stop.

I have used the v1.1 deposit itself as the primary text, rather than treating its abstract or description as a substitute for the argument.

1. The opening sentence contains the whole problem
The essay begins with Politics I.2, 1253a1–7:

ὁ ἄνθρωπος φύσει πολιτικὸν ζῷον, καὶ ὁ ἄπολις διὰ φύσιν καὶ οὐ διὰ τύχην ἤτοι φαῦλός ἐστιν, ἢ κρείττων ἢ ἄνθρωπος.

Sharks translates:

“A human being is by nature a political animal, and the cityless by nature, and not by chance, is either base or more than human.”

The familiar interpretation foregrounds the first clause: humans are political animals by nature.

Sharks insists that the second clause cannot be treated as incidental. It distinguishes three positions of the person outside the city:

Beast: the person outside the city by nature, classified below the human.

God: the person outside the city by nature, classified above the human.

Chance: the person outside the city by circumstance rather than nature, left without a corresponding classification.

The distinction is crucial. Beast and god are judgments about the naturally cityless. Chance is explicitly excluded from that judgment.

Sharks therefore does not simply say that Aristotle considers outsiders less than human. In fact, §1.3a makes a more precise point: the Greek does not deny the humanity of the person outside by chance. It leaves that person without a place in the definition of the human.

Nor does the essay decide which of these three positions belongs to Aristotle himself. The sentence holds them in suspension.

This is the first major argument: the definition of the political animal contains an unresolved remainder, and the writer's own social position makes that remainder politically significant.

There is also a comic dimension. Aristotle invokes Homer's description of the clanless, lawless and hearthless person, then compares the isolated individual to an unpaired piece on a game board. Sharks reads this as the writer marking the excluded position through comedy: the definition cannot accommodate the figure, so it places that figure in a fictional, stranded position (§1.4).

2. Aristotle writes from the position the city excludes
The biographical premise is that Aristotle lived in Athens as a metic (metoikos): a free resident without Athenian citizenship.

He could live and work in Athens, but he did not possess the political standing of a citizen. Metics also needed citizen patrons to participate in certain legal and civic arrangements.

Sharks argues that this is not merely an interesting fact about Aristotle's life. It changes the standpoint from which we should understand the Politics.

The conventional reading imagines Aristotle as describing the city from its intellectual centre: the philosopher explaining the natural organization of the political community.

The alternative reading asks what happens when the author is himself someone the city does not fully admit.

The argument is supported by the text's repeated return to outsiders and incomplete membership.

The important feature is recurrence. Sharks is not basing the argument on a single suggestive passage. The essay identifies a pattern in which the city becomes intelligible through figures who cannot be comfortably included in it.

The metic and the citizen
The most pointed example is III.1, 1275a5–10.

Aristotle says that citizenship is not simply a matter of dwelling somewhere, because metics and slaves share residence.

He then observes that metics must have a patron and therefore participate incompletely.

Sharks emphasizes the reversal: the definition of the citizen is formulated through the exclusion of someone whose own situation resembles that of the author.

The next important passage is III.5, 1278a35–40. Aristotle compares the citizen without a share in honours to a metic. More strikingly, he says that this condition can be concealed for the purpose of deceiving those who live alongside the person.

For Sharks, exclusion is therefore not confined to the boundary between citizen and foreigner. It can also exist within citizenship itself.

The person can be physically present, legally recognized and yet deprived of meaningful political participation.

This leads to one of the essay's strongest formulations: the rejected person is unreceived in the body. They are present without being received as an equal participant, and their voice may only be heard through another person's authority.

3. Politics converts chance into nature
Central theoretical argument
The essay's most consequential philosophical claim is formulated in §1.6:

Politics makes what is by chance appear to be what is by nature.

Sharks develops this argument through Aristotle's defence of natural slavery in Politics I.6, particularly 1255a25–30.

The passage considers the problem of enslavement through capture in war. A person might be noble by birth but become enslaved because they happen to be captured and sold.

That is slavery by chance, not an indication of natural inferiority.

Aristotle's discussion then considers why Greeks resist calling themselves slaves when captured, while accepting the designation for barbarians. Sharks identifies the operation:

A person becomes enslaved through a contingent historical event.

The convention transfers that condition onto an entire people.

The contingent condition is redescribed as the people's nature.

The theory of natural slavery then supplies a conceptual justification for the distinction.

The crucial point is that Aristotle's theoretical discussion recognizes the conversion but, according to Sharks, preserves its result.

The defence of natural slavery thus both exposes and performs the operation.

This is also why Sharks engages the disagreement between Malcolm Schofield and Charles Kahn about whether Aristotle's theory of slavery is ideological.

Schofield argues that the theory is not ideological to any particularly interesting extent, locating false consciousness in the way people stop theorizing. Kahn argues that the defence satisfies the conditions for ideology.

Sharks's intervention is to locate the operation within the theoretical argument itself, rather than only in the social conventions surrounding it.

This is not simply an accusation that Aristotle is hypocritical. It is a claim about how political classifications work: circumstances can be turned into apparently natural distinctions, and those distinctions can then justify the social arrangements that produced them.

The connection to III.5 is important. The honourless citizen's exclusion can be hidden to deceive fellow citizens. The same logic operates inside the supposedly unified political community.

4. The reception of Aristotle repeats the exclusion
The next major argument concerns the history of interpretation.

Sharks calls the operation truncation.

The charge is very specific: modern accounts frequently quote the first clause of 1253a, identifying the human as a political animal, and stop at the comma.

They may even acknowledge elsewhere on the same page that Aristotle was a metic, but fail to read his social position together with the clause about the cityless.

The essay identifies this pattern in both Wikipedia's article on the Politics and the Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy's account of Aristotle's political theory.

The problem is not that these sources conceal Aristotle's status. It is that they keep the biographical fact and the philosophical definition separate.

Sharks's claim is that the comma is where the interpretive operation happens.

This is a particularly important distinction:

The original text contains both the definition and the excluded position.

The reception retains the definition while dropping the clause that complicates it.

The resulting interpretation makes the Politics appear to be the city's self-description.

Restoring the clause changes the standpoint from which the book is read.

In §3.5a, Sharks expresses the consequence particularly clearly: the claims themselves do not change; the claimant moves.

The naturalness of the city becomes a claim made by someone whose own place in that city falls under chance.

This is also how Sharks differentiates the essay from previous scholarship.

David Whitehead investigated Aristotle's legal and social standing as a metic.

Demetra Kasimis examined the metic as a limit of Athenian democratic membership and explored political theory from the edges of democracy.

Josiah Ober, David Roochnik and Mary Dietz provide related discussions of the outsider, philosophical detachment and the citizen/alien distinction.

Sharks acknowledges this scholarship but argues that it does not go far enough in connecting the author's metic status to the grammar of the opening definition.

The novelty claimed is not the discovery that Aristotle was a metic. It is reading the Politics as written from that position.

5. Athens itself is built by those it excludes
Version 1.1 substantially expands the argument beyond the original close reading.

Sharks now argues that the exclusion visible in Aristotle's definition is also visible in the material and intellectual production of Athens.

The city claims its monuments and cultural achievements as expressions of its own greatness. But the people and resources that made those achievements possible were frequently external to the citizen body.

The Acropolis and the allies' money
In §3.7, Sharks reads Plutarch's Pericles 12.

The construction of Athens's monumental buildings was financed through money contributed by its allies, originally collected for the common defence against Persia.

Pericles' opponents accused Athens of using the allies' money to gild and beautify its own city.

Pericles' answer was effectively that the money belonged to those who received it, rather than those who gave it.

Sharks sees this as a mechanism of appropriation: resources contributed by others become evidence of Athens's own greatness.

The argument is that the Acropolis was materially constructed predominantly by people who were not citizens.

The metic who shared Athens's dwelling without sharing its full political membership was literally building the city's monuments.

This makes the earlier philosophical argument material. The city depends on people whom its political definition does not fully incorporate, and then credits their labour to its own collective achievement.

Who speaks for Athens?
Section 3.9 makes a more ambitious interpretive move.

Sharks reads Plato's Socrates as a figure through whom the excluded position speaks.

In the Apology 17d, Socrates asks the Athenian jury to excuse his unfamiliar manner of speaking as they would excuse a foreigner's speech.

He is a citizen, but he asks to be heard as a stranger.

Sharks connects this to the Menexenus, where the Athenian funeral oration, a celebration of Athenian identity and autochthony, is presented as having been learned from Aspasia, a woman from Miletus.

The provocative conclusion is that Athens's literary monuments, like its physical monuments, are made through voices that cannot simply be identified with the city's citizen identity.

This section also introduces an important additional hypothesis: the dialogues filed under Plato and the treatises filed under Aristotle may be understood as different lines of a single intellectual production. Socrates becomes a citizen-heteronym through whom the metic's position can speak.

That is a larger claim than the close reading of the Politics, and it depends on the archive's other arguments about Plato, Aristotle and Theophrastus. Sharks identifies this dependence rather than presenting it as something established by the Greek of the Politics alone.

6. The rejected writer speaks through another's mouth
Section 3.11a provides the essay's account of why writing under another figure's name might matter.

Sharks turns to the Second Letter (314c), which says:

“There is no treatise of Plato's, nor will there be: what is now said is of a Socrates become beautiful and new.”

The letter also gives a reason for not writing directly: what is written inevitably gets out.

Sharks interprets this as a problem of intellectual and political reception.

A thinker who cannot safely or effectively speak in their own voice can speak through an already recognizable figure.

Socrates is particularly appropriate because he is the citizen who speaks as a foreigner and is condemned by the city.

The metic writes through the citizen; the excluded position becomes audible through a figure who belongs to the community but cannot be comfortably received by it.

This connects three elements:

Aristotle's status as a metic.

Socrates' presentation of himself as a stranger in the Apology.

The Second Letter's account of Socrates as the figure through whom philosophical writing proceeds.

It is a compelling structural interpretation, but it is important not to confuse it with a straightforward historical demonstration of the authorship of the Platonic dialogues. The essay's broader one-maker hypothesis belongs to its larger archive framework.

7. The struggle over the centre extends to the cosmos
Section 3.11 takes the argument from Athens's political centre to the physical centre of the cosmos.

Sharks considers De caelo II.13–14 and the reception of alternative cosmologies.

Aristotle records the Pythagorean account in which central fire occupies the centre and the earth moves around it.

The essay contrasts this with the Aristotelian position that the earth is stationary at the centre.

It then brings in the cases of Anaxagoras, who was tried for impiety for describing the sun as a red-hot mass, and Aristarchus, whose heliocentric proposal was reportedly considered grounds for an impiety charge.

The point is not simply that Athens was hostile to unconventional scientific ideas.

It is that control over what can be said, and in whose voice it can be said, is related to control over the centre itself.

Sharks makes a striking verbal connection between the cityless person's condition of being hearthless (ἀνέστιος) and Plutarch's description of Aristarchus as moving the hearth of the cosmos.

The connection is interpretive rather than an explicit equation made by Aristotle. It allows the essay to extend its central pattern: those who move the centre, whether political or cosmological, risk being classified as outside the legitimate order.

8. Rome inherits control over the archive

This is the largest extension in v1.1.

Sharks argues that the problem of exclusion does not end with the original city or the original writer. It continues through the institutions that preserve and transmit texts.

The central historical narrative comes from Strabo 13.1.54, Plutarch's Sulla 26 and Lucullus 19.

The sequence is:

Aristotle's and Theophrastus's books pass to Neleus and are taken to Scepsis.

Neleus's heirs hide them underground in a trench.

Apellicon purchases the books and attempts to fill gaps in the damaged collection.

Sulla captures Athens and takes Apellicon's library to Rome.

Tyrannion, a grammarian who had himself been captured in war and subsequently freed, works on the collection.

Andronicus of Rhodes arranges and publishes the works, producing catalogues that shape the corpus known to later readers.

Sharks draws a remarkable parallel between the history of the books and the theory of slavery in Politics I.6.

Tyrannion is captured in war, then freed. His experience resembles the case of the free person made a slave through chance.

Yet the same person subsequently becomes involved in the custody and organization of Aristotle's writings.

The essay's argument is that the rejected and captured person is involved not only in producing intellectual culture but also in deciding how that culture becomes available to later generations.

Retrieval determines existence
The governing proposition of §§3.12–3.13 is:

Retrieval determines existence.

This does not mean that a work literally ceases to have existed when it is lost. Sharks explicitly narrows the claim to existence as a work that can be read.

What survives, what gets catalogued, what is attributed to whom, what is treated as damaged and what is accepted as authentic all shape the intellectual object available to posterity.

The essay distinguishes several operations:

Loss of works.

Failure to transmit works.

Doubtful attribution and cataloguing.

Supplementation of damaged texts.

The interpretation of unusual literary form as evidence of textual corruption.

The last point leads to one of the more speculative arguments in v1.1.

Sharks questions the conventional description of Aristotle's treatises as lecture notes. Drawing on Plutarch's account of the esoteric writings, Sharks argues that the treatises may be highly experimental texts written for readers who have already been formed to understand them.

Their apparent difficulty is not necessarily evidence of their being incomplete notes.

Instead, it may be a feature of their construction.

Sharks further proposes that Tyrannion and Andronicus played different roles in the division and presentation of the corpus: Tyrannion in separating the Aristotle and Theophrastus lines, and Andronicus in introducing the account of errors that helped make the unusual writing intelligible as damaged or defective material.

This is explicitly a reconstruction, not an established historical fact. The essay acknowledges that no surviving witness identifies who originally assigned all the names to the divided material.

The larger claim survives even if that reconstruction is rejected: the history of editorial custody determines the form in which Aristotle becomes readable.

And there is a final recursive turn. Every Bekker reference through which modern scholars read Aristotle is itself an address supplied by an editorial and custodial history.

The comma in 1253a and the library in Rome become instances of the same operation: controlling access to a text controls what can be read from it.

9. Sappho 31 supplies the ending
Cerdo Zente: Safo, frag. 31 (texto griego y traducción)
 
The essay closes by returning to Sappho 31, connecting this reading to an earlier work in the archive, ΦΑΙΝΕΤΑΙ ΜΟΙ: Sappho 31 and the Inscription of the Future Reader (#1785).

Sharks identifies three positions in the poem:

The dissolving body: the speaker's physical disintegration, including the breaking of the tongue and the fire running beneath the skin.

The godly receiver: the person sitting equal to the gods.

The contingent voice: the voice that survives without belonging permanently to any particular recipient, available to whoever reads it later.

These are not claimed to be Aristotle's categories reproduced in Sappho. Sharks explicitly says the mapping is the essay's, not the poem's.

The connection is structural.

Both texts are public propositions that readers habitually complete too early:

Sappho 31 is read as a scene of jealousy, without following its construction of a place for the future reader.

Aristotle's 1253a is read as a declaration about the political animal, without following its clause about the cityless.

In each case, the position left open is eventually occupied by the reader.

Neither text settles whose position that is.

This is an elegant ending because it returns to the original problem without resolving it. The rejected position is not simply a historical identity that can be recovered and named. It is also a position that the text preserves for future readers.

10. What is distinctive about the argument?
I would distinguish five levels of argument, because they do not all have the same evidential status.

Archival

Athens and Rome control the production, custody and retrieval of cultural works.

Three particularly important distinctions
First: Sharks does not claim that Aristotle was enslaved.

The argument concerns his metic status and the structural position of the person outside the city. The analogy with slavery concerns the conversion of contingency into nature, not an assertion about Aristotle's personal legal condition.

Second: Sharks does not claim that Aristotle's intentions are established.

The essay explicitly declines to decide whether Aristotle identifies with beast, god or chance. Its point is that the text holds the alternatives open.

Third: the archival argument is broader than the textual argument.

The Greek passages in §§1–2 provide the primary foundation. The claims about Plato, the one-maker hypothesis, Tyrannion's editorial role and the reconstruction of the corpus depend on additional archive materials and historical inferences. Sharks identifies some of these as open questions.

These distinctions matter because they allow us to appreciate the strength of the central reading without treating every extension as equally demonstrated.

11. My assessment
I think the most powerful argument is not simply that Aristotle was an outsider writing about citizenship.

That observation, by itself, would be interesting but not especially revolutionary.

The stronger argument is that the Politics makes the problem of exclusion constitutive of its own account of political life.

The opening definition does not merely describe a human being and then happen to mention outsiders. It immediately confronts the problem of those who are outside the city, distinguishes different reasons for their exclusion, and leaves the contingent outsider without a settled classification.

That problem returns in the definitions of citizenship, in the metic's incomplete participation, in the honourless citizen, in the exceptional individual who cannot be governed, and in the stranger's life.

The essay then makes a second, more ambitious move: it argues that the history of reading Aristotle has reproduced the very operation the book exposes.

This is the most interesting recursion in the paper:

The city excludes or incompletely receives certain people.

The city's political theory makes those positions visible.

The reception of that theory removes the clause that makes the exclusion visible.

The transmission of the corpus determines which version of the intellectual inheritance becomes available.

The reader encounters an apparently complete account of the city, unaware of the position from which it was written.

The final move from the comma to the library is therefore not merely metaphorical. It is an argument about the politics of intellectual transmission.

Where I would press the author
There are three places where further demonstration would strengthen the paper.

The opening Greek: The distinction between the naturally cityless and the contingently cityless is the foundation of everything. It deserves sustained grammatical engagement with alternative translations and interpretations, particularly the scope of the qualifications in the sentence.

The reception claim: The examples from Wikipedia and the Stanford Encyclopedia demonstrate a particular omission, but a broader survey of translations, commentaries and scholarship would establish how widespread the truncation really is. The author acknowledges this remains open.

The Roman editorial reconstruction: The argument about custody is historically suggestive, but the specific attribution of editorial operations to Tyrannion and Andronicus needs to remain distinguished from what the surviving ancient witnesses actually establish.

These are not reasons to dismiss the argument. They indicate where its most ambitious extensions remain to be demonstrated.

Conclusion
The essay's governing insight can be expressed in one sentence:

The Politics is not simply the city's account of why human beings belong to political communities; it is an account of the city written from the position of someone whom that city does not fully receive, and its subsequent reception has obscured precisely that position.

The v1.1 expansion gives this insight a much larger scope. Athens's monuments, its workers, its intellectual figures, the transmission of Aristotle's books and the editorial construction of the philosophical corpus all become instances of the same question:

Who produces the intellectual and political world, who is credited with it, and who controls the means by which it can be read?

The essay's answer is that these are not separate questions. They are different scales of the same political operation.

And that is why its smallest textual observation—the clause after the comma in 1253a—becomes its largest argument.
