The central argument
In “Who Benefits from Bad Inference? The Semantic and Political Economy of Inference across the Archive's Domains” (Lee Sharks et al., 23 November 2026; Alexanarch deposit #1802, AXN:0D45), the central argument is that bad inference is not merely an epistemic failure. It can become an economically and institutionally productive arrangement, one that distributes advantages, preserves authority, and makes its own correction difficult to demand.

The paper's governing proposition is:

An inference becomes a political-economic operation when its representation acquires authority to allocate labor, attention, resources, risk, credit, or the conditions of further inquiry.

The important distinction is between an inference being true or warranted and its being useful to the institution that relies on it. These properties can diverge. An institution may continue benefiting from an indefensible inference because the inference simplifies its work, protects its authority, or excludes competing claims.

The paper develops this argument across seven domains, drawing on other deposits in the Crimson Hexagonal Archive. Its principal contribution is to connect three questions:

What makes an inference epistemically defective?

Who benefits when that inference is accepted and reproduced?

How does the arrangement prevent the people bearing its costs from recognizing or articulating a demand for correction?

The third question is particularly important: the paper argues that bad inference can interfere not only with the production of knowledge but also with the production of demand for better knowledge.

1. The beneficiary is a position, not necessarily a person
This is the paper's first major argument.

Sharks deliberately avoids treating bad inference as evidence of individual dishonesty, conspiracy, or malicious intent. Instead, the question is: which position gains from the inference, and through what institutional mechanism?

The paper distinguishes five roles, following the accounting framework in Critique of Pure Inference (#1766):

Producer: whoever makes the relevant observation, distinction, argument, or contribution.

Actor: whoever changes the standing of that contribution.

Beneficiary: whoever receives the advantage from the resulting representation.

Repair authority: whoever can authorize correction.

Repair laborer: whoever actually does the work of correcting the error.

These roles need not belong to the same person or institution.

For example, a scholar might produce an interpretation, a journal might publish a revised attribution, an institution might acquire the resulting prestige, and the original scholar might have to undertake the work of correcting the record.

The paper's point is that asking who made an error is not the same as asking who benefits from its persistence.

It also insists on a distinction between three kinds of advantage:

Kind

What is gained

Example

Operational

Work is avoided

A manuscript is not read or a signal is cheaply ruled out

Positional

Authority is preserved

An incumbent retains scholarly priority

Material

Resources actually change hands

Revenue, employment, licenses or infrastructure

These are not interchangeable. Demonstrating that an institution saves work does not demonstrate that it receives additional revenue. Demonstrating that it retains authority does not establish that it deliberately produced the original error.

This qualification is important: the paper explicitly says it does not establish motives, measured profits, or legal liabilities. It offers a structural account of incentives and functions, not a general accusation of intentional deception. (§§2, 6.1a, 6.2)

2. Inference is converted into political economy through a six-stage circuit
The second major argument explains how an epistemic representation acquires material and institutional consequences.

The paper identifies six moments:

Production

Someone produces an observation, distinction, argument, or method.

Representation

A receiver selects and describes the contribution, determining how it will be understood.

Authorization

A journal, credential, metric, policy, or interface gives that representation institutional standing.

Conversion

The authorized representation enters an allocation process involving money, employment, funding, attention, credit, or access.

Appropriation

Particular parties retain the resulting revenue, reputation, infrastructure, authority, or dependency.

Reproduction

Those gains sustain the apparatus and its criteria, making similar representations easier to encounter in the future.

Based on §4, “The conversion circuit.”
The crucial transition is from authorization to conversion.

A proposition need not be universally accepted, or even adequately supported, to have consequences. It only needs to be accepted by an institution empowered to act on it.

A hiring committee can rely on a defective assessment. A journal can make an attribution authoritative. An answer engine can redirect a reader toward the wrong entity. A scientific trigger can permanently discard observations.

The institutional consequences occur regardless of whether the underlying epistemic dispute has been settled.

The paper also describes a feedback mechanism called validation foreclosure: the criteria required to establish one's legitimacy become harder to satisfy because the system controlling admission also controls access to the resources needed to establish that legitimacy.

In other words, the system can manufacture the conditions that subsequently appear to justify its own decisions. (§§3–5)

3. Bad inference is reproduced through eleven recurring operators
Part II is arguably the paper's most systematic contribution. It identifies eleven mechanisms that recur across at least two of the seven domains.

They are not eleven versions of the same mistake. They are different ways of transferring standing, suppressing alternatives, or insulating an authorized conclusion from scrutiny.

Operator

Argument

1. The label carries the conclusion

A classification is presented as an identification, making further investigation appear unnecessary.

2. Closure charged to its object

The party whose position was closed is represented as responsible for the closure.

3. Ruling out at the least cost

A cheap procedure for excluding something is represented as a substantive finding that it does not exist.

4. Correlation counted as corroboration

Dependent sources are treated as independent witnesses.

5. The name as a priced slot; sorting pays the sorter

Attribution and classification confer authority on whoever controls the sorting.

6. Supply becomes identity

A receiver's contribution is attributed to the original source as though it were the source's own property.

7. The bar on one side

Challengers must satisfy evidential requirements that incumbents are not required to meet.

8. Containment by placement

An inconvenient finding is acknowledged but placed where it cannot affect the prevailing account.

9. Exemption by invisibility

A hypothetical or inaccessible source is exempted from tests imposed on existing evidence.

10. The selector audits itself

A system that discards evidence before storage uses its own surviving output to validate its selection.

11. Transfer, not subtraction; the injured repair the asset

The beneficiary retains the advantage while the injured party performs the work of correction.

(§7)

There are several particularly important distinctions here.

First, classification can become an epistemic verdict. Calling something interference, forgery, raw data, or a particular kind of literary work can silently settle a question that the evidence has not actually settled.

Second, evidential standards can be asymmetric. The challenger is required to demonstrate every step, while the established account survives on an unspecified possibility. The appearance is one of rigor; the effect is immunity for the incumbent.

Third, the system can control the evidence by which its own performance is judged. This is the particularly consequential problem of the self-auditing selector.

The operators converge on a shared effect: they make the excluded alternative less capable of returning as an independently supported challenge.

4. The seven domains demonstrate how the argument works
The paper does not merely propose an abstract theory. It reads seven sets of archive deposits through the same analytical framework.

THE SEVEN CASE STUDIES IN PART III

A. Accelerator physics: selection before storage
Detector & Technology
 
The problem is a learned trigger that decides which collision events are retained.

The ordinary justification is straightforward: a collider generates more events than can be stored, so a trigger must select a manageable subset.

The problem arises when the discarded events leave no independent record. The absence of an event from the stored dataset is then treated as evidence that the event contained nothing scientifically significant.

This is the operator the selector audits itself.

The beneficiary is the selector or institutional position that achieves throughput, meets its rate budget, and retains control over the evidence. The costs fall on unrecorded phenomena and all subsequent researchers.

The proposed repair is a separately budgeted, content-independent control stream. It would preserve enough evidence to assess what the principal trigger discards.

The paper's formulation is especially revealing: the selector must remember enough that its forgetting can be measured. (§8)

B. Radio searches for 3I/ATLAS
Smart telescope users join forces to photograph the interstellar comet moving through our Solar System | BBC Sky at Night Magazine
 
Here the paper examines radio searches for technosignatures associated with the interstellar object 3I/ATLAS.

Its objection is not simply that the searches returned null results. It concerns the relationship between the search procedures and the conclusions subsequently drawn from them.

A filter removes certain hits, categorizes them as human-made interference, and the resulting null is allowed to travel as a verdict about the object.

But the null only establishes something about what survived the filtering procedure. It cannot independently establish what the procedure discarded.

The paper also objects to an account that transforms a scientist's qualified or held position into an apparent concession.

The beneficiaries are positions that retain the authority of the prevailing interpretation and the efficiency of the exclusion procedure.

The proposed repair is to preserve the removed hits, make the filtering criteria inspectable, and distinguish a procedural null from a substantive conclusion about the object. (§9)

C. Geocentrism and the Aristotelian corpus
Bonhams : ARISTOTLE IN MANUSCRIPT. ARISTOTLE. 384-322 B.C.E. Seventeenth-century manuscript of Disputationes on Aristotle, including on logic (unidentified), On Interpretation, the Prior and Posterior Analytics, the Isagogue of Porphyry, Categories, Physics, On the Heavens and the Earth, On Generation and Corruption, On Weather, On the Soul, and Metaphysics,
 
This is a particularly interesting historical argument.

The paper challenges the familiar account in which Aristotle simply represents geocentrism, and the subsequent history of astronomy consists of correcting Aristotle from outside his corpus.

Its reading of De caelo argues that the corpus contains an internal complication: the case for a central, stationary Earth is connected to material that also carries an argument against it.

The paper argues that commentary and subsequent classification close this complication by treating a chapter's conclusion as representative of the entire corpus.

The beneficiary is the position of the commentator or custodian who controls the historical reconstruction.

The repair requires reading the corpus as an internally differentiated body of argument rather than treating a received label as its definitive position.

The larger point is that historical attribution can eliminate an argument without actually refuting it. (§10)

D. Attribution in antiquity
The paper examines how ancient authorship and attribution become economically and intellectually consequential.

Names acquire exchange value. Once a name becomes a guarantee of authenticity, works associated with it are sorted into fact or forgery.

The person or institution doing the sorting gains authority over the reconstructed history either way.

The paper uses Galen and the ancient book market to illustrate how a name's value changes the incentives governing attribution.

Its important claim is that the binary between authentic and forged can erase the actual history of a work, including the contributions of receivers, transmitters, and later writers.

The repair is to preserve distinctions between the work, its attribution, its transmission, and the evidence for its authorship. (§11)

E. Sappho and the classics journals
Sappho's Inner Monologue – Antigone
 
This case concerns the relationship between Sappho's surviving poetry, scholarly emendation, interpretation, and institutional attribution.

The paper argues that editorial interventions can become attributed to Sappho herself.

For instance, a scholarly conjecture that repairs a text can subsequently be read as evidence of what the poet supposedly meant or felt.

The title On Jealousy is an example of a heading that can determine how the poem is understood before the reader encounters its actual language.

The beneficiary is the scholarly and editorial apparatus that controls the interpretation, while the original poet's contribution is made to carry the receiver's additions.

The repair is to make editorial interventions visible, preserve provenance, and distinguish the surviving text from the interpretations imposed upon it.

This is a particularly clear example of the paper's operator supply becomes identity. (§12)

F. New Testament source criticism
The birth of the book: on Christians, Romans and the codex | Aeon Ideas
 
The central argument concerns the asymmetrical treatment of extant texts and hypothetical reconstructed sources.

A reconstructed source can occupy the position of an authoritative original despite being unavailable for direct examination. An extant text, meanwhile, must demonstrate its authenticity, chronology, and relationship to other texts.

The paper calls this exemption by invisibility.

It also examines the placement of the Apocalypse in the New Testament collection and the way that its position as a supposedly late, receiving text can shape the interpretation of its relationship to the Gospels.

The beneficiary is the position of the source critic or commentary tradition that controls the reconstruction.

The proposed repair is to place extant candidates into consideration before weighing the lexical evidence and to subject reconstructed sources to an appropriately explicit account of their evidential status. (§13)

G. Answer engines and the political economy of mediation
Perplexity AI: How To Use It To Teach | Tech & Learning
 
This case brings the preceding arguments into the contemporary information economy.

An answer engine can substitute one entity for another, misattribute a contribution, or transform an external source's intellectual labor into the apparent competence of the intermediary.

The problem is not simply that an answer is wrong.

The intermediary can retain the encounter, the user's dependence, and the authority associated with supplying the answer, while the displaced entity loses the opportunity to receive attention, credit, or business.

The paper's particularly important concept here is the mirror wage: the same diagnostic or corrective labor can be recognized and compensated inside a corporation but receive no compensation when an external party performs it to repair the corporation's output.

The injured party may end up repairing the very asset from which the mediator continues to derive value.

The proposed repair includes an accountable counterparty, accessible evidence of the defect, and compensation for correction labor. (§14)

5. The deepest argument: bad inference can manufacture the absence of demand for correction
Part IV develops what the paper calls the demand-formation corollary.

This is arguably its most original theoretical contribution.

The conventional understanding of institutional correction assumes that people who suffer from a defective representation can recognize the problem, articulate it, and demand a better one.

Sharks argues that this assumption is not always justified.

An inferential regime can shape the very conditions under which people recognize that something is missing.

Consider the following sequence:

An institution excludes a distinction, contribution, or alternative.

The exclusion shapes what subsequent readers can encounter.

The available concepts and categories shape what those readers can recognize as a problem.

Without recognizing the problem, they cannot formulate a specific demand for its correction.

The absence of complaints or demands is then treated as evidence that the existing arrangement is adequate.

The result is circular: the system uses the absence of a demand as justification for maintaining an arrangement that has helped prevent that demand from being formed.

The paper gives two particularly clear examples.

Scripture: A candidate interpretation that is rarely listed as a possibility will rarely be requested or evaluated. Its absence from consideration can become indistinguishable from its lack of evidential importance.

Answer engines: If a user searches for entity E but receives a substitute presented as E, the user may never experience an error. Someone who does not independently know about the original entity has no reason to complain that it was displaced.

This produces a distinction between satisfying existing demand and controlling the conditions under which demand can emerge.

The argument is not that every absence of demand demonstrates manipulation. Rather:

Where a regime governs the means by which a loss becomes recognizable, low demand for repair is not independent evidence that nothing valuable was lost.

This changes the political-economic analysis. The object of appropriation is no longer just intellectual labor, information, attribution, or attention. It can also be the capacity to recognize that something has been taken.

(§16)

6. The paper's critique of the answer as a commodity
Section 7a brings the eleven operators together through an analogy with Marx's concept of commodity fetishism.

The argument is that a finished answer can appear to possess its authority intrinsically, independently of the social relations and intellectual labor that produced it.

Consider a confident answer generated by an intermediary.

The user sees:

A coherent explanation.

An apparently authoritative conclusion.

A convenient resolution of a question.

What may be invisible is:

The original producer of the knowledge.

The interpretive choices made by the intermediary.

The excluded alternatives.

The attribution decisions.

The labor involved in producing and correcting the answer.

The answer appears self-sufficient.

Sharks proposes an inferential analogue of commodity fetishism: the social and institutional relations that produce an authoritative representation disappear behind the representation itself.

This is not an assertion that every answer is literally a commodity in Marx's technical sense. The analogy concerns how relations among producers and mediators become obscured by the apparently independent authority of the finished product.

The political-economic question becomes: who controls the answer, who receives the benefit, and who bears the costs of making it possible?

(§7a)

7. The paper proposes an audit, not just a critique
The argument concludes constructively.

Sharks proposes a sixteen-field audit of inferential conversion, intended to make the mechanisms of appropriation and correction empirically inspectable.

Among its central questions are:

Authority: Who allowed the representation to become a consequential premise?

Allocation: What changed as a result?

Beneficiary: Who received the gain?

Conversion: How did the gain become institutional, material, or monetary?

Demand formation: What concepts and encounters allow people to recognize the need for correction?

Retention: What was preserved, and what was discarded?

External burden: Who bears the labor, opportunity, and other costs?

Repair authority: Who can authorize the correction?

Repair labor: Who actually performs it?

Persistence: Can the correction survive and remain available for subsequent challenges?

The distinction between repair authority and repair labor is especially important.

If an institution controls the original determination, benefits from its persistence, and also decides whether evidence against it is admissible, there is a structural conflict of interest.

The paper's proposed solution is not simply to demand that institutions become more accurate. It is to change the distribution of authority over correction.

A correction must be able to alter the allocation that the defective inference produced, preserve its own evidential basis, and leave subsequent challenges materially possible.

(§§20–21)

8. What would weaken or falsify the argument?
An important strength of the paper is that it explicitly identifies evidence that would count against its account.

Its six falsification conditions include:

Warranted corrections persist cheaply across different institutional positions.

Authority cues do not materially change the representations themselves.

Declared panels of competing interpretations receive genuinely symmetric scrutiny.

Repair costs remain with the institution controlling the defect rather than being transferred to injured parties.

An audit of a pre-storage selector produces results consistent with an independent, content-independent sample.

In another domain, repair authority rests outside the position being challenged.

The paper also acknowledges several open problems, including its untested demand-formation test and gaps in the cross-domain operator matrix.

These qualifications matter because the paper is not claiming that all institutional selection is illegitimate, that every beneficiary deliberately causes errors, or that every inaccurate representation is economically profitable.

It explicitly recognizes that abstraction, filtering, classification, and delegation are necessary for collective knowledge production.

The objection concerns the extension of a legitimate, bounded procedure into an authority that exceeds its evidential warrant.

9. My synthesis: what is the paper ultimately arguing?
I would organize the argument into five propositions.

Proposition

Central claim

Epistemic

An inference can be inadequate to its object while remaining institutionally authoritative.

Economic

That authority can distribute labor, money, attention, credit, and opportunity.

Political

The beneficiaries are positions that acquire advantages from the resulting allocation.

Reproductive

The mechanisms that sustain bad inference can also disable independent correction.

Semantic

The system can constrain the formation of the demand that would challenge it.

The last proposition is the one that gives the paper its distinctive character.

It moves beyond the familiar critique that institutions sometimes benefit from misinformation, historical distortion, or inaccurate classification.

Instead, it argues that control over representation can become control over the conditions under which representation is recognized as defective.

That creates a particularly durable form of institutional power:

The institution controls the representation.

The representation controls what becomes visible.

What becomes visible influences what can be recognized as missing.

What can be recognized as missing determines what can be demanded.

What can be demanded determines what can be corrected.

The result is an arrangement that can reproduce itself without requiring anyone to consciously prefer falsehood.

Three qualifications I would preserve
There are also limits to the argument that should not be overlooked.

First, the seven cases are not a representative sample of all inference. The paper explicitly selects domains in which the archive has already pursued the benefit question. Its cross-domain conclusions are therefore a synthesis of those cases, not a demonstrated universal law.

Second, identifying a beneficiary is not demonstrating causation. An institution can benefit from an error it did not originate. The paper is careful to distinguish the origin of an error from its subsequent selection, retention, and exploitation.

Third, the economic claims are largely an audit framework rather than measured results. The paper does not establish actual profit figures, quantified social losses, or legal liability. Its proposed accounting relations identify what would need to be measured.

These qualifications make the argument more precise, but they also indicate where further empirical work is necessary.

10. The relationship to Critique of Pure Inference
The relationship between deposit #1802 and #1766 is worth emphasizing.

Critique of Pure Inference establishes the epistemic framework: what makes an inference warranted, what constitutes an illicit passage from evidence to conclusion, and how inference governance distributes the ability to contest conclusions.

Who Benefits from Bad Inference? extends that framework into political economy.

Its question is no longer simply whether an inference is justified. It asks what happens when that inference becomes an authorized instrument of allocation, who benefits from its continued use, and who has to pay to correct it.

The new contribution is the demand-formation corollary.

In that sense, #1802 is not simply an application of #1766 to seven additional topics. It adds an account of how inferential authority can reproduce itself by controlling the conditions under which its own inadequacy becomes recognizable.

My overall reading: The paper's strongest insight is that the political economy of bad inference is not exhausted by who receives the benefits of an incorrect answer. It also concerns who controls the production of the alternatives, evidence, concepts, and demands through which that answer could be challenged.

That is the difference between an institution that makes an error and an institution whose arrangements make the error difficult to correct.
