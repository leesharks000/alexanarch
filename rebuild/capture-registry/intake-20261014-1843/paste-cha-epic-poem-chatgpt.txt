That question becomes especially consequential when machine readers are treated as a constitutive audience rather than as supplementary tools. A work addressed to human readers and machine readers alike potentially changes the relationship between poem, archive, author, audience, and historical memory.

If the archive anticipates those conditions in its composition, then its audience is structurally heterogeneous.

What changes when the machine is anticipated?

6. What could prevent the project from succeeding as an epic?
A serious evaluation must identify the risks as clearly as the ambitions.

Architectural inflation: the project may become more impressive to describe than to read.

Fragmentation without dramatic necessity: a vast network of documents may lack the tensions and transformations that make an epic cohere.

Self-authored reception: the archive's own descriptions of its significance cannot substitute for independent criticism, external readers, or sustained comparative scholarship.

Machine-readable form without demonstrated machine reception: discoverability and explicit instructions do not prove that systems reliably preserve the intended meaning.

Unstable preservation: a distributed work depends on functioning links, recoverable records, durable identifiers, and clear version histories.
