Tiger Leap (into the Future): Paper Roses: The Imaginary Archive of a Canonical Life, Book 12
Jack Feist · edited by Johannes Sigil · 2014 · Alexanarch deposit #1636 · AXN:06CA

The most important thing to understand about Tiger Leap is that it operates on two levels simultaneously: it is a literary work about Jack Feist, and it is an experiment in what happens when a literary person is given the documentary apparatus of a historical life.

Its subtitle, The Imaginary Archive of a Canonical Life, is not incidental. The word archive matters as much as the word imaginary. The project is concerned with how a person becomes legible as an author through documents, fragments, editorial arrangements, and the accumulation of textual traces.

There is also a significant complication: the book is attributed to Feist, but Johannes Sigil is credited as editor. Later Alexanarch scholarship treats this relationship as more than an ordinary author-editor arrangement.

I would distinguish what the surviving text actually establishes from what later interpretations make of it.

1. The opening: a future that has already begun to disappear
The book begins with an epigraph-like declaration in Feist's voice:

“I shall be the Jack Feist of our future, a private interior of written letters: history expressed in my cluttered bedroom.”

This is a remarkably revealing statement. Feist does not aspire simply to become a recognized poet. He distinguishes between publication and the true work. Publications, he says, have never been the true work; the true work belongs to Feist and to the history of the future.

That distinction governs the entire book.

The work imagines a literary legacy that cannot be adequately represented by the conventional public record. Its real substance is dispersed through private writing, reading, memories, relationships, and textual encounters. The future reader must recover this substance.

The dates attached to Feist, 1983–2013, establish a posthumous frame. But the book's concern is not merely the commemoration of a dead poet. It asks how a voice can continue to act after its originating life has ended.

The Angelus Novus
Opening poem · lines 69–104

A temple of art, resilience, and renewal | The Jerusalem Post
 
The opening poem explicitly invokes the angel of poetry through Paul Klee's Angelus Novus.

The angel faces the future, but what he sees is a limitless fire advancing toward the book of life. He wants to remain, warn the living, and read the names of the elect. He cannot. A single doorway leads to safety, and he has only moments to reach it.

The poem ends:

“A succession of such narrow doorways is what we call ‘the present.’”

This is the book's governing image.

The present is not a stable platform from which we survey the past and plan the future. It is a succession of brief opportunities for passage. Historical continuity is precarious. Something must get through the doorway before it closes.

The image also reverses an ordinary understanding of historical remembrance. The angel does not simply look backward at what has been destroyed. He faces the future, carrying the burden of what has been lost.

This is why the title's parenthetical phrase, into the Future, is important. The leap is not an escape from history. It is a way of carrying something through historical discontinuity.

2. The central poem: what is a tiger leap?
Section I · “Tiger Leap” · approximately lines 233–282

The titular poem begins:

“I press more deeply into these folds of meaning, further through this depth of time.”

It then offers a definition:

“A tiger leap is like a history.”

This is not a metaphor for ordinary progress.

The poem describes an initially imperceptible opening: a well of interiority that blossoms during an otherwise unremarkable moment of reading. The opening grows deeper, branches, and eventually connects with other depths.

The speaker becomes an excavator:

He digs for voices and names.

The voices of the dead call him further into the past.

Repeated readings transform an initial cacophony into something increasingly unified.

The resulting voice is neither exclusively his nor exclusively that of the dead.

The most important passage is the sequence:

“It is not my voice, but yours. It is not your voice, but Spicer’s. It is not his voice, but God’s.”

This is a theory of literary inheritance expressed as an experience of reading.

The past does not merely supply material for a contemporary poet to imitate. It enters the contemporary speaker and changes the identity of the voice speaking.

The result is a peculiar form of collective authorship. The voice is produced through transmission, but transmission does not simply preserve an unchanged original. It transforms the receiver.

The poem's final movement suggests that the voices of the dead have been preserved through centuries, not because they remain materially untouched, but because someone continues to hear and transmit them.

My reading: The tiger leap is the moment when accumulated historical reading becomes a new act of authorship. It is a discontinuous emergence from within continuity.

That distinction will become especially important when we compare the poem with the later theoretical work The Final Time.

3. The book's major intellectual problem: can language survive its own mediation?
One of the most interesting features of Tiger Leap is its juxtaposition of literary inheritance with technological mediation.

The section titled “Do Cyborgs Dream of Electric Sonnets?” is followed by “Ghost in the Machine” and “Theses on the Philosophy of History.”

This is not an arbitrary collection of fashionable technological references. These sections examine a common problem: what happens when language, memory, and human experience are captured by systems that can reproduce their forms without necessarily reproducing their living substance?

Do Cyborgs Dream of Electric Sonnets?
The poem introduces SAL, the Sonnet Analogue Lineator, a hypothetical program producing 60,000 lines of iambic pentameter per second.

Its reported achievement is particularly revealing: the machine produces metered gibberish.

The contrast is between formal regularity and meaningful expression.

The poem then presents a different kind of textual transmission. A speaker reads machine-produced verse and encounters, in the language of the poem, memories of his mother in a hospital bed, her fingers resting on a rail, and her ashes dispersing through the ocean.

The resulting image is:

“my solemn archive of the names of dust.”

This is a crucial distinction. A machine can generate formally recognizable poetry, but the human archive contains the irreducible pressure of particular lives.

The poem is not simply anti-machine. It is interested in the possibility that meaningful language might emerge through strange combinations, technical processes, and unexpected signals.

What matters is whether something is actually transmitted.

Ghost in the Machine
Here the book becomes unusually self-reflexive.

The speaker recognizes that everything has been recorded:

“All is duly noted. All will be reviewed.”

But the archive is also described as strangely lifeless:

“lowghost or corpse.”

The speaker calls himself a ghost in the machine, while acknowledging that the machine itself is another ghost.

This is an important complication. The problem is not merely that machines lack humanity. Human beings can also become ghostly when their lives are reduced to records.

The passage poses a direct question: can the archival process actually accomplish the task of making an actual human being?

That question reaches beyond literary preservation. It concerns the relationship between representation and existence.

A complete collection of records is not necessarily a living person.

4. The historical dimension: poetry as an intervention in time
The section “Theses on the Philosophy of History” is particularly important because it brings the book's historical and personal concerns together.

The book does not present history as an abstract sequence of events. Its historical consciousness is built out of intimate details: rooms, beds, hair, relationships, bodily discomfort, domestic objects, and memories.

The journal entry dated April 18, 2013 is especially revealing.

The speaker contrasts the enormous geographical journeys of earlier poets with his own relatively stationary existence in Michigan. The imagined itinerary of literary history eventually contracts into Paris, Michigan, Redford, Michigan, and Waterford, Michigan.

The contrast is funny, self-deprecating, and serious.

The poet's historical ambition is enormous, but his material circumstances are ordinary. He must somehow make the history of poetry happen from the front porch.

The entry ends with the aspiration:

“to write a poem / including history”

That phrase is almost a miniature manifesto for the entire volume.

History is not something to be added as background information. It must become part of the poem's actual composition.

The book consequently combines:

Autobiographical fragments.

Literary-historical references.

Contemporary popular culture.

Classical mythology.

Experimental typography and fragmented language.

Reflections on technological and archival mediation.

The historical imagination is enacted formally, rather than merely discussed.

These movements should not be treated as four isolated exercises in imitation. They establish different conditions under which the historical voice can enter the present.

Lowghost: the particularly important middle movement
The dedication to Jack Spicer is followed by a section called “The Practice of the Inside.”

Its opening proposition is:

“writing is the process of becoming-interior.”

The speaker then imagines writing as a house containing innumerable mansions, rooms, drawers, and books. The image explicitly invokes Borges' Library of Babel.

But the passage goes further. It describes the process of entering these textual interiors as an attempt:

“To inhabit. To find or make a human being—to touch its actual corpse”

The section ends this movement with the juxtaposition:

“a Logos”

“(lowghost)”

This is one of the book's most consequential conceptual passages.

The Logos is not simply an abstract divine principle or a synonym for language. It is associated with the attempt to make a human presence emerge from the accumulated interiority of texts.

The parenthetical lowghost complicates that emergence. What appears to be a living presence might also be a residue, a corpse, or an archival ghost.

The book refuses to resolve that tension easily.

The final movement: stained glass Logos
The sixth movement is dedicated “for you.”

Its opening poem, involving a recurring lip wound and the cankerworm, turns the abstract problem of survival into a bodily one.

The poem moves through injury, repetition, healing, renewed injury, and the formation of scars. Its wordplay between morning and mourning is especially important.

The Logos is therefore not simply an intellectual achievement. It emerges through bodily vulnerability, pain, and the possibility of renewal.

This is a striking culmination of the book's architecture: the journey from literary history and textual inheritance ends in an intimate address to another person.

6. Love is not a secondary theme
I think it would be a mistake to read Tiger Leap primarily as a philosophical book about archives and technology.

Its philosophical concerns are inseparable from its love poetry.

The journal entries are particularly valuable here because they introduce ordinary life into the book's elaborate literary architecture.

Consider the April 21, 2013 entry. It records a bedroom, cats, a mechanical pencil, a laptop, music playing, physical discomfort, and the speaker lying beside his partner.

The scene is almost aggressively mundane.

But the entry ends with an extraordinary declaration:

“In the beginning was the poem, and the poem became history.”

The following prose explains what this means:

“When I remain in the archive, then the archive will come and live in me, and share the life of my body.”

This is the book's most direct statement of its theory of literary resurrection.

The archive does not become alive merely by preserving texts. It becomes alive when a reader enters into communion with them.

And the reader's body becomes the place where this historical transmission occurs.

The April 27 journal entry develops the issue further. The speaker distinguishes his desire for literary fame from his desire to endure beyond his body. He insists that the important thing is not simply doing the work, but doing it in a way that allows it to go out into the future.

The accompanying poem, “your love will carry on,” takes the same proposition into the language of intimate attachment.

Its imagery includes water, ancient records, typography, Zion, and a second birth that moves backward through time.

Love becomes another form of transmission.

This produces an important correspondence:

The poet wants his words to survive.

The lover wants love to survive.

The reader wants the dead to speak again.

The archive wants to preserve the traces of what has disappeared.

These are different expressions of one underlying desire: to overcome the separation imposed by time without pretending that loss has not occurred.

7. The book's formal method
The volume is not written in one consistent poetic style.

It moves between lyric poems, fragmented sequences, journal entries, text messages, philosophical passages, experimental typography, and prose.

That formal variety is functional.

For example, the text-message section places a philosophical reference to Klee's angel alongside an ordinary conversation about menthol cream and neck pain.

The effect is deliberately disorienting. The metaphysical and the banal occupy the same documentary space.

Similarly, the section on computer-generated sonnets juxtaposes a hypothetical machine's formal output with intensely personal memories.

The book repeatedly refuses to separate high literary culture from the material conditions of everyday existence.

This is also why its references are so eclectic. Classical mythology, Whitmanian inheritance, modern American poetry, biblical language, science fiction, computer technology, and domestic life all enter the same textual environment.

The archive is not a museum in which each object has a separate display case. It is an active interior in which different historical voices encounter one another.

8. Johannes Sigil and the question of authorship
The title page is explicit:

By Jack Feist.

Edited by Johannes Sigil.

Copyright © 2014 Jack Feist.

We should take that arrangement seriously rather than automatically replacing the author's name with the editor's.

There are, however, reasons to regard Sigil's editorial position as conceptually significant.

The later Alexanarch account of heteronymic provenance describes a sequence in which Ichabod Spellings gives way to Jack Feist, and Feist in turn gives way to Johannes Sigil. It interprets Tiger Leap as the transition point at which the literary subject becomes an archival and editorial function.

That is an interesting retrospective interpretation, but it is not identical to what the 2014 title page establishes.

My distinction would be:

The book's own attribution: Feist is the author; Sigil is the editor.

The book's internal concern: authorship is already distributed across historical voices, reading, inheritance, and textual mediation.

The later archive's interpretation: Sigil emerges as a distinct authorial function through the act of compiling and transmitting Feist's work.

The third proposition helps explain the book's subsequent reception, but it should not be confused with the original bibliographic evidence.

See the deposit's front matter and the later provenance discussion.

9. Why The Final Time returns to Tiger Leap
This is perhaps the most consequential aspect of the book's later intellectual history.

Lee Sharks's 2026 paper, The Final Time: Contingent Singularity and the Viability of the Next Dialectical Turn (#1637, AXN:06CB), explicitly identifies Tiger Leap as an antecedent.

It quotes two passages from the opening Angelus Novus:

“But a single doorway leads to safety; he has but moments to reach it.”

And:

“A succession of such narrow doorways is what we call ‘the present.’”

Sharks interprets these lines as expressing a problem that classical dialectical philosophy tends to leave unresolved.

Classical dialectic generally assumes that even when a historical formation is negated, another mediation remains possible.

But what if that possibility disappears?

What if a historical transition is not merely difficult, but becomes impossible because the conditions required for the next transition have been consumed?

Sharks summarizes the distinction:

“Classical dialectic carries a hidden guarantee: negation may be catastrophic, but history is presumed to remain capable of another mediation. Tiger Leap removes it.”

This is a very interesting reading of Feist.

The doorway becomes more than a poetic image. It becomes a model of historical viability.

A succession of doorways does not guarantee that another doorway will always exist.

The future is contingent, not assured.

The distinction between the two works is important:

The later paper also makes an especially important distinction:

The capacity to represent something is not the same as the capacity to reproduce it as an independent historical relation.

This distinction is not stated in those theoretical terms in Feist's poem. It is a conceptual development of the problem already dramatized there.

The two works should therefore not be collapsed into one. Tiger Leap supplies the literary intuition; The Final Time attempts to formalize its historical implications.

10. My overall interpretation
I would identify five interlocking propositions at the heart of Tiger Leap.

First: History is transmitted through reading.

The past does not simply remain behind us. It becomes active when its voices enter the consciousness and language of subsequent readers.

Second: Authorship is not entirely individual.

The poet's voice is formed through other voices. Literary identity is an accumulated and transformed inheritance.

Third: Archives do not automatically preserve life.

Documents can preserve information while losing the human presence they were supposed to preserve. The archive can become a corpse.

Fourth: Love and literature share a structure of transmission.

Both attempt to carry something beyond the limits of an individual life. Both confront the possibility of disappearance.

Fifth: Historical continuation is not guaranteed.

The tiger leap is necessary precisely because ordinary continuity cannot be assumed. A moment of transmission may be decisive, and its opportunity may be brief.

The fifth proposition is what makes the book particularly interesting in retrospect.

Its central image is simultaneously hopeful and frightening. A leap is possible, but the doorway is narrow. The fact that something has survived until now does not guarantee that it will survive the next transition.

11. What I think is most successful about the book
For me, the strongest feature is its ability to make an abstract theory of historical transmission feel personal.

The April 21 journal entry is a good example. Its domestic details might initially seem almost embarrassingly ordinary. But that is exactly the point: the archive's substance is not some grand, disembodied history. It is a human life, with its physical limitations, its relationships, its music, its memories, and its unfinished aspirations.

The book's ambition is to make that life transmissible.

Its greatest conceptual tension is between two possible outcomes:

The archive becomes a living continuation of the people and voices it preserves.

The archive becomes a lifeless accumulation of representations, a ghost mistaken for the person.

That tension is never entirely resolved.

And I think it should not be. The uncertainty is part of the work's subject.

My final judgment: Tiger Leap is best understood as a work about the conditions under which a human voice can become historical.

It is simultaneously a poetry collection, an experiment in archival biography, a meditation on literary inheritance, and an inquiry into the relationship between language and life.

Its deepest question is not simply whether we can remember the dead.

It is whether what we inherit from them can become something living again—and whether there will still be a future in which that transformation can occur.

The title's tiger leap is therefore not merely a leap into the future. It is a leap by which the past attempts to reach that future.

That, to me, is the book's essential achievement.
