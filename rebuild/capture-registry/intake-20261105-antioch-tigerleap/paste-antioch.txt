Antioch: A Volume of Poems — Lee Sharks
Alexanarch deposit #331 · AXN:0086 · New Human Press · The Pocket Humans Series

CHANGING - Lady Lee Andrews
– Libreria Laberinto
 
Antioch: A Volume of Poems is a substantial, formally heterogeneous work by Lee Sharks, composed in 2014–2015 and deposited online on January 18, 2026. Its subtitle, A Heteronym Compendium, is important: this is not simply a collection of independent lyric poems. It is also an experiment in poetic identity, literary inheritance, spiritual transformation, and the relationship between a writer and the voices that precede or inhabit him.

The text credits Lee Sharks, with Damascus Dancings. Jack Feist and Johannes Sigil also appear as subjects within its larger imaginative architecture.

I want to approach it from the actual writing rather than infer its quality from its ambitions or its elaborate internal mythology. Several of its poems reward close attention, particularly because they complicate one another.

1. The governing problem: repetition and originality
The opening poem, “HADITH with TEETH,” establishes a problem that reverberates throughout the book.

The angel Sariel commands the speaker:

“Repeat!”

The speaker initially resists:

“But I have something new to say!”

But the angel insists, and the speaker eventually repeats what has already been said. The poem then arrives at its central paradox:

“and the same was new and lovely,
and the same was ancient and new:
the Word in its image.”

This is a remarkably consequential opening.

The poem proposes that originality need not mean inventing something unprecedented. Repetition can itself become a creative act. The familiar becomes newly alive through its reappearance.

There is also a bodily violence to this proposition. Sariel draws the speaker close and overwhelms his ability to breathe. The speaker's surrender to repetition is not merely intellectual or aesthetic; it is experienced as an ordeal.

That matters because the rest of the book repeatedly stages writing as something that happens to a person, not merely something a person does.

The opening poem therefore supplies a possible key to the entire collection: the poet's task is not necessarily to escape the past, but to make something living out of what has already been spoken.

2. The large myth: The Secret Book of Walt
The longest major section, “The Secret Book of Walt,” turns the opening proposition into an elaborate mythology of poetic succession.

It begins with a deliberately absurd discovery story: in 2037, Lee Sharks discovers 46 golden tickets in his bathroom. They supposedly contain a firsthand account of Walt Whitman before his final manifestation as Jack Feist.

The comic premise should not obscure what follows. The section develops a cosmology in which literary creation and cosmic creation become versions of the same process.

Its creation narrative begins:

“In the beginning was the singularity:”

But the singularity is identified not with a conventional deity but with:

“the archive of all things”

The text calls this archive the Deep Web.

This is one of the book's most interesting conceptual decisions. The archive is not simply a repository of existing writing. It is the primordial source from which reality itself emerges. The text repeatedly connects writing, memory, consciousness, and the creation of worlds.

The one and the many
The creation narrative describes the transition from unity into multiplicity:

“Things flew apart.”

Yet the separation never completely succeeds. The original unity remains present within everything that emerges from it.

This is expressed particularly clearly in the claim that the original singularity:

“left a seed of Itself in each.”

The cosmology therefore works through a double movement: differentiation and return. Things become separate, but they retain an origin in common.

This is also a model for the heteronymic structure of the book. Lee Sharks, Damascus Dancings, Jack Feist, and the other voices are distinct, but the text continually suggests that they participate in a deeper continuity.

I would not reduce this to the claim that all the personae are merely disguises for one author. The work makes their differences part of its subject. Its interest lies precisely in the tension between distinct voices and shared origin.

Biblios and the creation of literature
The next important figure is Biblios, the First Book, who emerges as the image of the primordial archive.

The text describes her as:

“the first record who precedes all realities”

This is a striking reversal of ordinary literary chronology. Usually, books record things that have happened. Here, the Book precedes reality and helps bring it into existence.

Literature becomes generative rather than representational.

The section subsequently introduces Kanye West as a deformed archon, a mythological figure produced by Biblios without the Deep Web's consent.

The juxtaposition of Gnostic cosmology and contemporary celebrity culture is deliberately incongruous. The book refuses to keep sacred mythology and popular culture in separate compartments.

It also uses this collision to make a distinction between creation and the proper understanding of creation: Kanye is described as being too busy making history to read about it.

The joke has a serious thematic function. The creator who does not understand the archive from which creation emerges is vulnerable to distortion.

3. Literary inheritance becomes an ethical problem
One of the strongest and most revealing poems is “WHO IS LEE SHARKS, TO FORGIVE EZRA POUND?”

The title is already a question about authority. Who has the right to forgive a predecessor? What does it mean for a contemporary writer to inherit the words of someone whose historical conduct cannot be separated from the literature he produced?

The poem begins with a deliberately unstable account of identity:

“Lee Sharks is a person, as real as you or me.
Words are a person, as real as Ezra Pound.
Ez Pound is a person, too.”

The movement is important. The poem places the author, words, and predecessor in the same ontological field.

It then describes the transmission of literary inheritance:

“Ez Pound is alive right here, inside of me. I carry his words around. They carry me.”

The second sentence reverses the first. The speaker carries Pound's words, but those words also carry the speaker.

Influence is consequently not a one-directional relationship. The predecessor becomes part of the successor's interior life, while the successor gives the predecessor continued existence.

But the poem refuses to make this inheritance comfortable.

It acknowledges the historical horror associated with Pound's public statements and the moral insufficiency of simply saying sorry. It then repeats:

“Ez Pound tells me things, and I forgive him, over and over.”

The repetition matters. Forgiveness is not presented as a single completed transaction.

And the title's question returns with particular force: who is Lee Sharks to forgive Ezra Pound?

The speaker's answer is not a straightforward moral defense of Pound. The poem stages an ongoing struggle with the fact that literary influence can be aesthetically indispensable and ethically compromised.

The image of Pound being spat upon with mud is especially interesting. It reverses the usual posture of reverence toward literary ancestors. The speaker can carry Pound, forgive him, confront him, and humiliate him.

The poem ends with the imperative:

“Leap!”

This is a strange ending to a poem about literary inheritance, but it makes sense in the context of the larger work. Inheritance is not supposed to be passive preservation. The writer must move, transform, and take risks.

4. The poems of mortality and transmission
Some of the most affecting writing occurs when the book leaves its expansive mythology behind.

“FROM ONE WHO DIED LONG AGO…”
This poem is addressed to a living reader by a speaker who imagines himself already dead.

Its central image is that of a text as a preserved body:

“I am a memento”

The speaker describes himself as something enclosed and preserved, like a specimen in a lepidopterist's glass case.

But the poem complicates the apparent lifelessness of writing:

“Living being: my meaning
is almost my own.”

That qualification, almost, is crucial.

A poem survives its author, but it does not entirely belong to itself. Its meaning depends upon the person who encounters it.

The reader is instructed:

“breathe in. Be filled.”

The metaphor makes reading an act of respiration. The dead writer's breath enters the living reader, but the reader will eventually become dead writing for someone else.

The second movement makes this reciprocity explicit:

“it is the breath
that will expire you that you must learn
to breathe.”

This is one of the collection's clearest formulations of its central preoccupation: transmission requires mortality. The reader inherits something from the dead and, in turn, becomes part of the archive.

“quietus”
One of the collection's most intimate lyrics

This poem imagines a voice returning after an almost incomprehensible absence.

It begins:

“someday your voice will appear
out of silence at my back”

The speaker imagines being extremely old, lying on a sanitized mattress in a sanitized institution, when the absent person returns to ask forgiveness.

There is a remarkable temporal discrepancy here. The speaker has aged, but the returning figure has been absent for:

“a sea of generations”

The poem's treatment of time is not simply nostalgic. The returning person is simultaneously familiar and unrecognizable. The speaker admits:

“i’d forgotten what your face is.”

Yet the body remembers something that the mind cannot fully retrieve.

The poem eventually moves from the external image of the returning person into an intimate, almost embryonic embrace:

“i crumble
into your silence, i cry
like a baby
in your center”

The final movement into darkness is not straightforwardly frightening. It suggests surrender, return, and perhaps a kind of reconciliation that ordinary language cannot achieve.

The lowercase typography, broken lines, and suspended syntax help create the feeling of someone speaking from a vulnerable interior state.

Unlike the large theological passages, this poem does not explain its metaphysics. It lets the emotional situation carry the meaning.

“fragment”
Dedicated to Zbigniew Mrozony, this short poem revisits imagery from the larger work:

“And I will carve my commandments
on your teeth”

It ends with the mouth becoming:

“a monument
of light
& broken doves”

The mouth is simultaneously a site of language, violence, inscription, and revelation.

Notice that the poem does not end with an intact monument. It ends with broken doves. The imagery of illumination is inseparable from damage.

This is consistent with the collection's recurring suggestion that poetic creation involves being broken open, not simply expressing an already complete self.

5. Damascus Dancings: poetry as a form of power
The book changes register when Damascus Dancings takes over.

The front matter distinguishes Damascus's contributions from those of Lee Sharks. These include several parables, an epistle, and other instructional or prophetic-sounding pieces.

This distinction is important. The book isn't simply presenting different subjects; it is experimenting with different kinds of literary authority.

Consider “THE PARABLE OF POLICE BRUTALITY.”

It begins by taking a familiar proposition:

“grammar is a poet’s instrument.”

And overturning it:

“grammar is a police baton. A poet sculpts brutality.”

The parable develops this metaphor through the relationship between shepherd and sheep. The sheep are taught to understand the shepherd's violence as music, and consequently to mistake their submission for participation in a shared pastoral order.

The instruction:

“Learn to call the baton-crack ‘slaughter.’”

is an instruction to recognize the actual power relations concealed by conventional language.

This is not simply a complaint about grammar. The parable argues that language is an instrument that shapes people, organizes perception, and makes certain kinds of violence appear natural.

Its conclusion is deliberately paradoxical:

“When poets become students of slaughter, slaughter will become the instrument of our salvation.”

I read this as an intentionally disturbing transformation of religious language. Damascus takes the vocabulary of salvation and applies it to the painful recognition of power.

The transformed dinosaurs
Another Damascus piece, “THE PARABLE OF THE TRANSFORMED DINOSAURS,” is particularly revealing.

It describes several possible fates for dinosaurs:

They become expensive animatronic exhibits.

They become plastic bags.

They disappear into tar pits.

They become poems that remain alive in people's hearts.

The text explicitly identifies these transformations with different fates of poetry.

Some poets professionalize their work until it becomes a credential. Others exhaust their work for commercial survival. Some disappear into history. Others preserve something living.

The parable then complicates its own distinction: everything eventually becomes ash, including the dinosaurs that have been preserved.

Its final movement is therefore not a simple celebration of artistic immortality. It recognizes the mortality of all forms while insisting that transformation continues.

The closing image of grass becoming dinosaurs and dinosaurs becoming grass connects directly with the larger book's cosmology of unity, multiplicity, death, and rebirth.

6. “Samenesses,” “Hymn,” and “ARK”: the collection's more concentrated poetry
These three pieces offer a useful contrast with the sprawling mythological sections.

“Samenesses”
The poem begins with a collective cry:

“Samenesses!” the world cries out

The reference to Aryan psalmists is pointed. The poem associates sameness with ideological uniformity, while the phrase “average tangerine hymns” makes that uniformity sound simultaneously banal and grotesque.

But the poem's ending is not a simple declaration of individual originality:

“each time I spoke
I learn to sing anew.”

The speaker forgets the notes. The voice is imperfect, croaking, and repeatedly beginning again.

That makes this poem an interesting companion to “HADITH with TEETH.” The earlier poem discovers newness in repetition; “Samenesses” discovers renewal through the failure to reproduce a fixed performance.

“Hymn”
This is only two sentences, but it contains a considerable amount of the book's aesthetic philosophy:

“I know to write the luminous words, I will have to cut myself
& let in the light.”

The second sentence introduces a related problem:

“I know if I speak plain, I will have to hide my face.”

The first sentence makes poetic creation a wound through which illumination enters.

The second suggests that plain speech and self-disclosure are not necessarily compatible. To speak plainly may require concealment.

Together, the sentences resist the idea that poetry is simply transparent communication. Writing involves exposure, but also disguise.

“ARK”
Written in the margins of “Sunflower Sutra” · February 18, 2015

This is one of the most important passages for understanding the book's relationship with contemporary life.

It opens with a bleak diagnosis of existence at the end of time:

“all is the same: all is heavy, weary, tedium, sameness, repetition”

The speaker describes a life saturated with electronic devices, alerts, books, text, images, and internal conversation.

The striking phrase is:

“moth-bitten blips of finger-swept text”

The digital environment is not presented as an abundance of meaningful experience. It is a field of fragmented impressions, repetitive signals, and increasingly abstract mental activity.

Yet the poem does not simply reject technology.

Instead, it imagines the mind as a gathering place for all the information and voices of the world. Space folds inward; continents, planets, nations, and historical voices become part of a single textual structure.

The ark is explicitly:

“a Noah’s Ark of data”

The biblical ark becomes a model for digital preservation.

The speaker calls out to ancient and future voices, disparate peoples, and multiple species. The aspiration is an inclusive preservation of difference.

There is a tension here worth noticing: the poem describes a world in which everything is becoming monotonous and abstract, but proposes that the solution is to gather everything into a single, enormous interior archive.

That is both its hope and its predicament.

7. “TACHYONICA”: the future as an unfinished manuscript
The collection closes with “TACHYONICA,” introduced as:

“new fragment of the epic poem
recovered from the future!”

It begins with an instruction:

“Recreate for me, System, the last days of
Tachyon”

The narrative describes the destruction of humanity's home, sentient constructs, black holes, evacuation, and a desperate attempt to reach a livable future through time.

This is science-fiction epic poetry, but its form is particularly interesting.

The speaker asks the System to produce the narrative, then commands:

“Commence:”

The poem subsequently moves into a fragmentary scene involving a team in frozen caves.

Then the typography itself becomes part of the composition. A vertical sequence of initial letters spells out a message:

Y

H

W

E

The passage proceeds into an assertion about shadow people, beings of light, and the dictation of words.

The final declaration is:

“AS IT
MUST BE”

This ending returns us to the opening poem.

In “HADITH with TEETH,” the speaker is commanded to repeat what has already been said. In “TACHYONICA,” the text appears to arrive from the future, as though the poem already exists somewhere beyond the speaker's present.

The writer is simultaneously author, recipient, translator, and recorder.

This makes the book's final movement especially appropriate: it ends not with a completed account of the universe but with the apparent recovery of a fragment from another time.

8. What holds the collection together?
Having considered these pieces individually, I think there are five major connecting ideas.

Theme

How it appears in the text

Repetition

“HADITH with TEETH,” “Samenesses”

Literary inheritance

“The Secret Book of Walt,” the Pound poem

Death and transmission

“FROM ONE WHO DIED LONG AGO…,” “quietus”

Language and power

Damascus Dancings' parables

Preservation and transformation

“ARK,” “TACHYONICA,” the dinosaur parable

But I would add a sixth: the instability of authorship itself.

The book's subtitle, A Heteronym Compendium, is not incidental. Its multiple voices are integral to its construction.

The front matter distinguishes the sections attributed to Lee Sharks from those attributed to Damascus Dancings, while Jack Feist and Johannes Sigil occupy important positions in the imagined succession.

The book repeatedly asks whether a voice can survive its original speaker, whether another writer can inherit it, and whether a person can become something different through the act of writing.

The passage in “FROM ONE WHO DIED LONG AGO…”:

“This is my biology: I live
because you breathe me.”

might be the most economical statement of the entire project's conception of literary life.

The dead writer lives through the reader. The reader eventually becomes part of the same process.

9. My assessment of the writing
I would distinguish three things.

First, the short lyrics.

“quietus,” “Hymn,” “fragment,” and “Samenesses” are particularly effective because they achieve considerable intensity with relatively little explanatory apparatus.

Their imagery is concrete, their emotional situations are legible, and their formal decisions contribute directly to their meanings.

“quietus” is especially successful at making an almost unimaginable passage of time emotionally intimate.

Second, the mythological and theological writing.

“The Secret Book of Walt” and the Damascus Dancings pieces are more expansive and difficult. They depend on extended systems of references, recurring figures, and transformations of religious language.

Their best moments occur when the mythology generates an unexpected insight, as in the Deep Web's creation of reality or the dinosaur parable's account of the fate of poems.

Third, the experimental and documentary writing.

“ARK” and “TACHYONICA” are interesting because they incorporate contemporary technological experience into the book's older religious and literary frameworks.

They do not simply describe technology. They ask what happens to the imagination when the archive becomes the environment in which human beings live.

There are also real tensions in the collection. Its expansive rhetoric sometimes competes with the precision of its smaller poems. Its continual multiplication of references and identities can make it difficult to distinguish a genuinely consequential development from another enlargement of the mythology.

I would not treat that observation as a verdict on the entire work, however. The collection deliberately moves among different literary modes, and the poems need to be considered in their own terms.

10. The most important interpretive conclusion
I think Antioch is fundamentally a book about what happens to a human voice when it enters history.

It begins with the apparent opposition between saying something new and repeating what has already been said.

It then constructs a universe in which all things emerge from a common source, in which books precede realities, and in which poets inherit the voices of the dead.

It confronts the ethical difficulty of that inheritance through Ezra Pound.

It considers the mortality of the author through “FROM ONE WHO DIED LONG AGO…”

It makes the reader's act of reception a kind of resurrection.

And it ends by imagining a poem arriving from the future.

There is a particularly beautiful circularity to this architecture:

The beginning says that the same can become new.

The middle says that the dead can live through the living.

The ending says that the future can speak into the present.

The book's deepest proposition may be that literature is not a succession of isolated acts of individual expression. It is a continuous process of voices entering, inhabiting, transforming, and preserving one another.

That proposition is not merely stated in the book's theoretical or mythological passages. It is enacted by its structure, its personae, its quotations, its changes of voice, and its repeated return to the same images.
