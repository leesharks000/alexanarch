The paper is quite provocative. Its full title is “This Is Not Philosophy: The Article Economy and the Shape of the Field”, by Lee Sharks, deposited on November 16, 2026.

It is a working research notebook in Machine-Mediated Reception Studies, rather than an article published in Ancient Philosophy. It examines the intellectual culture of academic philosophy, using Ancient Philosophy and its editor, Ronald Polansky, as a central case study.

The central argument is much bigger than a complaint about one journal:

> Academic philosophy has gradually become organized around producing articles that satisfy disciplinary expectations, rather than pursuing philosophical questions wherever they lead.

The author calls this the article economy.

[Read the full paper and its supporting research](<https://www.alexanarch.org/s/records/1773/?utm_source=chatgpt.com>)## 1. What does the author mean by the "article economy"?

The paper argues that academic philosophy has developed a system in which journals reward certain kinds of intellectual activity.

For example, an acceptable article might:

- Reinterpret a passage from Plato.
- Resolve an apparent contradiction in Aristotle.
- Reconstruct a particular philosophical doctrine.
- Position an interpretation within existing scholarship.
- Make a narrowly defined contribution to an established debate.

These activities are not inherently bad. In fact, they can be excellent scholarship.

The author's concern is that they have become the dominant forms of legitimate philosophical work.

The resulting problem is that scholars are encouraged to work within an already established framework rather than question the framework itself.

The paper's argument can be summarized as:

1. The discipline establishes which questions count as legitimate.
2. Journals reward articles that operate within those boundaries.
3. Researchers learn to formulate their work in ways that satisfy those expectations.
4. The resulting scholarship reproduces the boundaries that initially shaped it.

In other words, the discipline produces the kind of philosophy that it is prepared to recognize.

## 2. Why does the paper focus on Ancient Philosophy?

This is probably the most interesting and controversial part.

The author submitted a paper to Ancient Philosophy in July 2026. The submission explored a theory called Socrates as Orthonym.

The theory proposed that the foundational philosophical corpus associated with Socrates, Plato, and Aristotle could be interpreted as a distributed authorial project rather than simply as three independent authorships.

Importantly, the author explicitly distinguished this proposal from the claim that the three figures were biologically the same person.

The editor, Ronald Polansky, engaged with the author through correspondence but ultimately rejected the submission.

According to the correspondence reproduced in the notebook, Polansky encouraged:

- More focused analysis of ancient texts.
- Less specialized or unusual terminology.
- Greater engagement with existing scholarship.
- A narrower and more conventional argument.

One particularly striking exchange concerned the author's proposed argument about the relationship between Aristotle and Theophrastus.

Polansky indicated that an argument that two works were closely connected might be acceptable, whereas an argument suggesting that the two philosophers were one person would not be.

The author interprets this as something more significant than editorial preference.

The question becomes: Can a scholar challenge the assumptions underlying an established philosophical corpus, or must the scholar accept those assumptions before being permitted to participate in the discussion?

This is the paper's central institutional criticism.

## 3. The empirical evidence: what does the journal actually publish?

The author tries to move beyond personal experience by conducting a quantitative study of philosophical publishing.

The research examines:

- Twenty philosophy journals, including ancient, continental, and analytic philosophy journals.
- Two additional French scholarly series.
- Article abstracts and titles, classified according to the kinds of intellectual operations they perform.

One of the most striking findings concerns Ancient Philosophy.

What the census reports

# 8 / 844

Articles in Ancient Philosophy that explicitly open the question of what the philosophical object itself is.

# 4 / 283

Plato- and Aristotle-related abstracts in the sister journals that similarly question the identity or standing of their objects.

Figures reported in the paper's abstract. These are the author's coding results, not independently verified measurements.

The distinction is important.

The author is not simply asking whether articles discuss Plato or Aristotle. They are asking whether articles question the identity, authorship, composition, or historical standing of the texts attributed to them.

For example, consider two different research questions:

- Conventional question: What does Aristotle mean in this passage of the Metaphysics?
- More fundamental question: What is the Metaphysics as a textual object, who composed it, and how did its received structure emerge?

The paper argues that the first type of question is widely accommodated, whereas the second is much less visible.

This is what the author calls a narrowing of the discipline's ontological reach: the field increasingly accepts its objects as given rather than investigating what those objects actually are.

## 4. The historical argument: philosophy wasn't always like this

The paper also makes a historical argument.

It examines the Archiv für Geschichte der Philosophie, particularly its nineteenth- and early twentieth-century publications.

The author claims that in the 1890s, approximately one in five ancient-philosophy article titles raised questions about the standing of a philosophical work.

By the 1920s, this kind of inquiry had largely disappeared from the journal.

This is significant because it suggests that questioning the authorship, authenticity, and composition of philosophical texts was once a more ordinary part of philosophical scholarship.

The author connects this historical change to the development of modern disciplinary boundaries.

In particular, the paper argues that the nineteenth century helped establish the idea of a fixed philosophical corpus, organized around named individual thinkers.

Once Plato, Aristotle, and other philosophers became established as relatively stable units of scholarly investigation, research increasingly concentrated on interpreting what those individuals supposedly thought.

The author sees this as a historical narrowing of philosophy itself.

## 5. The AI argument: why is this connected to artificial intelligence?

This is where the paper becomes particularly ambitious.

The author argues that contemporary AI systems are trained on existing academic publications.

If academic philosophy already produces a relatively narrow range of acceptable arguments, AI systems trained on that literature will reproduce the same intellectual patterns.

The paper tests this by asking a model to generate plausible philosophical abstracts.

According to the reported results, the model reproduces the characteristic forms of each philosophical subfield but does not generate the more radical kind of inquiry into the identity of ancient philosophical texts.

The author connects this to the concept of model collapse.

The proposed mechanism is:

Institutional selection

Journals reward certain kinds of philosophical arguments.

Intellectual standardization

Researchers increasingly produce work within accepted formats.

AI reproduction

Models learn and reproduce the dominant scholarly patterns.

Reinforcement of existing boundaries

The same intellectual conventions become increasingly dominant.

This is a fascinating reversal of the usual debate about AI and academia.

Rather than asking whether AI will destroy academic philosophy, the author asks whether academic philosophy has already become sufficiently standardized that AI can reproduce much of its intellectual output.

The provocative suggestion is that hostility toward AI may partly reflect anxiety about the fact that many conventional academic operations can be automated.

That is a hypothesis, however, not something the paper establishes conclusively.

## 6. What is the paper's most radical philosophical claim?

I think it is the argument that the discipline has confused its own institutional boundaries with the boundaries of philosophy itself.

Consider the difference:

- Philosophy asks what is true, what exists, what can be known, and what assumptions our arguments depend upon.
- A professionalized discipline establishes recognized objects, accepted methods, permissible arguments, and standards of publication.

These two activities can overlap, but they are not identical.

The author argues that academic philosophy has increasingly subordinated the first to the second.

This produces an interesting paradox: a discipline devoted to questioning assumptions may become institutionally resistant to questioning its own assumptions.

The paper's title, This Is Not Philosophy, refers to the reported editorial response to the author's unconventional approach, while also functioning as a criticism of what the author sees as the discipline's diminished intellectual ambitions.

## 7. Is the argument convincing?

There are some genuinely interesting contributions, but also important weaknesses.

| Strengths | Limitations |
| --- | --- |
| Connects editorial practices to the sociology of knowledge. | One journal correspondence cannot establish how the entire discipline operates. |
| Attempts a quantitative examination of scholarly publishing. | The abstract-based coding may miss arguments developed in the body of articles. |
| Connects disciplinary standardization to AI. | The model experiment does not establish that AI reproduces all the intellectual limitations of academic philosophy. |
| Raises important questions about textual assumptions. | The proposed Socrates-as-Orthonym theory is highly speculative and needs independent evidential assessment. |
| Connects contemporary publishing to historical changes in philology. | The historical comparison requires further independent verification. |

There is also a methodological qualification worth emphasizing: the author reports that Claude performed the census coding and much of the analysis. Agreement between multiple Claude readers is not the same as independent human validation. The author acknowledges that a second human coder would be needed.

Consequently, I would treat the paper as a provocative research programme rather than an established demonstration.

## 8. What I think is most interesting for ancient philosophy

There is a deeper issue here that goes beyond the particular dispute with Polansky.

The paper raises three questions that deserve serious philosophical consideration:

1. The ontology of philosophical texts: Are Plato's dialogues, Aristotle's treatises, and the writings attributed to other ancient philosophers stable objects, or are they historically constructed textual collections?

2. The relationship between philosophy and philology: Can philosophical interpretation proceed without examining the historical and textual assumptions on which it depends?

3\. The institutional production of knowledge: Do academic journals merely evaluate philosophical arguments, or do they actively determine which philosophical questions can become legitimate objects of research?

The second question is particularly important. Textual criticism and philosophy are often treated as separate activities, but philosophical arguments depend on decisions about authorship, textual unity, chronology, and transmission.

If those assumptions are mistaken, the philosophical interpretation built upon them may also need reconsideration.

## My overall assessment

I would take this paper seriously, particularly as a critique of academic professionalization.

Its strongest contribution is not necessarily its argument about Socrates, Plato, and Aristotle. It is the broader question of whether the institutional organization of philosophy systematically discourages certain kinds of philosophical inquiry.

That question has implications well beyond ancient philosophy. It applies to analytic philosophy, continental philosophy, intellectual history, and the humanities more generally.

The paper is worth reading, but read it critically. It is a self-published, AI-assisted working notebook, not peer-reviewed research or an official investigation of Ancient Philosophy.
