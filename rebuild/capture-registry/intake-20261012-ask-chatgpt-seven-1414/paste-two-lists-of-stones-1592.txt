The central argument of Lee Sharks’s “Two Lists of Stones: De lapidibus and the Apocalypse — a groundwork note on relations, withholding readings (v0.1)” is that Theophrastus’s De lapidibus and the Apocalypse of John exhibit a series of specific, verifiable correspondences in their language and treatment of stones, seals, and colour. Sharks argues that these correspondences deserve close reading, but that establishing them is not the same as explaining them. The essay’s method is deliberately restrained: it lays out the evidence without claiming direct influence, proposing a source, or imposing an allegory.

The key to the argument is the distinction Sharks draws between establishing relations and interpreting their meaning. The note is a groundwork for subsequent interpretation, not a completed interpretation itself.

1. The two books organize stones according to different principles
Sharks begins by comparing two catalogues that, despite their different purposes and historical contexts, share several mineral names and descriptions.

In Theophrastus’s De lapidibus, stones are organized primarily by their material properties and δύναμις — their power or capacity to do something. Some stones melt, others resist fire; some colour water, petrify objects, attract things, or test gold and silver.

In Revelation 21:19–20, stones are organized spatially. Twelve precious stones occupy the twelve foundations of the wall of the New Jerusalem, from jasper at the first foundation to amethyst at the twelfth.

The contrast matters. Theophrastus asks what a stone does and how it behaves; John specifies where a stone belongs in the architecture of a visionary city.

Sharks's point is not that the lists are identical or serve the same function. It is that they share enough of their vocabulary and descriptive material to make the relationship between their two modes of cataloguing worth investigating. Both treat stones as elements through which a larger account of the world can be organized.

The essay supports this argument by comparing individual entries, including emerald (σμάραγδος), sard (σάρδιον), sardonyx (σαρδόνυξ), amethyst (ἀμέθυστος), hyacinth (ὑάκινθος), and crystal (κρύσταλλος). Their roles differ, but their recurrence supplies the evidence for comparison.

2. The word σφραγίς: a seal made from stone versus a seal imposed on a text
The essay’s most developed comparison concerns the Greek word σφραγίς, associated with a seal or signet.

In De lapidibus, Theophrastus describes a red, unburnable stone called ἄνθραξ, from which small seals are carved. He also mentions a variety found near Miletus that is angular and contains hexagonal forms.

In Revelation, sealing works differently. The seal is something placed upon or applied to something else:

In Revelation 5:1, a book or scroll is sealed.

In Revelation 7:3–4, people are sealed and then counted.

In Revelation 10:4, John is forbidden to write down what the voices of the seven thunders have said.

In Revelation 22:10, by contrast, John is instructed not to seal the book’s prophetic words.

Sharks emphasizes the opposition between the two operations. In Theophrastus, a seal is an object manufactured from a stone. In Revelation, sealing is an action performed upon a book, people, or speech.

The ending of Revelation makes this comparison especially suggestive. The command not to seal the prophecy reverses the earlier restrictions on access and inscription. Sharks also points to Revelation 22:18, where the warning against adding to the book's words offers a further means of guarding the text.

The argument here is that the same vocabulary participates in different material and textual practices. A stone can produce a seal; a seal can restrict access to revelation; and the final instruction can prohibit sealing altogether. Sharks establishes this sequence without declaring that the stone is a symbol for the book or that the two texts share a single underlying meaning.

3. κόκκινον: the same crimson at two ends of a material chain
The next correspondence concerns κόκκινον, the Greek word for crimson or scarlet.

Sharks juxtaposes two unusually concrete uses of this colour:

In Theophrastus, crimson is associated with a pigment-making process near Ephesus. The text describes material that is ground, washed, and separated into settled layers, and credits a named inventor from ninety years earlier.

In Revelation, crimson appears in the imagery of the woman and the beast and in the merchants’ catalogue of luxury goods in Revelation 18:12.

The distinction is between a colour as something produced and a colour as something worn, displayed, and traded. One text describes the material and craft through which the pigment comes into being; the other situates crimson within apocalyptic imagery and commercial exchange.

This is more than a shared colour word in the abstract. The essay draws attention to the word's placement in two accounts of material life: a technical description of pigment production and a vision in which colour is entangled with power, luxury, and the circulation of goods.

The argument is that the correspondence opens a material question as well as a literary one. What connects the production of a colour to its appearance in a vision of wealth and catastrophe? Sharks does not answer that question. Instead, the juxtaposition makes the question available for subsequent interpretation.

4. Hexagons and the measured city: mineral form versus architectural geometry
The note then places the hexagonal forms in Theophrastus’s Miletian stone alongside the measured, square geometry of the New Jerusalem in Revelation.

The relevant details are quite specific:

The stone near Miletus is unburnable, angular, and contains ἑξάγωνα — hexagons.

Revelation 21 describes the city and its dimensions, including the city's equal length, breadth, and height in 21:16. The essay characterizes the relevant geometry as a tetragon, or four-sided figure.

The contrast is between geometric form embedded in a natural material and geometry imposed upon or realized in a constructed, measured city.

The significance of this comparison is methodological. Sharks is not simply collecting instances of stones in ancient texts. The essay is testing whether a particular formal feature — geometry — can be traced across two different descriptive settings: mineralogical observation and apocalyptic architecture.

The correspondence does not establish that Revelation's city derives its geometry from Theophrastus's mineral description. Rather, it identifies a potentially meaningful relation whose interpretation would require further argument.

5. The white ψῆφος: public testing versus a private name
Another important comparison concerns the white ψῆφος, or pebble, in Revelation 2:17. The recipient is promised a white pebble bearing a new name, known only to the person who receives it.

Sharks sets this against the stones in Theophrastus, whose properties can be made public through repeatable tests. If a stone colours water, attracts another material, or tests gold, its capacity can be demonstrated and checked. Its knowledge is available to observers who can repeat the procedure.

The white pebble presents a different epistemic situation. Its inscription bears a name that is not generally accessible. The name is personal and withheld from everyone except its recipient.

The essay condenses this contrast into two possibilities: the stone as “an instrument, or an address.”

This is a crucial distinction in the note's argument:

Instrument: A stone is known through what it does, through its material properties and the tests that establish them.

Address: A stone bears a name directed to a particular recipient, whose significance is not publicly verifiable in the same way.

The contrast is not simply between science and religion. It concerns two different relationships between material objects and knowledge: one reproducible and public, the other personal and restricted.

The essay leaves open how these forms of knowing might relate. But the juxtaposition broadens its subject beyond shared mineral names: stones can mediate not only descriptions of the world but also relations between persons, names, and knowledge.

6. The argument against premature allegory
The previous comparisons might tempt a reader to construct a single interpretive system. Perhaps the stones encode a common cosmology; perhaps Theophrastus supplies a source for John's imagery; perhaps the shared vocabulary implies a historical connection.

Sharks explicitly declines to make these moves.

The note distinguishes between establishing a correspondence and establishing what that correspondence means. Its stated practice is to record the relevant words, locations, and textual details, then stop before offering a reading.

That restraint has three consequences.

Resemblance is not proof of dependence. The fact that two texts share vocabulary does not, by itself, demonstrate that one borrowed from the other.

Correspondence is not an allegorical key. A reader cannot simply assign each stone a symbolic meaning and treat the resulting scheme as established by the evidence.

Interpretation must remain accountable to the particulars. Any future reading must explain the actual differences between the texts, not merely their similarities.

This is why the title calls the work a groundwork note. The term describes a particular scholarly task: establish the relations that an interpretation will have to account for, without pretending those relations already determine a unique interpretation.

The essay's restraint is therefore substantive, not merely stylistic. It is an argument about how literary and historical claims should be made.

7. Exodus and Ezekiel: the unresolved question of earlier sources
Sharks also identifies Exodus 28 and Ezekiel 28 as older relatives of John's stone imagery, but deliberately leaves those connections untraced.

These biblical passages matter because they offer further contexts for understanding precious stones and their arrangement. Tracing their relation to Revelation could help establish a more extensive history of the imagery.

But Sharks treats that genealogical work as something that would itself constitute a reading. The note therefore does not use the earlier passages to settle the meaning of Revelation's stones or to explain away the parallels with Theophrastus.

This decision reinforces the essay's larger principle: the presence of a plausible source or parallel does not eliminate the need to establish precisely what is transmitted, transformed, or shared.

The most important qualification is that this is not an essay claiming to have solved the relationship between Theophrastus and Revelation. Its declared achievement is narrower: to identify a set of textual correspondences precise enough to constrain future interpretations.

That distinction also defines its limitation. A correspondence table can demonstrate that particular words and descriptions occur in both texts, but it cannot by itself establish historical transmission, authorial intention, or a unified symbolic system. Those would require additional evidence and argument.

The note's strongest contribution is thus its combination of philological specificity and interpretive caution. It insists that shared words, material processes, geometries, and practices of inscription deserve to be read together, while refusing to confuse the discovery of a relation with the discovery of its meaning.

In short: Sharks argues that the relation between mineralogy and apocalypse should first be established at the level of the text — word by word, object by object, operation by operation — before anyone claims to know what that relation signifies.
