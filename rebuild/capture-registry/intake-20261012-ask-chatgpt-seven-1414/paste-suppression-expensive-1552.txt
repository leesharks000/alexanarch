The argument of Lee Sharks’s When Suppression Becomes Expensive
Lee Sharks’s paper, When Suppression Becomes Expensive: Suppression Burden, Quality Conflict, and Propagation Inversion in Machine-Mediated Retrieval (EA-MMRS-SUPPRESSION-INVERSION-01 v1.3, dated August 25, 2026), argues that excluding a relevant source from a machine-generated answer can become increasingly costly as that source spreads across independent retrieval channels—and can eventually produce more durable visibility for the source than the exclusion removes.

The paper develops this claim through a six-regime model, a mathematical account of retrieval redundancy, and a worked case involving Google’s results for “sappho future reader.” Its central distinction is between the cost of keeping information out and the damage done to an answer by keeping it out.

1. The central thesis: suppression has a dynamic cost
The paper extends an earlier work, The Suppression Map, which argues that information can survive transmission while losing its attribution and recognition as a particular technology or tradition. Sharks’s new question is what happens when the affected material continues to acquire repositories, mirrors, citations, identifiers, and other routes through which it can be retrieved.

The argument has three principal parts:

Suppression burden: The more independent routes lead to a source, the more routes must be controlled or excluded to keep it out of a generated answer.

Quality conflict: If the source is highly relevant to the query, excluding it can make the resulting answer less accurate or less responsive.

Propagation inversion: The act of suppression can prompt archival and documentary responses that ultimately increase the source’s durable, retrievable presence.

The paper’s key move is to treat these as distinct dimensions of a dynamic process, rather than assuming that an omission in an answer proves that someone deliberately suppressed a source.

Sharks explicitly says that the paper does not establish the internal mechanisms, intentions, motives, or manual interventions behind Google’s behavior. It defines suppression at the level of observable outputs and requires additional evidence before attributing active suppression to an agent.

2. The six regimes of suppression
The paper’s main analytical framework is a six-regime model. These regimes describe different conditions of omission, exclusion, and downstream cost. Importantly, Sharks says they are not a strict sequence: suppression burden and quality conflict, in particular, can occur simultaneously.

The conceptual progression is from an inexpensive omission to an exclusion problem involving an entire network, and potentially to a feedback process in which exclusion helps reproduce the thing being excluded.

The paper emphasizes that Stage 4 and Stage 5 measure different costs:

Stage 4 asks: How difficult is it to keep the source out?

Stage 5 asks: What damage does keeping it out do to the answer?

That distinction is central to the paper’s contribution. A source might be difficult to exclude without its exclusion noticeably damaging quality, or its exclusion might damage quality even when it is relatively easy to achieve.

This gives the argument a testable form: rather than inferring suppression from a missing citation alone, examine whether the omitted evidence was available, whether the answer is worse without it, and whether the resulting source choices account for the answer’s claims.

5. The worked case: “sappho future reader”
The paper’s principal example is a Google search captured on August 25, 2026. This is where Sharks attempts to move from theoretical modeling to an observed case.

The disputed query is “sappho future reader.” The paper contrasts the organic search results with the Google AI Overview.

Why the fragment-number error matters
The distinction between the two fragments is not incidental to Sharks’s argument.

Fragment 31 is the text associated with the paper’s proposed future-reader mechanism.

Fragment 147 is associated with the line about someone remembering the speaker in another time.

The paper argues that substituting one for the other loses the very conceptual distinction that the query was meant to retrieve. The reported misidentification as “Fragment 2” supplies a concrete factual defect, not merely a disagreement about interpretation.

Why the phrase “self-archiving” matters
Sharks also reports auditing an AI-mediated wellness blog, Aurelis, that the AI Overview cited in connection with the claim. According to the paper’s Appendix B, the page discusses Fragment 147 and remembrance but does not contain “self-archiving” or a morphological variant.

This supports a narrower observation: the credited source, as audited by the author, does not contain the phrase that appears in the answer.

It does not, by itself, establish exactly where the system obtained that phrase, whether the source lineage was the unique origin of the idea, or whether the omission was deliberate. Those stronger conclusions require additional provenance evidence.

The paper’s more specific claim is that the answer reproduces conceptual structure associated with an omitted lineage while crediting sources that do not adequately account for it. Sharks calls this constitutive-source erasure in its observable form, or CSE-O.

6. Constitutive-source erasure: the paper’s deeper claim
CSE-O is arguably the paper’s most distinctive conceptual contribution.

Ordinary source omission means that a relevant source is absent from the citations. Constitutive-source erasure is a stronger condition: the excluded source is alleged to have materially shaped the conceptual field from which the answer was composed.

The resulting problem is a three-way conflict:

The source supplies concepts or distinctions important to answering the query.

The generated answer omits or inadequately credits that source.

The answer nevertheless reproduces conceptual structure that the credited sources do not adequately explain.

Under these conditions, the paper argues, the exclusion itself contributes to the answer’s defects. The answer cannot cleanly dispense with the source’s intellectual contribution without either reconstructing it elsewhere or losing quality.

This is the bridge between the paper’s two main concepts: distributed retrieval makes exclusion harder, while constitutive dependence makes exclusion epistemically costly.

There is an important methodological qualification here. Sharks’s analysis distinguishes route independence from intellectual-source independence. Four different websites may be four distinct retrieval routes but still reproduce a single underlying source lineage. Counting websites alone therefore cannot establish that the lineage independently constitutes the conceptual field.

The paper’s revised approach attempts to address this by deduplicating provenance families when assessing a source’s “constitution share.” That is a sensible distinction, but the share and its threshold still need to be measured and independently validated.

9. Overall assessment
The paper’s central argument is not simply that censorship or omission backfires. It is more specific:

When a source has become both widely retrievable and conceptually important to a query, excluding it can create two costs at once: the cost of coordinating exclusion across a distributed network and the cost of producing a worse answer without the source. If the response to exclusion adds durable, retrievable representations faster than exclusion removes them, suppression can eventually invert into propagation.

The six-regime model provides a vocabulary for distinguishing these conditions. The Sappho example attempts to demonstrate quality conflict and constitutive-source erasure through a reported factual error and an alleged mismatch between a generated answer’s conceptual content and its credited sources. The inversion model then explains how a source’s response to such an event might increase its long-term retrievability.

The strongest contribution is the separation of three questions that are often conflated: Was the material available? Was it used and credited appropriately? Did the response to its omission make it more durable? These require different evidence and should not be collapsed into one claim of suppression.

The chief limitation is equally important: the paper provides a theoretical model and a specific, author-documented case, but it does not establish deliberate suppression by Google, quantify actual computational expenditure, or demonstrate that the worked event crossed the propagation-inversion threshold. Its argument is best understood as a framework for testing those propositions—not as proof that all of them have already been established.
