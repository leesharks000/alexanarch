Lee Sharks’s Catalogues and Wills: A Groundwork Note on the Peripatetic Transmission Texts — Relations Established, Readings Withheld is an exercise in textual evidence management. Its central argument is methodological: before explaining how Aristotle’s and Theophrastus’s books were transmitted, one must distinguish what the surviving texts actually state from what later accounts lead us to believe happened.

The note, dated September 6, 2026, compares four wills preserved by Diogenes Laertius, two catalogues of philosophical works, Strabo’s account of the books’ transmission, Plutarch’s account of Andronicus of Rhodes, and related textual evidence. It repeatedly separates observable textual relations from historical inferences that the evidence does not yet settle.

An important qualification: Sharks explicitly calls this a groundwork note, not a completed historical interpretation. The author’s claims concern what can be established from the texts, how their statements relate, and where the evidence remains insufficient. The arguments below therefore distinguish the note’s positive findings from the conclusions it deliberately withholds.

1. The method: establish relations before offering interpretations
Sharks’s first argument concerns how the evidence should be handled.

The note’s scope statement specifies a limited set of operations: counting, locating passages, recording co-occurrences, and comparing verbatim clauses. It treats Diogenes Laertius, Strabo, and Plutarch as textual witnesses whose statements must be examined against one another, rather than as transparent windows onto historical events.

The author also distinguishes a will as a preserved testamentary text from an independently surviving contemporary object. For example, when the note says that Theophrastus’s will assigns his books to Neleus, it means that the will preserved in Diogenes Laertius says so; the document does not independently verify the historical execution of the bequest.

This method has two consequences:

A claim made by one source cannot automatically be attributed to another source that is silent about it.

A numerical or verbal correspondence does not, by itself, establish a causal explanation.

The note’s subtitle, “Relations Established, Readings Withheld,” expresses this distinction. Sharks wants subsequent interpretations to begin with a record of the evidence that they must explain, rather than with a familiar historical narrative that determines in advance what the evidence is supposed to mean.

This is not a claim that interpretation is impossible. It is a claim that interpretation should come after the evidence has been properly delimited.

2. The four wills describe different arrangements for books and the school
One of the note’s strongest arguments comes from placing the four wills side by side rather than treating them as interchangeable evidence for institutional succession.

Sharks identifies a striking asymmetry: Aristotle’s will says nothing about books or the succession of his school, whereas the later wills make explicit provisions for books and, in different ways, the school or its premises.

The note establishes this by examining the Greek text of each will in Diogenes Laertius, Book V. The absence in Aristotle’s will is presented as a textual observation: Sharks reports no occurrence of the βιβλί- word stem in that will, and no disposition naming a school or successor. Instead, the will addresses guardianship, marriage, property, manumission, images, and burial arrangements.

The point is not merely that these wills contain different bequests. Sharks identifies three different ways of classifying the books:

All books.

All books except the testator’s own writings.

Read books versus unpublished books.

These categories should not be collapsed into one general idea of a library being handed down. They distinguish different kinds of property, different recipients, and different instructions.

Theophrastus’s books and the Peripatos are treated differently
The most consequential example is Theophrastus’s will. Its book clause is immediately followed by a separate disposition of the garden, the peripatos (the covered walk associated with the school), and the houses adjoining the garden.

The premises are assigned to a group of named friends and to those wishing to study and philosophize there together. The text prohibits alienation and private appropriation, describing the arrangement as a common possession, like a sanctuary.

The books, however, are assigned to Neleus individually.

Sharks stresses the juxtaposition of these clauses: the will places the books under an individual bequest and the premises under a collective regime. The commons rule governing the premises is not explicitly extended to the books.

That difference is important because it prevents an easy equation between the transmission of the library and the continuity of the school. Neleus receives the books and is also one of the ten people named in connection with the premises; Strato, the later scholarch, is another. But the will does not explicitly appoint a successor to head the school.

The argument is that custody of books, ownership of premises, and succession to philosophical leadership are distinct questions in the documentary evidence. The text establishes the distinction without explaining why the testator arranged things this way.

The argument proceeds through three observations.

A. Theophrastus’s catalogue has a distinctive internal structure
Sharks finds that the Theophrastan list begins with a short unordered section, proceeds through the alphabet once, restarts and proceeds through it a second time, and then ends with a tail of roughly thirty titles that is not alphabetically ordered.

The author also identifies nine internal doublets: titles that occur twice in the list.

These features are observable in the catalogue as transmitted. Sharks suggests that the two alphabetical runs may reflect distinct source lists joined together, but explicitly classifies that explanation as an inference. The surviving sequence does not identify the sources or establish how the list was assembled.

This distinction matters: the author argues that the catalogue has a structure requiring explanation, not that its structure already proves a particular history of compilation.

B. The two catalogues share twelve identical titles
Sharks lists twelve titles appearing letter for letter in both catalogues, including On Kingship (Περὶ βασιλείας), On Animals (Περὶ ζῴων), On Nature (Περὶ φύσεως), Protrepticus (Προτρεπτικός), and Eroticus (Ἐρωτικός).

But identical titles do not establish that the entries refer to identical physical books or even to the same work in every case.

Sharks leaves open three possibilities: two distinct works with the same title, a single work listed twice, or one library represented under two headings. The catalogue comparison establishes the overlap; it does not resolve the identity of the items.

C. One entry preserves alternative attribution in its own wording
The particularly revealing entry is:

Ὑπομνημάτων Ἀριστοτελικῶν ἢ Θεοφραστείων

The phrase identifies notes or commentaries as Aristotelian or Theophrastan. Sharks emphasizes the disjunctive ἢ (“or”), because the catalogue itself retains an alternative attribution.

The author does not decide which attribution is correct, nor infer a specific process by which the uncertainty arose. Instead, the entry provides direct evidence that the transmitted catalogue does not assign every item to a single, unambiguous author.

Taken together, these findings support a restrained conclusion: the catalogues contain overlaps, repetitions, and uncertain attributions that complicate any attempt to treat them as transparent lists of distinct, securely identified works.

4. Strabo supplies a transmission narrative that Aristotle’s will does not corroborate
This is the note’s central historical problem.

In Geography XIII.1.54, Strabo describes a chain of custody for Aristotle’s and Theophrastus’s books. Sharks highlights Strabo’s repeated use of the verb παραδίδωμι (paradidōmi, to hand over or transmit):

Aristotle hands his library to Theophrastus.

Theophrastus hands the books to Neleus.

Neleus takes them to Scepsis and passes them to his successors.

Strabo then describes the books being kept away, buried to protect them from collection by the Attalid kings, damaged by damp and worms, sold to Apellicon of Teos, taken to Rome by Sulla, handled by the grammarian Tyrannion, and copied in ways Strabo considers defective.

Sharks does not simply reject this account. Instead, the note compares each link in Strabo’s narrative with the relevant testamentary evidence.

Historical claim in Strabo

Comparison with the wills

Aristotle handed his library to Theophrastus.

Aristotle’s will mentions no books or library.

Aristotle left his school to Theophrastus.

Aristotle’s will does not name the school or a successor.

Theophrastus’s library included Aristotle’s books.

Theophrastus’s will bequeaths “all the books” to Neleus without specifying their authorship.

Theophrastus left the books to Neleus.

This agrees with the explicit bequest in Theophrastus’s will.

The asymmetry is the point. Strabo’s account agrees with the will about the bequest to Neleus, but supplies an earlier link between Aristotle and Theophrastus that the will does not attest.

Sharks’s argument is that agreement at one point in a transmission narrative does not independently verify every preceding link.

This does not prove that Strabo is wrong. Aristotle’s will might simply omit a transaction that occurred elsewhere or at another time. The note’s narrower claim is that the will cannot be cited as direct evidence for Strabo’s account of Aristotle’s library passing to Theophrastus.

The same caution applies to Strabo’s claim that Aristotle was the first person known to have assembled books and that he instructed the kings of Egypt in organizing a library. Those claims occur in Strabo’s narrative; they are not statements established by Aristotle’s will.

The problem of Andronicus
Sharks also compares Strabo’s account with Plutarch’s Sulla 26. Plutarch names Andronicus of Rhodes and describes his action as:

ἀναγράψαι τοὺς νῦν φερομένους πίνακας

That is, drawing up the catalogues then in circulation.

The note observes that Andronicus is named in Plutarch’s account but not in Strabo’s passage. This is significant because the two authors describe related aspects of the books’ transmission and subsequent circulation, yet they do not tell the same story in the same terms.

Sharks does not reconcile the accounts or determine exactly what Andronicus did. The author establishes the difference between them and leaves its historical implications open.

On that basis, the author calculates that the digitized Aristotle represents approximately 35% of the estimated catalogue total and the digitized Theophrastus approximately 12%.

But the note explicitly refuses to call these figures measurements of lost works.

Why?

The numbers are produced by two different operations:

The ancient catalogue totals record the line counts attributed to lists of works.

The modern totals count words in the surviving works included in particular digital editions.

These measurements have different units, scopes, and textual histories. The word totals also depend on what works and editions are included. Sharks notes, for example, that the broader Bekker corpus of Aristotle is closer to 1.05–1.1 million words, while the seven digitized works counted for Theophrastus omit some material.

The comparison therefore cannot establish how much text has been lost. It establishes a numerical relationship between the transmitted catalogues and the particular surviving corpora counted.

The author calls these “two acts”: cataloguing and counting what survives. Their difference is real, but its historical meaning is not self-evident.

This is an especially clear example of the note’s method. Rather than suppressing the striking numerical discrepancy, Sharks makes it visible while refusing to turn it into a conclusion the data cannot support.

6. The note’s unanswered questions are part of its argument
Sharks concludes by identifying eleven underdetermined areas. These are not incidental gaps appended to an otherwise complete historical account. They define the limits of what the preceding evidence establishes.

The most consequential include:

The transfer from Aristotle to Theophrastus: How did it happen, given that Aristotle’s will is silent and Strabo supplies the transmission claim?

The different bequest regimes: Why were the books assigned to one individual while the school premises were placed under a collective arrangement?

The books excluded by Strato: What happened to the writings Strato distinguishes from the books he bequeaths to Lycon?

The twelve overlapping catalogue titles: Do they refer to identical works, separate works with the same titles, or repeated entries?

The two alphabetical sequences: Were they copied from distinct lists, and how did the unordered tail enter the catalogue?

The competing accounts of transmission: How does Strabo’s account compare with the alternative involving Ptolemy preserved in Athenaeus?

The relationship between Strabo and Plutarch: What precisely was Andronicus’s contribution to the catalogues?

The books available to the later Peripatetics: How should Strabo’s statement that the school possessed few books, mostly exoteric ones, be compared with the predominantly surviving treatise corpus?

The names Erastus and Coriscus: Does their appearance in Strabo and Plato’s Epistle VI establish any relationship beyond the textual co-occurrence?

The final question is methodological as much as historical: what further evidence would be needed to move from a recorded correspondence to a defensible interpretation?

The note does not answer these questions because its purpose is to establish a record against which future answers can be tested.

Overall assessment
The main arguments can be summarized in four propositions:

1. The wills do not support a simple equation between the inheritance of books and the succession of the Peripatetic school. Aristotle’s will is silent about books and philosophical succession, while Theophrastus’s will treats the library and the school premises under different arrangements.

2. The catalogues are internally complex records. Their overlapping titles, repeated entries, alphabetic sequences, and alternative attributions leave questions about the identity and organization of the listed works unresolved.

3. The familiar narrative of Aristotelian transmission must be distinguished from what each surviving source independently attests. Strabo supplies a custody chain that the wills only partially corroborate, while Plutarch supplies additional information about Andronicus.

4. Numerical discrepancies are not, on their own, measurements of textual loss. The ancient catalogue totals and the modern word counts describe different bodies of evidence and cannot be equated without further assumptions.

The deeper point is that Sharks is not offering a new, fully reconstructed history of the Aristotelian corpus. The author is arguing for a disciplined way of constructing one.

The text’s restraint is substantive: it refuses to let a coherent narrative, an apparent catalogue structure, or an impressive numerical ratio stand in for evidence that has not actually been established. Its intended achievement is to make subsequent historical interpretations more accountable to the surviving Greek texts.

In one sentence: Catalogues and Wills argues that the history of the Peripatetic books must be reconstructed from the precise relations among wills, catalogues, and later transmission accounts, while keeping explicit the difference between what those sources establish and what historians still need to explain.
