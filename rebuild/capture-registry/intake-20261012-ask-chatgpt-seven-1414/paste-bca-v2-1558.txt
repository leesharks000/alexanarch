1. The foundational problem: selection destroys the evidence needed to audit selection
In §§1–2, Sharks starts from a problem in scientific measurement rather than from a claim that machine learning is inherently unsuitable for science.

A high-throughput experiment cannot necessarily store every event. It must select a subset, and learned triggers may make that selection using reconstruction errors, latent representations, or other model-dependent scores.

The difficulty is that the trigger’s failures may be systematically hidden. If a scientifically interesting event receives a low score and is discarded, the later researcher may have no way to establish that the event ever occurred. The missing event is not merely a false negative in a performance table: it is missing evidence for constructing that table.

The reasoning is:

A trigger determines which events survive.

The surviving archive is therefore conditioned on the trigger’s decisions.

Evaluating the trigger against that archive alone excludes the very events needed to measure its rejection errors.

Consequently, apparent performance on the surviving data cannot establish how well the trigger would have performed on the full incoming population.

The paper summarizes the issue as follows: “The data needed to measure the selector's errors may itself be destroyed by the selector.” (§1) 

Alexanarch

This is the fundamental epistemic claim: the instrument controls not only which events become data, but also which errors can subsequently become knowledge.

2. The proposed solution: a control plane, not another trigger
Sections 3–8 develop the Baseline Capture Architecture. Sharks argues that measurement and discovery are different functions and must be designed separately.

The architecture has four channels:

C0
Content-independent probability sample

Retain a known-probability sample before the audited trigger determines whether events survive. Inclusion must not depend on the event’s content or anomaly score.

C1
Full-rate predecision representation tap

Record the representation actually consumed by the trigger, together with its score, decision, model or firmware version, and relevant operating conditions.

C2
Probability-weighted enrichment

Oversample promising or unusual strata to improve sensitivity to rare events, but record their inclusion probabilities so that the resulting data remain statistically interpretable.

C3
Shadow selectors

Run additional triggers on common inputs and log their decisions without allowing them to control acquisition. This makes it possible to measure overlapping blind spots.

These channels feed a replay bank: a versioned, provenance-preserving record that allows researchers to evaluate an old trigger against event classes defined in the future. (§§3–8) 

Alexanarch

The four channels are complementary, not interchangeable. C0 provides the independent statistical control; C1 documents what the original trigger actually saw; C2 improves rare-event exposure under a known sampling law; and C3 measures the relationships among competing selectors’ errors.

That division of labor is the architecture’s central design decision.

If that criterion determines which events are retained, it can recreate the original problem.

Sharks does not claim that a small baseline can guarantee discovery of arbitrarily rare phenomena. Instead, he separates the clean control from a second, enriched sample.

C2 can deliberately sample some strata more heavily, provided the inclusion probabilities remain known and positive. The retention fraction can then be estimated using inverse-probability weighting. The text carefully distinguishes the classical Horvitz–Thompson estimator, which is unbiased for population totals under the stated sampling conditions, from the Hájek ratio estimator used for retention fractions, which is design-consistent but not generally exactly unbiased at finite sample sizes.

The argument is therefore not that statistics can overcome every limitation of finite data. It is that efficiency improvements must preserve the information needed to quantify their own selection effects. (§6)

5. Why multiple selectors do not necessarily provide independent protection
In §7, Sharks challenges the assumption that running several different triggers automatically protects an experiment against unforeseen events.

Two selectors may achieve good performance individually and still miss many of the same events. If their errors are correlated, architectural diversity may provide much less additional discovery coverage than their different designs suggest.

The C3 shadow plane allows multiple selectors to score the same inputs without controlling which events enter the control archive. Researchers can then measure each selector’s miss rate and the overlap between their misses.

For two selectors, the paper defines the joint miss probability and compares it with the product of the individual miss probabilities. Their difference measures excess miss dependence at the chosen operating points.

This changes how alternative architectures should be evaluated. Instead of comparing only benchmark performance, latency, or resource consumption, researchers can ask whether a new selector actually covers regions that existing selectors miss.

The replay bank extends this principle through time. By preserving the original representation, sampling probabilities, decisions, thresholds, model versions, and integrity information, it allows researchers to define a new anomaly class years later and ask how the earlier instrument would have treated it.

Sharks makes a particularly useful distinction in §8: training data describe what a model learned from; the replay bank is intended to characterize what the model would have rejected. (§§7–8)

6. The practical claim: this is a feasible measurement system, not an idealized demand for unlimited storage
Sections 9–12 translate the theory into an implementation and reporting standard.

The primary output is a retention map, rather than one aggregate bias score. It describes how the trigger’s probability of retaining events varies across retrospectively defined classes and relevant physical or representational dimensions.

The paper proposes that learned triggers be documented not only through model architecture, training data, loss functions, latency, and benchmark curves, but also through:

The declared fidelity locus and its upstream information losses.

Baseline sampling rate and effective sample exposure.

Retention surfaces and directional tests.

Model-version drift and overlap between shadow-model misses.

Regions where no meaningful retention bound has been established.

That final distinction is important. A region known to have low retention is not the same as a region whose retention has never been measured.

Sharks grounds the feasibility argument in existing accelerator infrastructure, including CMS Zero Bias data, Level-1 Data Scouting, and the Global Trigger test crate. He explicitly does not claim that these systems already implement BCA. His claim is that they demonstrate the feasibility of important component operations; what remains missing is a common protocol connecting them for the specific purpose of measuring selection.

The proposed minimum implementation includes a randomized bypass sample, a decision ledger, at least one shadow selector, replay provenance, and recurring retention reporting. Enrichment is optional initially. If a full-rate or full-fidelity archive is infeasible, the specification permits a reduced representation or sampling arrangement, provided its limitations are declared. (§§9–12)

7. The numerical demonstration: does the proposed control actually work?

The experiment uses a synthetic stream of four million events, a rank-4 linear autoencoder, and four injected anomaly classes. Because the data are constructed, the true retention rate of each class is known. Four acquisition regimes are compared at different but comparable storage budgets.

The experiment also exposes a limitation of the proposed solution. For the rare class, the uniform sample expected only 0.16 observations and captured one. The enriched design, meanwhile, captured none of that class because it fell outside the enrichment band. The paper uses these outcomes to show that a valid sampling architecture can still have inadequate power for a particular target.

Another important result concerns the failed initial enrichment design. By concentrating on high trigger scores, it starved the very region where missed events were likely to occur. Sharks reports this failure rather than presenting only the corrected design, reinforcing his argument that the control plane itself needs auditing.

What the validation establishes—and what it does not
The demonstration supports the claim that, in this controlled setting, the proposed architecture can estimate known retention rates and expose failures that trigger-only or correlated-selector archives conceal.

It does not establish that BCA has been deployed successfully in CMS or another live experiment, that the method guarantees discovery of unknown phenomena, or that the synthetic retention rates predict the performance of real scientific triggers.

The paper is explicit in §14 that BCA cannot solve the unknown-unknown problem, cannot recover information destroyed upstream of its fidelity locus, and cannot guarantee that a sufficiently rare event will enter the baseline. Its numerical validation uses synthetic data rather than newly analysed experimental data. (§§13–14)

8. The deeper argument: scientific instruments need an independent means of measuring their omissions
The paper’s final argument in §§15–16 is methodological. Sharks is not proposing that learned triggers should be abandoned or that they are uniquely unreliable. Nor is he arguing that all events should be stored.

He is arguing that a system whose scientific value depends on finding the unforeseen must preserve some evidence independently of its own learned definition of what is worth retaining.

That leads to three broader conclusions:

Selection performance and selection measurement are different engineering objectives. Optimizing a trigger for discovery is not the same as constructing an unbiased means of evaluating its failures.

Claims of model independence require evidence beyond benchmark performance. A system can perform well on specified classes while remaining insensitive to other classes that its benchmarks do not represent.

Uncertainty about what was lost should become an explicit scientific result. A retention map should report both measured blind spots and regions where retention remains uncharacterized, instead of allowing missing evidence to appear as evidence of successful selection.

The title’s phrase control plane captures this division: the proposed architecture does not determine what the experiment considers interesting. It preserves an independent evidentiary basis for measuring the consequences of that decision.

Overall assessment
Read on its own terms, the paper is a proposal for selection metrology: making the omissions of an irreversible learned selection system measurable after the fact.

Its reasoning proceeds from a structural problem—selection destroys the denominator needed to measure selection—to an architectural solution, a statistical estimation framework, an implementation proposal grounded in existing accelerator capabilities, and a synthetic validation demonstrating both the method’s potential and its limits.

The strongest demonstrated result is not that the architecture makes anomaly detection more accurate. It is that an independent probability sample can reveal a known class of events that a trigger-only archive and a second, similarly blind selector fail to characterize.

The central open question is whether the architecture can be implemented at the necessary operating rates in real experiments, with sufficient sample exposure and appropriate retrospective class definitions, to deliver scientifically useful measurements of actual trigger blind spots. The text provides a feasibility argument and a controlled numerical demonstration, not a completed deployment or an empirical answer to that question.
