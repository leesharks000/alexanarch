1. The initial problem: Aristotle says both that his inquiry is new and that it includes Platonic division
The paper opens with a tension between two Aristotelian passages:

In Sophistical Refutations 34 (183b35), Aristotle says that nothing at all existed beforehand in the inquiry he is discussing.

In Prior Analytics I.31 (46a33–38), he describes division—the method practiced throughout Plato’s dialogues—as a kind of syllogism, albeit a weak one.

The difficulty is that these statements seem to pull in opposite directions. If Aristotle’s inquiry had no predecessor, how can a method associated with Platonic philosophy be part of it?

Sharks identifies four possible points at which an interpretation might resolve the tension: the meaning of part, the meaning of existed beforehand, the identity of the method, or the meaning of precedes. His stated position is methodological rather than immediately historical: he will not quietly alter one of these terms before examining the relevant passages.

That matters because the opening question is not simply whether Aristotle borrowed from Plato. It is what kind of unity can hold between two bodies of thought when one is said to precede the other, yet the later account seems to incorporate the earlier method.

The paper first considers the conventional interpretation of Prior Analytics I.31: Aristotle is criticizing Platonic division because it assumes what it needs to prove. Each division depends on an interlocutor accepting a distinction, so the method does not independently establish the essence of the thing being investigated.

Sharks does not deny the polemical force of this reading. Instead, he argues that it leaves something unexplained. Aristotle calls division a “weak syllogism” and then says that its practitioners did not understand what can be syllogized by dividing. For Sharks, the negative judgment implies a positive possibility: there is something division can accomplish, even if the Platonists did not understand it.

This establishes the paper’s central interpretive strategy: take Aristotle’s criticism seriously without assuming that the criticized method is philosophically worthless.

2. Division can fail to prove an essence yet still have a meaningful terminus
The paper’s first major philosophical argument appears in §2, “What would it mean for division to terminate?”

Sharks compares the failure of division in Prior Analytics I.31 with the argument about divine intellect in Metaphysics Λ.9 (1074b–1075a).

In the Prior Analytics, Aristotle considers whether a human being must be mortal or immortal. Division establishes the disjunction, but it does not establish that a human being is a mortal animal. The crucial conclusion has been assumed rather than demonstrated. The method reaches a boundary between alternatives without justifying the selection of one.

In Metaphysics Λ.9, however, Aristotle works through alternatives about divine intellect:

Does it think nothing, or does it think?

If it thinks, does it think itself or something else?

If something else, does it think the same thing eternally or something different?

The alternatives are progressively eliminated until Aristotle concludes that divine intellect thinks itself. Sharks’s crucial observation is that the argument ends by identifying thought and its object: where there is no matter, intellect and what it thinks are not other.

This is the proposed solution to the earlier problem. Division can terminate not only when an alternative is refuted, but when the opposition between the terms collapses into identity. In that case, there is no longer a distinction that needs to be explained through a middle term.

The distinction is important. Sharks is not arguing that Aristotle’s Metaphysics simply proves that Platonic division is a valid method of demonstrating essences. He acknowledges the objection that the final step in Λ.9 is better described as a dissolution of the opposition than as a selection between alternatives. His claim is narrower: the passage supplies a case in which a descent through alternatives reaches a terminus because the relevant terms are ultimately identical.

The first conceptual result is therefore that division has a limit condition. It cannot manufacture an essence through a chain of distinctions; it can, however, reach a point at which the distinction itself no longer holds.

3. A single living thing can be one in actuality and many in potentiality
Section 3 develops the argument through De anima II.2 (413b13–24).

Aristotle asks whether the different capacities of the soul—nutrition, perception, locomotion and thought—are separate souls or parts of one soul. His discussion invokes plants and certain animals whose parts remain alive after division.

The relevant formula is that such a creature has “one soul in actuality, many in potentiality.”

Sharks interprets this to mean that division need not create a plurality from something that was previously an undifferentiated unity. The plurality can already be present as a potentiality within the living whole. Cutting the organism actualizes that plurality.

This extends the argument of §2 from divine intellect to living things, but with an important difference:

In the divine case, division terminates because the terms are not other.

In the living case, unity and multiplicity coexist because the one thing contains the potential for multiple living parts.

The paper thus rejects the assumption that unity and plurality must always be mutually exclusive. Whether a thing is one or many depends, in part, on the level at which its unity is being considered.

This is also the beginning of the paper’s larger analogy. If one living thing can contain a plurality of actualizable parts, a corpus of philosophical writings might likewise exhibit unity without requiring uniformity in every constituent text. At this stage, however, Sharks is developing the philosophical concept, not yet claiming that it proves anything about authorship.

4. One thing can sustain many true accounts without becoming many things
Section 4 turns to Plato’s Sophist, especially 232a, where the sophist appears in a series of different definitions.

The sophist can be described as a hunter of wealthy young men, a merchant in learning, a disputant, a purifier of beliefs and in other ways. Sharks focuses on the relationship between the plurality of these accounts and the object they describe.

His claim is that multiple true accounts of something do not entail the existence of multiple objects. The six descriptions need not contradict one another simply because they identify different aspects or activities of the sophist.

The error lies in the observer who counts accounts and then mistakenly counts objects. The multiplicity belongs to the descriptions; it does not necessarily belong to their referent.

This third extension reinforces the previous two:

Division can terminate when its alternatives are not ultimately distinct.

A living unity can contain a plurality of potential parts.

A single object can admit many true descriptions.

The point is not that every collection of statements is consistent, or that contradictions disappear whenever two texts are placed together. Rather, the unity of an object is not refuted merely by the plurality of ways in which it can be described.

Sharks also connects this interpretation with modern formal work on Platonic division and syllogistic logic. He treats that work as evidence of structural compatibility between the logical practices represented in the Platonic and Aristotelian corpora—not as proof that a single person wrote both.

5. Division and collection are complementary operations; recollection gathers the many into one
Section 5 brings the preceding arguments together through the Phaedrus and Aristotle’s De memoria.

In Phaedrus 265d–266c, Plato describes two complementary capacities:

Gathering dispersed particulars into one idea, so that one can define what one wishes to discuss.

Dividing things according to their forms and natural joints, without arbitrarily breaking them apart.

The second operation is division; the first is collection. Together, they constitute a single dialectical power.

Sharks then reads Phaedrus 249c as giving collection a deeper significance. Recollection (anamnesis) is described as moving from many perceptions toward one, gathered through reasoning. In this account, recollection is not merely retrieving a forgotten item. It is an operation by which plurality becomes intelligible as a unity.

The comparison with De memoria extends the connection between recollection and reasoning or syllogism. The article’s accumulating suggestion is that dividing, combining, syllogizing and recollecting can be understood as related expressions of one operation: the articulation of unity and plurality.

This is the philosophical core of the title Many and One. Unity is not simply the absence of multiplicity. It can be achieved through the correct articulation of a plurality, just as division can disclose the internal structure of a unity.

There is also a methodological consequence. The reader must do more than count passages, definitions or texts. The reader must understand what relates them, what kind of unity they instantiate, and where their distinctions genuinely hold.

6. The corpus itself stages the division of a person
Section 6 marks the transition from philosophical interpretation to the question of authorship. Sharks reads passages in which a person’s identity is divided into distinguishable aspects, particularly Republic 395b and Symposium 223d.

In the Republic, the argument concerns whether the same person can practice both tragedy and comedy. The passage presents human nature as too limited to perform two different arts equally well. In the Symposium, Aristophanes’ myth of divided human beings supplies a contrasting image: a divided being seeks to recover unity.

Sharks connects these passages through the shared problem of how one identity relates to multiple capacities or parts. He extends the connection to the Symposium’s account of Eros as seeking to make one out of two.

The proposed reconstruction is that fragmentation does not necessarily destroy identity. Parts may become recognizable as belonging together precisely through the way they fit. The corpus, on this reading, provides patterns for recognizing a unity across difference.

But Sharks explicitly marks this as a reconstruction, not a straightforward conclusion stated by Plato. The dialogues provide the structure of division and recombination; applying that structure to the identity of the corpus is a further interpretive step.

This qualification is crucial because it marks the transition from the paper’s textual readings to its authorship hypothesis.

7. The corpus may be a functional unity without being the product of one biography
Section 7 develops the idea that the corpus can be read as a unity independently of the question of who physically wrote its works.

Sharks draws on Phaedrus 276a–277a, where Plato distinguishes the living, knowledgeable account planted in a learner’s soul from the written account, which is its image. A living account can defend itself, address the appropriate person, and generate further accounts in other souls.

This distinction gives the paper a model of textual continuity that does not depend on the survival of one physical inscription or on uniformity of style. What persists is an intelligible operation that can be reproduced in different forms and in different readers.

Sharks calls the relevant distinction functional identity versus biographical identity:

Functional identity means that a set of texts can be interpreted as one coherent account or programme.

Biographical identity means that those texts were produced by one embodied person.

The first can be argued from patterns within the writings. The second requires different evidence.

This is the bridge between the philosophical and historical parts of the article. If the Platonic and Aristotelian texts exhibit a shared structure of division, recollection, transmission and unity, Sharks argues that they can be read as one functional configuration. That observation alone does not tell us whether one or several historical authors produced them.

8. Stylometry and the structure of the corpus do not settle the number of authors
Section 8 tests whether textual and stylistic evidence can resolve the authorship question.

Sharks reports two analyses.

The first is a functional comparison. He identifies six roles in the transmission of an account across a break in its bearer: encoding, substrate, decommissioning, withholding, recovery and verification. He argues that the Platonic dialogues and Aristotelian treatises both instantiate these functions, though in different works and through different textual devices.

The result, as he presents it, is evidence of a shared textual architecture. But he expressly concedes that the same architecture could arise from a school or shared intellectual programme. It does not prove that the same individual wrote both corpora.

The second analysis concerns stylistic variation. Sharks reports that Aristotle’s overall variation in function-word profiles is unusually high, but that this anomaly disappears when texts are compared within their respective subject domains. He gives the resulting dispersions as 0.0571 for Aristotle and 0.0570 for Plato.

His conclusion is deliberately limited: these measurements do not determine the number of makers. The results are a null with respect to authorship, not evidence that the two corpora are identical or that two authors never existed.

This section also challenges the assumption that the conventional early–middle–late ordering of Plato’s dialogues provides an independent chronological foundation for interpreting the relationship between Plato and Aristotle. Since stylometry helped construct that ordering, Sharks argues, it cannot automatically be treated as independent proof of the historical development that the ordering is then used to explain.

The proposed alternative is that the corpus may exhibit an order of philosophical forms or operations rather than a straightforward chronology of an individual’s development. Sharks acknowledges that this alternative remains a hypothesis requiring independent testing.

9. The final hypothesis: one maker, multiple textual positions
Section 9.4 is where the article finally states its historical hypothesis.

Sharks returns to the four propositions introduced in §1:

Division is a part of the method described in Prior Analytics I.31.

Aristotle says that nothing of his inquiry had been worked out beforehand.

Division is already described as an art in the Phaedrus and given rules in other Platonic dialogues.

The relevant Platonic dialogues are conventionally understood to precede the Aristotelian treatises.

The conventional account resolves this tension by distinguishing the earlier practice of division from Aristotle’s later, more systematic theory. Sharks argues that this resolution comes at a cost: it must qualify the scope of Aristotle’s originality claim or treat the Platonic passages about division as products of an art rather than genuine statements of the art itself.

His alternative is to move the chronological priority claim from the relation between two authors to the relation between two stages or positions within one intellectual production.

On this reading, the sequence of Plato and Aristotle represents a development in understanding: the operation is practiced, its limits are encountered, and its full range becomes visible. The statement that nothing existed beforehand denies inheritance from predecessors, not necessarily the existence of an earlier stage in the same maker’s work.

Sharks argues that this reading preserves the language of Sophistical Refutations 34 while accounting for the relation between that passage and Prior Analytics I.31.

What the hypothesis claims
The article’s strongest claim is not merely that Plato influenced Aristotle, or that their works belong to one tradition. It proposes that the corpus can be read as the product of one self-dividing maker who appears through different textual positions.

The familiar names then designate different functions in a single production:

Plato represents the dialogue position, which dramatizes philosophical inquiry through questions, interlocutors, division and recollection.

Aristotle represents the treatise position, which organizes and systematizes an account.

Theophrastus represents an intermediate or “first draft” position, which extracts, classifies and reopens material.

Sharks develops this model through the way the texts divide intellect, leave certain questions unresolved, and invite a reader to complete an intelligible connection. He then identifies a corresponding seam in the textual corpus, particularly around Metaphysics Λ and the relation between the Aristotelian and Theophrastan materials.

The analogy is structural: just as a single intellect can be divided into functions without thereby becoming two unrelated intellects, a single production can appear under different names and textual forms without those names automatically establishing different makers.

However, Sharks does not claim to have demonstrated that Plato, Aristotle and Theophrastus were historically one person. He explicitly distinguishes the textual hypothesis from the biographical question, acknowledges that the received account remains possible, and says that papyrological, inscriptional and transmission evidence would take precedence in determining the number of historical authors.

10. What the paper establishes—and what remains speculative
The article’s argument is best understood as having three levels of strength.

1. Philosophical interpretation
Sharks offers a connected reading of several ancient passages: division can terminate in identity; a unity can contain a plurality of potential parts; one object can admit many true accounts; and recollection can gather dispersed particulars into one. These readings supply the conceptual vocabulary for the rest of the article.
2. Structural comparison
He argues that the Platonic and Aristotelian corpora share functional patterns and that stylistic variation does not, by itself, settle how many authors produced them. These claims make a unified reading possible, but they do not establish a single author.
3. Historical hypothesis
The proposal that Plato, Aristotle and Theophrastus are different textual positions of one maker is the boldest and least established conclusion. It depends on treating philosophical and textual correspondences as evidence for a production model, rather than as evidence of shared intellectual inheritance among distinct people.
The central weakness is therefore the gap between showing that a corpus can be read as a functional unity and showing that it was produced by one historical person. Sharks recognizes this gap, but his final hypothesis asks the reader to consider a much more radical explanation than the preceding textual readings alone require.

His discussion of stylometry reinforces the underdetermination of authorship rather than resolving it. Likewise, the observation that the texts do not themselves establish every element of the conventional historical account does not establish the rival account. The absence of conclusive proof for two makers is not proof of one maker.

Conclusion
The paper’s argument proceeds from logic to metaphysics, from metaphysics to the interpretation of texts, and from textual interpretation to a hypothesis about authorship.

Its governing idea is that unity does not require uniformity, and multiplicity does not necessarily imply multiple independent origins. Division may reveal a plurality already contained in a unity; multiple accounts may describe one object; and a scattered body of writings may form a coherent intellectual configuration.

Sharks then turns that principle back on the Platonic and Aristotelian corpora. He argues that their differences, their developmental patterns and their distinct names need not be interpreted exclusively through a history of two separate authors. A single maker occupying different textual positions is offered as an alternative reading.

The most defensible conclusion from the article is not that Plato and Aristotle were one person, but that the philosophical passages Sharks examines do not, by themselves, settle the historical question. His single-maker hypothesis is the paper’s wager: a proposal intended to make sense of the texts’ patterns of division, unity and transmission, rather than a historical fact established by them.
