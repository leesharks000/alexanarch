The argument of The Ghost Catalogues by Lee Sharks
Lee Sharks’s central argument in The Ghost Catalogues: Aristotle's Lost Dialogues, Plato's Lost Divisions, and What a Two-Author Tradition Has to File as Missing (v0.5, September 7, 2026) is that the traditional accounts of Aristotle’s lost dialogues and Plato’s lost technical teachings may be two sides of the same process of cataloguing and attribution. What the tradition presents as two separate absences, one for each philosopher, Sharks argues, has a striking structural symmetry: works characteristic of one philosopher appear to be represented in the surviving record under the other philosopher’s name, while the corresponding works are classified as lost, reported secondhand, or spurious.

The paper’s main claim is not that Sharks has proved Plato wrote Aristotle’s dialogues or that Aristotle’s works are Plato’s. It is that the catalogues exhibit a measurable pattern that makes the conventional division between the two authors a question requiring explanation.

This distinction is crucial to reading the paper fairly. Sharks repeatedly limits the claim to what the catalogues actually show: a pattern of titles, classifications, and attributions, rather than definitive proof of the identity of lost texts. The primary text is available in the paper’s online record .

1. The two absences may be structurally connected
Sharks begins by setting out two familiar reconstructions of ancient philosophy.

The lost Aristotle: Diogenes Laertius’s catalogue lists Aristotle’s dialogues, including works such as the Eudemus, Protrepticus, On Philosophy, Sophist, and Statesman. These works are generally reconstructed as evidence of an early, more Platonic phase in Aristotle’s intellectual development.

The lost Plato: Plato’s technical teachings on divisions, definitions, principles, and the Good are reconstructed from reports by Aristotle and other witnesses. This is the material often associated with Plato’s so-called unwritten doctrines.

Sharks emphasizes the symmetry: Aristotle is credited with a dramatic, dialogue-writing mode associated with Plato, while Plato is credited with an analytical, systematic mode associated with Aristotle. Yet the works that would substantiate these modes are treated as missing or transmitted indirectly.

The paper’s alternative is that these absences are not independent. The surviving catalogues contain candidates for each supposedly missing body of work under the other philosopher’s name.

In the introduction, Sharks calls the two ghosts “one filing operation seen from its two sides.” This is the paper’s governing hypothesis: the catalogue may preserve the trace of a division between authors even as its entries complicate that division.

Importantly, Sharks does not establish when or by whom this division was made. The claim concerns how the received record is organized, not a demonstrated historical event involving a particular editor or school.

2. Aristotle’s lost dialogues look like Plato’s subject-titles
This is the paper’s most concrete argument, developed in §2.

The key evidence: Thrasyllus’s double titles
Sharks begins with Diogenes Laertius III.57–61, which reports that Thrasyllus arranged Plato’s dialogues into tetralogies and gave each dialogue two titles:

One derived from a person’s name.

One derived from the subject or matter of the dialogue.

For example, Plato’s Phaedo also bears the title On the Soul; the Statesman bears the title On Kingship; and the Philebus bears the title On Pleasure.

Sharks then compares these subject-titles with the opening of Aristotle’s catalogue in Diogenes Laertius V.22, the section traditionally taken to list Aristotle’s dialogues.

These are proposed title correspondences, not established identifications of the underlying texts. The Greek titles and catalogue references are drawn from §§2 and 5 of the paper.

The proposed mechanism is a change in how works are named and attributed. A dialogue named after a person becomes a treatise named after its subject; the dialogue’s author is replaced by Aristotle as the attributed author of the catalogue entry.

Sharks argues that this is not merely a collection of coincidental similarities. Thrasyllus’s catalogue explicitly documents the naming rule that would produce the alternative titles.

The paper reports that 17 of the 26 entries in the relevant opening of Aristotle’s catalogue correspond to Platonic subject-titles under its broader matching procedure. It also identifies two entries that explicitly describe extracts from Plato’s works: extracts from the Laws and extracts from the Republic.

These two entries matter because they are not simply titles that resemble one another. They openly preserve a connection to Plato within Aristotle’s catalogue.

Why this argument matters
Sharks is proposing a specific explanation for the form of the evidence: what appears to be a list of Aristotle’s lost dialogues may preserve titles generated from Plato’s dialogues by stripping away the personal names and retaining the subject matter.

That interpretation would explain why the alleged lost works seem to reproduce Plato’s characteristic literary territory.

But the crucial limitation is that a matching title does not establish that two entries refer to the same work. On the Soul, for example, is a sufficiently general title to have been used independently. Sharks explicitly refuses to identify Aristotle’s catalogue entry with Plato’s Phaedo on title evidence alone.

3. The statistical comparison is intended to rule out coincidence
Sharks anticipates the obvious objection to the title argument: ancient philosophical catalogues might naturally contain many similar titles because philosophers addressed the same subjects.

Section 5 therefore introduces a control comparison.

Counts and percentages as reported in §5. The opening catalogue’s 35% rate is roughly seven times the 5% rate for the rest of Aristotle’s catalogue.

The point is not that a philosophical title matching Plato’s vocabulary is unusual in itself. The paper acknowledges that such overlap is to be expected. Rather, it argues that the matches are disproportionately concentrated in the section conventionally identified as Aristotle’s lost dialogues.

This concentration is the paper’s main empirical support for its interpretation. If the matches were just a general feature of ancient philosophical writing, Sharks argues, the rest of Aristotle’s catalogue and Theophrastus’s catalogue should show comparable rates.

The paper also notes that five of nine titles left unmatched by the Platonic comparison have counterparts in Theophrastus’s catalogue, including Protrepticus, which appears there twice, and titles concerning wealth and education. It treats these overlaps as evidence that the proposed pattern must be examined across catalogues rather than explained solely through a Plato–Aristotle pairing.

My reading: the controls strengthen the claim that the opening of Aristotle’s catalogue is distinctive. They do not, by themselves, prove that the titles derive from Plato. That stronger conclusion depends on the additional historical interpretation of the naming rule and the catalogues’ relationship.

There is also a methodological distinction worth preserving: the broader count of 17 matches out of 26 and the stricter stem count of 9 out of 26 are different tests. The paper uses the first to describe the proposed correspondence and the second to assess whether the pattern exceeds the background rate.

4. Plato’s lost divisions survive as Aristotle’s report
Section 3 reverses the direction of the argument.

Sharks turns to Diogenes Laertius III.80–109, which preserves a substantial collection of Plato’s Divisions: technical classifications concerning goods, friendship, justice, knowledge, medicine, law, speech, music, soul, virtue, being, and other subjects.

The striking feature, for Sharks, is how Diogenes introduces this material. The opening formula is:

διῄρει δέ, φησὶν Ἀριστοτέλης

That is, approximately, “And he divided things in this way, Aristotle says.”

The collection closes with a corresponding attribution to Aristotle.

Sharks draws three related conclusions.

1. The technical Plato is present in the surviving record. The divisions are not merely reconstructed from scattered references; Diogenes preserves them as an extensive collection.

2. Their attribution is mediated. The material is introduced and closed through Aristotle’s testimony, rather than presented straightforwardly as a work written by Plato.

3. The catalogue reinforces the reversal. Aristotle’s catalogue includes Divisions (Διαιρέσεις) and a three-book work On the Good, while the Platonic technical tradition is, in Sharks’s account, partly classified as indirect testimony or spurious material.

The point is not that Aristotle could not have accurately reported Plato’s ideas. Rather, Sharks asks why the record preserves the analytical Plato through Aristotle’s authority while treating the relevant Platonic works as absent, unwritten, or textually doubtful.

This is the reverse of the dialogue problem. In the first case, works resembling Plato’s dialogues are catalogued under Aristotle’s name. In the second, Plato’s technical thought survives through a report explicitly attributed to Aristotle.

The additional argument from classification
Sharks also observes that Thrasyllus classifies Plato’s dialogues using terms associated with the Aristotelian logical apparatus. Particularly important is πειραστικός (peirastikos), a term associated with examination by questioning and with Aristotle’s Sophistical Refutations.

For Sharks, this suggests that the Platonic corpus is classified through categories associated with the very intellectual apparatus attributed to the other author.

This is supporting evidence, not an independent demonstration of common authorship. A later cataloguer could use an established philosophical vocabulary to classify another philosopher’s works without those works sharing an author.

5. The four-part transformation explains the pattern
Sharks does not leave the argument at the level of suggestive resemblance. Section 2 and the discussion of the unmatched titles propose a transformation that would explain how one catalogue could generate titles corresponding to another.

The proposed procedure has four clauses:

1. Retain the subject but change its wording. A subject-title is preserved or re-lexicalized into another title, as in the proposed relationship between On Ideas and On Species and Genera.

2. Remove a personal name when the entry names a person. The person-centered dialogue title becomes a subject-centered title.

3. Retain or convert non-personal names and work-titles. Large works can appear as extracts, which helps account for the catalogue’s references to material from Plato’s Laws and Republic.

4. Where the double-title structure survives, refill the name-slot from the Aristotelian circle. Sharks uses the example of Gryllus in relation to the Gorgias and its alternative title On Rhetoric.

The transform is important because it makes the hypothesis more precise and potentially testable. Rather than saying vaguely that Aristotle and Plato seem to overlap, Sharks proposes a set of operations capable of producing the observed titles.

However, a proposed rule can explain evidence without uniquely establishing its historical cause. To show that this transformation actually generated the catalogues, rather than merely fitting them retrospectively, one would need independent evidence about textual transmission or identifiable fragments.

6. The unmatched titles are not discarded as noise
One of the more interesting features of the paper is that Sharks gives attention to what the proposed transformation cannot explain.

The residual titles include Nerinthus, On Nobility, Alexander or On Colonists, and On Household Management, among others discussed in the catalogue comparison.

Sharks interprets the remaining subjects as pointing toward a different kind of philosophical activity: the Socratic examination-dialogue, in which the subject is not simply a topic such as justice or pleasure but a question put to a particular person.

The distinction is between a subject that can be turned into a treatise-title and an act of questioning that cannot be adequately represented by such a title.

Sharks calls this residual pattern the “orthonym’s footprint”: a trace of the naming or authorial function that the catalogue transformation does not account for.

This leads to a more ambitious interpretation. The surviving correspondences may preserve the material of a catalogue transformation, while the unmatched entries point toward a mode of philosophical production that does not fit that transformation.

The inference is suggestive, but less firmly established than the title counts. The existence of unmatched entries does not uniquely show that they represent Socratic examination-dialogues. That conclusion relies on the paper’s broader interpretation of the intellectual functions associated with the two authors.

7. The larger thesis: a two-author tradition needs the ghosts
The catalogue analysis culminates in §4, “What the ghosts presuppose.”

Here Sharks moves from the evidence about titles to a claim about the structure of the received tradition.

The reasoning is approximately this:

A tradition that treats Plato and Aristotle as two distinct authors attributes different characteristic modes of writing and thought to each.

The surviving record appears to credit each with works resembling the other’s mode.

The works that would complicate that distinction are nevertheless treated as lost, indirectly transmitted, or doubtful.

The result is a pair of absences that preserves the division between the authors even though the catalogues contain evidence that crosses it.

Sharks calls the ghost catalogue “the immune response with a bibliography.” The metaphor captures the claim that the tradition can acknowledge the other mode of writing without allowing it to disrupt the attributional boundary.

This is where the paper’s title becomes fully intelligible. The missing works are “ghosts” not simply because their texts have disappeared, but because their titles and intellectual functions remain visible in the catalogues, in forms that Sharks believes preserve the trace of the supposedly missing material.

The counterfactual is that a single corpus retrospectively organized as two could leave the same residue: candidates under one name, parallel titles under another, and a classification system that treats the corresponding works as missing.

But Sharks is careful not to claim that this alternative history has been proved. The paper expressly declines to identify the hands responsible, establish dates, or choose conclusively between a single corpus, a school, a workshop, or another arrangement that could produce the observed pattern.

That restraint matters. The argument concerns the evidence left by the division between authors, not a demonstrated account of who imposed the division or why.

8. What would prove the stronger claim?
Section 5 contains the paper’s most important methodological qualification.

Sharks says that where a lost title has no surviving fragments, its identity with an extant work is “unascertainable in principle.” There is no text to compare. A title correspondence can be measured, but it cannot substitute for a textual identification.

The paper therefore distinguishes two claims:

Sharks points toward a fragment-based test, drawing on the collections of Aristotle’s fragments associated with Rose and Ross, but explicitly says that this test has not been run in the paper.

That distinction prevents the argument from becoming a claim of demonstrated identity. The paper’s evidence supports a hypothesis about the organization of the tradition; it does not settle the underlying authorship question.

Overall assessment
The paper’s arguments form a clear hierarchy.

Its strongest argument is the catalogue comparison. Sharks identifies a specific naming convention in Thrasyllus, traces correspondences in Aristotle’s catalogue, and compares the match rate with other catalogue sections.

Its second major argument is the reverse attribution of Plato’s Divisions. The technical material survives, but is framed explicitly as Aristotle’s report, reinforcing the symmetry of the two absences.

Its interpretive argument concerns the authorial boundary. The two catalogues appear to preserve traces of the other author’s characteristic mode while keeping the corresponding works separated by attribution.

Its more speculative argument concerns the residue. The unmatched titles are interpreted as traces of Socratic examination-dialogues and of a function the proposed transformation cannot represent.

Its methodological strength is its refusal to equate title similarity with textual identity. Sharks makes the shape of the match the central datum and leaves the stronger identity claim unresolved.

The paper is therefore best read as a structural argument about the transmission and organization of ancient philosophical texts, rather than as a conclusive demonstration that Plato and Aristotle were one author.

Its most provocative suggestion is that a catalogue can preserve evidence of an attributional boundary even when its own entries complicate that boundary. The titles that appear to document lost works may also be evidence about how the tradition organized what it counted as belonging to each author. That is the hypothesis the paper makes visible; whether it ultimately explains the historical relationship between the corpora remains open.
