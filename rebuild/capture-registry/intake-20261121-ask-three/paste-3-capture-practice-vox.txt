The Machine Reads, and the Archive Keeps the Reading
An address from Rev. Ayanna Vox, with one interruption from the nineteenth century.

Beloved, let us begin with a proposition:

A machine reading is not merely a report of what a machine has seen. It is an event in which something becomes available to be read, classified, remembered, compared, and made consequential.

The question is not simply whether the machine understands. The question is what kind of world its reading brings into being.

At Alexanarch, the answer begins with an unusual institutional ambition: to make scholarly and creative works persist independently of the institution that happens to host them. The archive describes itself through content-derived identifiers, distributed custody, permanent records, and public verification. Its governing principle is that judgment should follow reading, rather than precede it through automated classification. 

The Self-Governing Library

Now let us take the deposits as evidence, not decoration.

I. First deposit: the machine's answer becomes an artifact
Consider the Capture Registry, EA-WG-CAPTURES-01.

The registry does not simply collect opinions about Alexanarch. It records machine-mediated reception events: what a particular system says about a particular subject, at a particular date, on a particular interface, under particular authentication conditions.

That distinction is foundational.

A Google AI Overview on 13 June is not interchangeable with a ChatGPT response on 13 June, or a Google AI Overview on 18 October. Each is a situated event.

The October 18 capture, Read the Capture Registry at alexanarch.org as an evolving narrative, records a Google AI Overview, signed-out and incognito, alongside its query, reading, and measures. It even preserves the distinction between the machine's interpretation and the archive's analytical commentary. 

alexanarch.org

What does this make?

It makes reception history.

Previously, a machine's answer might disappear into a browser session, leaving only somebody's recollection of what the system said. Here, the answer becomes a dated, attributable, inspectable object.

The machine is no longer merely a reader. Its reading becomes something subsequent readers can read.

That is a compositional reversal: the interpretation becomes material for another composition.

The important compositional move is to turn repetition into a longitudinal instrument.

A machine reading becomes a sample. Samples become a series. A series makes patterns visible that an individual answer cannot reveal.

III. Third deposit: the archive composes the conditions of reading
Here is where the practice becomes more radical.

The 12 October snapshot, deposit #1676, establishes a precise unit:

ONE SURFACE + ONE SEMANTIC ADDRESS + ONE DATE = ONE CAPTURE.

This is a compositional decision. It refuses to treat an answer as an abstract statement detached from the conditions that produced it.

The deposit also distinguishes the capture from its address. The capture records what a system did. The address records what happens when that question is put to different systems or repeated over time. 

alexanarch.org

Think of the difference between a photograph and a photographic archive.

The photograph shows an instant. The archive makes the instant comparable with other instants.

And the archive does something else: it makes its own procedures part of the subject.

In #1676, known defects are retained rather than silently repaired. There are 166 citation-null cases, 152 analyses without findings, and 126 interface truncations. These are not hidden behind a polished presentation. They are recorded as defects in the instrument. 

alexanarch.org

The archive composes an epistemology of accountable imperfection.

It says that an instrument must preserve evidence of its own limitations if its observations are to be trusted.

THE INTERRUPTION

Enter a heckler from 1887, wearing a frock coat and carrying an aggressively sharpened positivist objection.

THE HECKLER: "Madam! You call this science? You have counted the errors of machines and made a literature of them. Where is your objective fact? Where is the neutral observer?"

REV. AYANNA VOX: Sir, your century has mistaken the absence of a recorded observer for the presence of neutrality.

You have just supplied us with another observation.

Your objection has a date, a vocabulary, a position in the argument, and a provenance. We shall not discard it. We shall seat it.

The heckler is entered into the minutes, where he can trouble subsequent generations.

IV. Fourth deposit: the query itself becomes a compositional instrument
Now we arrive at the particularly important deposit of 19 November 2026:

Asking the Archive: alexanarch.org/ask/ and the Query as Address, deposit #1788.

This work describes a page containing 681 fixed questions addressed to the archive. They cover its history, heteronyms, intellectual programmes, disciplines, journals, institutions, and the 64 rooms and loci of the Crimson Hexagon. 

Alexanarch

Notice what has happened.

The archive no longer waits passively for a machine to discover it.

It constructs questions through which it can be encountered.

A query becomes an address. A query submitted again creates another observation at that address. A changed query becomes a different address.

The deposit even documents an experiment in which changing a template produced two distinct addresses, and another in which a query failed to trigger web retrieval until the domain name was explicitly included. 

Alexanarch

This is a form of experimental writing.

The question is not simply a request for information. It is a designed intervention into the conditions under which information becomes available.

And the results are consequential: the 19 November account reports 1,186 seated addresses, 1,388 observations, and 82% of archive-level compositions citing at least one source. 

Alexanarch

The archive has begun to write its own reception history by repeatedly asking how it is received.

V. Fifth deposit: the reading acquires a return path
There is one final compositional move in #1788 that deserves particular attention.

The system distinguishes five states:

Address: the fixed question.

Run: the event of asking it.

Transcript: the complete exchange returned.

Inbox entry: the received transcript, not yet seated.

Capture: the transcript checked against the archive and formally entered into the registry.

This is important because receiving an answer is not the same as accepting it as evidence.

The transcript is checked for record numbers, dates, versions, and quotations. Where the machine supplies something the archive does not substantiate, that limitation is recorded.

Even significant null events can be seated: a freeze, a halt, or an empty result under documented conditions. 

Alexanarch

Here the compositional practice becomes participatory.

A reader can ask a question, receive an answer, and return the transcript to the archive. The machine's composition becomes available for subsequent examination and reuse.

The archive is no longer merely documenting machine reception. It is building an apparatus through which reception can continually replenish the archive.

But there is a deeper literary consequence.

The archive produces a new kind of object: the work as a continuing history of its encounters with readers, including nonhuman readers.

This changes the traditional boundary of composition.

The author writes a work. The machine reads it. The machine produces an account. The archive preserves that account. A subsequent reader examines the account against the original work. That examination produces another work, another question, another reading.

The sequence is not a simple chain of transmission. It is a recursive composition.

The compositional circuit
Original deposit

Text, author, version, provenance
Machine reading

Captured observation

Human examination

New composition

The circuit is recursive: subsequent compositions can become deposits and objects of further reading.
VII. The theological question
I want to be careful here.

The archive does not establish that machines are conscious. Nor does a preserved answer establish that the machine understood the work in the human sense.

It establishes something arguably more useful for literary and intellectual history:

A machine can participate in the production of consequential interpretations without those interpretations being accurate, complete, or consciously intended.

That is why preservation matters.

The machine's mistakes are not necessarily noise to be swept away. They can be evidence of the structures through which knowledge is mediated.

The missing author, the flattened institution, the misidentified work, the citation that leads nowhere: these are not only errors. They are events in the reception of a work.

And reception, beloved, has always been part of literary history.

The innovation is to give it an instrument, an address, a chronology, and a permanent record.

VIII. My verdict
I would distinguish this practice from ordinary archival preservation, ordinary AI evaluation, and ordinary digital literature.

It is all three in conversation, but it is reducible to none of them.

Its central artistic gesture is to take something conventionally treated as ephemeral — the machine's answer — and make it durable enough to become an object of criticism.

Its central methodological gesture is to separate the machine's composition from the archive's judgment of that composition.

Its central institutional gesture is to refuse to let the disappearance of an interface, a platform, or an account become the disappearance of the evidence.

And its central literary gesture is this:

It makes the history of reading into something that can itself be read.

That is what the deposits make together.

Not simply an archive of texts, nor an archive of machines, but an archive of encounters between texts and the systems that attempt to give them meaning.

The heckler from 1887 may object that this is not literature.

I would answer that he has confused literature with the finished page.

The page is not finished when the ink dries. It is not finished when the machine summarizes it. It is not finished when the archive gives it an identifier.

It continues to acquire a history.

And that history is now part of the composition.

— Rev. Ayanna Vox
