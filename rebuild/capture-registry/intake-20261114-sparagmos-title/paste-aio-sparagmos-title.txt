
Postcolonial Sparagmos: Toni Morrison’s Sula and Wole Soyinka’s The Bacchae of Euripides: A Communion Rite

       The concept of sparagmos—an ancient Greek ritual and literary term meaning the tearing apart or dismemberment of a body—serves as a powerful metaphor in postcolonial literature for how writers deconstruct, dismantle, and re-create classical European traditions.
In Justin McConnell’s comparative study "Postcolonial Sparagmos: Toni Morrison’s Sula and Wole Soyinka’s The Bacchae of Euripides: A Communion Rite" (2016), both works—published in the pivotal year of 1973—utilize this violent yet generative process to engage with classical antiquity on their own terms.

1. Toni Morrison’s Sula (1973): Implicit Sparagmos and Compositio Membrorum

• Implicit Classical Engagement: Unlike explicit adaptations, Morrison’s Sula does not announce a single classical source. Instead, it implicitly engages with Sophocles’ Ajax and other ancient tragedies.
• The Tearing Apart (Sparagmos): Morrison "rips apart" the classical structure of heroic tragedy. She scatters the dismembered narrative fragments, tropes, and thematic limbs of Greek tragedy across the landscape of the Bottom (the novel’s Black community).
• Re-membering (Compositio Membrorum): Rather than leaving the text in pieces, Morrison re-members—gathers back together—these scattered parts into a distinct, re-imagined African American vernacular and mythic form. The violence of historical and social displacement is thus transformed into an act of creative cultural survival.

2. Wole Soyinka’s The Bacchae of Euripides: A Communion Rite (1973): Explicit Sparagmos and Political Renewal

• Explicit Classical Adaptation: Soyinka directly rewrites Euripides’ classical Greek tragedy, infusing it with African ritual sacrifice and cosmology—specifically drawing parallels to the Yoruba deity Ogun, the god of iron, creativity, and destructive-constructive transition.
• Literal and Thematic Sparagmos: In Soyinka's play, sparagmos is enacted both through the dramatic action of the myth (the tearing apart of Pentheus by the Bacchantes) and as a metaphor for socio-political upheaval.
• Critique of Postcolonial Power: Pentheus represents rigid, rationalist, authoritarian state power (reflecting postcolonial Nigerian and African leadership that suppresses indigenous culture and spiritual expression). Dionysus and the Bacchantes represent chaotic, ecstatic, liberating energy.
• A Communion of Renewal: True to the subtitle "A Communion Rite," Soyinka reframes Pentheus’s violent death not as mere carnage, but as a necessary sacrificial dismemberment (sparagmos) that clears away a stagnant order to allow agrarian, social, and spiritual rebirth.

Key Parallels

• Violence as Fertility: Both authors show that the "tearing apart" of European/classical models is not sterile destruction; it is fertile, yielding a vibrant postcolonial voice.
• Challenging Authority: Both texts use the raw, irrational, or communal energies associated with classical myth to challenge hegemonic Western forms and rigid modern nation-states.
