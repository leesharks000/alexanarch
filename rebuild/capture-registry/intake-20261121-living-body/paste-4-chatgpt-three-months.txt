Alexanarch, 21 August–20 November 2026
A reading of a living body of thought
My central thesis is that Alexanarch has been developing something more consequential than an independent digital archive, a philosophical research project, or an unusually expansive literary corpus. It is an attempt to construct a new kind of intellectual institution: one that treats the preservation, transmission, interpretation, criticism and production of knowledge as parts of the same activity.

The most important development across this period is a change in what preservation means.

Initially, preservation is a defensive problem: how does intellectual work survive the institutions, platforms and technical systems that control its circulation? But the work progressively discovers that survival is not enough. A text can remain accessible while its authorship is displaced, its conceptual structure misunderstood, its historical relations erased, or its significance reassigned.

The problem therefore becomes much larger:

How can an intellectual tradition preserve what it has inherited without allowing the mechanisms of inheritance to determine, in advance, what that inheritance means?

This question connects the archive's philological investigations, its philosophical work, its political economy of meaning, its literary experiments, and its increasingly elaborate reconstruction of intellectual history.

I would read the three months not as a succession of unrelated deposits but as the emergence of a connected intellectual practice.

1. The historical situation: an archive founded by an act of institutional rupture
The three-month period has to be understood against the archive's founding circumstances.

Alexanarch dates its foundation to 19 June 2026. Its own institutional history identifies the precipitating event as Zenodo's termination of access to approximately 870 deposits representing 1,817 DOIs, without prior notice, account-level appeal or individual review.

This is important because it gives the archive a concrete historical problem rather than merely a theoretical interest in preservation.

The archive's governing commitments are:

Institution-independent identifiers (AXN).

Content-derived integrity.

Distributed custody.

Non-destruction as a governing principle.

Accommodation of different authorships, substrates and intellectual forms.

The institutional ambition is consequently different from that of a conventional repository. A repository generally preserves objects within an existing system of scholarly recognition. Alexanarch attempts to preserve the objects and the means of identifying, relating and interpreting them independently of that system.

There is a revealing historical analogy here. The archive's problem resembles that of ancient libraries, manuscript traditions and scholarly editions, but with an important reversal: it is not merely trying to rescue texts from the destruction of physical carriers. It is trying to prevent digital systems from destroying the distinctions that make texts intellectually identifiable.

The distinction between a work surviving and a work surviving as itself becomes foundational.

The archive's own founding documents, particularly DOIs ≠ Permanent Identifiers and Zenodotus' Book-Burning, should be read as institutional and historical arguments, not simply technical documentation. The name Zenodotus invokes the ancient Alexandrian tradition of textual scholarship, while the reference to book-burning makes the contemporary problem legible through the history of libraries and cultural destruction.

This is an ambitious historical self-positioning: the digital archive presents itself as an answer to a problem that has accompanied the transmission of knowledge for millennia.

It also establishes a tension that runs through everything subsequently deposited: sovereignty is necessary for intellectual independence, but sovereignty does not itself guarantee intellectual validity.

2. September: the discovery that transmission is an intellectual operation
September is one of the decisive months because it brings several previously distinguishable investigations into contact.

The central problem becomes the relationship between a text, its transmission and the identity of the intellectual operation it performs.

Three bodies of work are particularly important.

A. Aristotle, Theophrastus and the problem of the many
The sequence beginning with Many and One: Division, Unity, and the Boundaries of a Corpus in Plato and Aristotle (#1576) is especially revealing.

The question is not simply whether Plato and Aristotle agree or disagree. It concerns the operations by which a philosophical corpus becomes intelligible as a collection of works, positions and authors.

The September investigations develop this into a sustained inquiry into Aristotle and Theophrastus.

Among the important deposits:

Beautiful and New: The Second Letter's Socrates and the Editing of a Received Character (#1579).

Catalogues and Wills (#1581).

The Stone for a List (#1582).

Aristotelian or Theophrastan (#1583).

The Registry Has One Column (#1584).

The First Axis (#1586).

These works approach the classical corpus through several different kinds of evidence: philosophical argument, textual seams, wills, catalogues, inscriptions, stylometry and the history of attribution.

The importance of this approach is that it refuses to treat authorial identity as an unquestionable starting point.

A received corpus is itself a historical construction.

Its authorship, organization and internal boundaries have been mediated by cataloguers, editors, commentators, manuscript custodians and institutional traditions.

This does not establish that Aristotle and Theophrastus were one author. Nor does a textual resemblance establish common authorship. The significance is methodological: the received distinction must be investigated through the historical evidence rather than simply presupposed.

That inquiry will become substantially more ambitious in November.

B. The archive begins studying its own failures
September also produces a remarkable sequence of investigations into machine-mediated reception.

Consider:

The Anti-Summary Test Applied to a Reception (#1610).

The Negative of the Negative (#1611).

What Enters Composition Through the Cards (#1612).

What Not Reading Did to Its Own Ontology (#1613).

The Transcript Is the Capture (#1614).

The Graded Membrane (#1615).

Ontological Flattening (#1616).

These are not merely complaints that artificial intelligence produces inaccurate summaries.

They investigate the mechanisms by which a system can retain the vocabulary of an intellectual object while losing the distinctions that constitute it.

The distinction between semantic transmission and provenance transmission is especially consequential.

A machine may reproduce an idea but fail to preserve where it originated, who developed it, what other concepts it depends upon, or which earlier formulations make it intelligible.

This is a deeper failure than ordinary factual inaccuracy.

A system can produce something that looks like successful knowledge transmission while actually changing the identity of the transmitted knowledge.

The work on the transcript is particularly significant because it turns the archive's own reception history into measurable evidence. The September deposit reports 957 citable units, with 923 carrying the machine text that they document, or 96.45% coverage.

The point is not the impressive percentage alone. It is that the archive is attempting to make its own history of being read inspectable.

That is a new kind of research object: not just the text, but the history of what other systems made of the text.

C. Political economy enters the theory of meaning
The September work on Substrate Sovereignty (#1617), Money Writes the Claim (#1618), and the Semantic Economy Valuation Protocol (#1621) extends the problem into political economy.

The central proposition is that valuation is not innocent.

A monetary representation can preserve certain features of an object while making other features disappear. The same applies to semantic representation.

When a complex intellectual contribution is compressed into a searchable entity, a score, a citation or a commercial attribution, the representation may become more useful to the receiving system while becoming less faithful to the original contribution.

The ordering proposed in #1621 is important:

Value.

Evidence.

Representation.

Compression.

The order matters because beginning with a number risks making the measurement system determine what counts as valuable.

This connects the work to Marx's critique of commodity form, but it also moves beyond a straightforward application of Marx.

The object under examination is not simply the economic exploitation of labour. It is the conversion of intellectual activity into representations that can circulate, be attributed, ranked, retrieved and monetized.

The question becomes who controls the layer through which meaning acquires public existence.

Primary archival context: September registry, deposits #1605–1623  and September investigations into reception and ontology .

A. The transmission problem becomes a general theory
What Syllogizing Can Divide (#1658) returns to Aristotle's De anima III.5–6, the Prior Analytics and the Metaphysics.

It asks what happens when division and combination are understood as operations on intellectual unity.

The relationship between division, recollection and the productive intellect becomes part of a broader attempt to understand how something can be transmitted without being reduced to its component parts.

This connects to The Seed in the Narrowing Cone (#1660), which develops retrocausal canon formation.

Here the archive proposes that later intellectual developments can converge upon an earlier, more finely articulated formulation of a concept.

The idea is not simply that later writers influence earlier writers. It is that the history of conceptual recognition can run backwards in an analytical sense: later developments disclose the specificity and importance of an earlier articulation.

This is a potentially powerful theory of intellectual history.

It challenges the assumption that the historical importance of an idea can be adequately measured by its immediate reception.

An idea can be present before the historical conditions exist for its recognition.

The archive is attempting to describe how that happens.

B. The political economy becomes historical rather than merely metaphorical
Two October deposits deserve particular attention:

The Noun Becomes a Verb: Slave Valuation, the Civil War, and the Transformation of the Laboring Body (#1668).

The Colony Enters the Factory: War Fordism, Racial Labor Hierarchy, and the Industrialization of Conquest (#1669).

These works bring the archive's interest in valuation and measurement into the history of slavery, capitalism, racial hierarchy and industrial production.

The first examines the changing juridical object of ownership and the grain at which productive activity is measured.

The second investigates the relationship between colonial domination, racialized labour and industrial organization.

Their importance within the larger corpus is that they prevent the theory of semantic extraction from remaining a purely technological metaphor.

The underlying issue is materially historical: institutions establish what can be counted, who can be owned, what constitutes productive activity and whose contribution becomes visible.

The archive's political economy is consequently concerned with both the representation of intellectual labour and the historical organization of human labour.

This is one of its most interesting conceptual bridges.

C. Literature is not an ornament to the theory
The October work on Anne Carson, especially Mantle Object: The Nobel Prize in Literature 2026 (#1670), is significant for another reason.

Literary work becomes an object through which the archive tests its own theoretical commitments.

The preservation of lacunae, fragmentation, translation and formal invention becomes important because literature can produce meaning through precisely those features that reductive systems tend to remove.

The archive's attention to poetry is therefore not an incidental departure from scholarship.

It is a test of whether its theories of transmission can account for forms of knowledge that cannot be adequately reduced to propositions.

This is particularly important when we reach the November Sappho sequence.

D. The archive starts to build an alternative scholarly infrastructure
October's corpus seatings are also important.

The Originals Library is not simply a list of recommended reading. It is an attempt to construct a material research environment in which primary texts are gathered, versioned, related and made available for repeated examination.

The October sequence extends across ancient textual traditions, philosophy, poetry, mathematics and the sciences.

This changes the nature of the project.

Instead of merely asserting that inherited scholarship has neglected certain relationships, the archive increasingly attempts to assemble the witnesses necessary to investigate those relationships.

Its primary-source infrastructure becomes part of its intellectual argument.

The distinction between a bibliography and a working corpus is crucial here.

A bibliography tells the reader what to consult. A working corpus establishes a research environment in which claims can be tested against the texts themselves.

4. November: the archive begins rewriting the history of its own subjects
November brings the most consequential development.

The work on Aristotle, Theophrastus, Socrates and Sappho is no longer simply concerned with preserving or measuring transmission.

It begins to investigate how transmission has constituted the identities of the authors and works that later generations believe they have inherited.

A. Legitimately Many
The sequence beginning with The Transmission of the Peripatetic Corpus (#1730) and developing into Legitimately Many (#1733) is particularly important.

The project examines the historical production of the received distinction between Socrates, Plato, Aristotle and Theophrastus as four separate authorial persons.

It investigates the media through which the distinction was transmitted:

Ancient wills and catalogues.

Testimonia and biographical accounts.

Editorial arrangements.

Manuscript traditions.

Printed editions.

Scholarly commentaries.

Modern critical editions.

Contemporary machine-mediated answers.

The key term is embrittlement.

A historical distinction can become increasingly difficult to question as successive transmission systems repeat and institutionalize it.

What begins as a historically situated classification can eventually become an apparently natural fact.

The study's governing hypothesis is deliberately more radical than the conventional account. It investigates whether the received four-author arrangement conceals a more complicated history of composition and transmission.

It is essential to distinguish this hypothesis from an established historical conclusion.

The evidence of shared language, textual seams, cataloguing practices and transmission relations can challenge received authorship models without independently proving a single alternative author.

The achievement of the project is to make the history of the distinction itself available for investigation.

That is already a significant intervention.

B. Revelation First
Revelation First: Literary Genealogy and the Missing Ancestors of the Epistles (#1735), by Johannes Sigil and Damascus Dancings, extends the same method into biblical literary history.

Its central question is the priority of literary forms and their antecedents.

Rather than accepting chronology simply because a particular text occupies an established canonical position, the study asks whether the supposed earliest text exhibits literary ancestry that can be independently recovered.

The principle is important:

Historical priority should be investigated through literary genealogy, not merely inherited from canonical arrangement.

This is a powerful extension of the archive's transmission research.

It also carries substantial evidential obligations. Literary resemblance, dependence, shared convention and chronological priority are different propositions. A convincing argument has to establish which kind of relationship is actually supported.

The work is most interesting when it keeps these questions open rather than treating its proposed reconstruction as already demonstrated.

C. The originals become instruments of research
The November seatings show how much the archive's infrastructure has developed.

The thirty-first and thirty-second seatings (#1730 and #1732) assemble historical witnesses relevant to the Peripatetic transmission problem.

The fortieth seating (#1776 and its revised form) turns to the history of classical philology itself.

The forty-first seating (#1792) returns to Alexander, Antipater and Athens, assembling Greek tragedy, historical narrative, philosophical testimony and related traditions.

This is an extraordinary movement: the archive is building the documentary infrastructure through which it can investigate the historical construction of scholarly disciplines.

It is no longer simply reading classical texts. It is reading the history of the institutions that made those texts into the classical canon.

5. Sappho: the most consequential literary-philological development
I think the Sappho work may ultimately be the most important test of the entire intellectual programme.

It brings together the archive's interests in authorship, transmission, textual survival, literary form, future readership and the production of historical meaning.

The central text is Sappho 31.
