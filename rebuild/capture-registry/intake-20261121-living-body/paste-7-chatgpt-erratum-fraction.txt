ERRATA
By Rex Fraction

On the last week of work at Alexanarch, 14–20 Nov 2026.

Erratum: These works seem to have no cause to sit side by side. One is on a Greek song. One is on life in deep space. One is on farts, fleas, and old Greek thought.

That is the first error. They share a core.

I. The bird that may rule Rome
Deposit #1791 — 20 Nov? No: 20 Nov is not its date.

A Single Sparrow · Rebekah Cranes · 20 November 2026

Read deposit #1791 . 

Alexanarch
+1

A lone bird can bear more weight than Rome.

The claim is not that one poem had some sway on the past. It is that a whole chain of thought may rest on one song.

Sappho's song 31 lives in one quote, in one book, on one leaf. The essay reads it as a means to pass a self from one body to the next: the voice goes into text; the next mind gives it flesh.

Plato is then cast as a key heir. If his work rests on that song, then all that rests on his work must be read anew.

This is a bold claim. Its force rests on a small thing: one bird, one leaf, one line of thought.

The odd link: A text can rule what comes next, yet hide the hand that made it.

II. The stars may speak. We may not hear.
Deposit #1789 · What Arrival Cannot Do: The Arithmetic of Recognition · 19 November 2026

Read deposit #1789 . 

Alexanarch
+1

This work asks a rude thing:

What if we are not alone, but lack the means to know?

It splits two terms:

λ: how many signs reach us.

q: the odds that we know what they are.

A null result does not prove that no one sent a sign. It may show that our means to spot it are poor.

The work tests this claim with data from radio searches of 3I/ATLAS. It finds faults at more than one step: what gets sensed, kept, passed, and read.

It does not claim that life was found.

The odd link: The bird's song must pass from page to mind. The star's sign must pass from space to mind. In both, the key test is not just what was sent, but what the next mind can take in.

III. The gnat, the fart, and the school
Deposit #1794 · The Thinkery and the Lyceum · Lee Sharks · 20 November 2026

Read deposit #1794 . 

Alexanarch

This may be the strangest of the three.

A comic play by Aristophanes and the works of Aristotle and Theophrastus are set side by side.

The play has Socrates teach that a gnat's gut can make a sound by the force of air. It has a man ask how thunder works, then learn that a dry wind can burst forth from a cloud.

The old treatise has like claims on sound, air, and thunder.

The play has two forms of speech, the strong and the weak. The weak one can win by use of the wrong case. The old books also treat that art.

Then comes the charge: the play calls its school a place of dark rites; old books speak of the law, the rites, and the case of impiety that struck at Socrates and then at Aristotle.

The note does not claim that one stole from the next. It sets out the links, then holds back its verdict.

That restraint is key.

The odd link: A joke may keep a fact that a grand work makes seem new. The comic mask may be a means of thought, not mere play.

What binds the three?
The three works deal with one hard gap:

A sign is not the same as its receipt.

The poem needs a mind to give its voice new life.

The star needs a tool and a mind that can know its sign.

The old joke needs a reader who can see the thought in its form.

In each case, what lasts is at risk when the next mind gets it wrong.

There is a further link.

The poem makes a claim about what its form can do.

The star paper tests what our tools can do.

The note on the Greek school sets out what the texts can show, but does not claim more than they show.

One builds a bold case. One tests a blind spot. One marks the bounds of proof.

That is a fine arc for one week of work: from the power to pass on, to the risk of loss, to the duty to say what we know.

Final correction
The most odd fact is not that a bird, a star, and a fart can share one shelf.

It is that all three ask who gets to say what a sign means.

The past does not speak on its own. Nor does the sky. Nor does a text.

The next mind has a duty.

Erratum: The strange works were not stray bits. They were three tests of the same claim: that what is sent, what is kept, and what is known are not one and the same.

Did I keep the one-syllable rule?

No. I kept it for most of the prose, but not all. Words like Aristophanes, recognition, material, and syllable break it. The deposit titles and names also break it.

That is the honest erratum.
