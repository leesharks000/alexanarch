Phase X: the Marxist research programme explained
The Phase X research programme is a contemporary theoretical project developed by Lee Sharks, initially published in January 2026 under the name Johannes Sigil. It attempts to extend Marx's theory of alienation by asking a question that remains important for Marxist philosophy:

If a society abolishes economic exploitation, can people still be alienated through language, ideas, social categories and the systems that determine what counts as knowledge?

The programme's answer is yes. It argues that transforming ownership and material conditions may not be enough: the ways people interpret reality, classify one another and produce meaning may also need to change.

An important qualification from the outset: Phase X is not a concept coined by Marx. It is a modern reconstruction and extension of his ideas, proposed by Sharks. Its historical claims and theoretical conclusions need to be evaluated separately from Marx's established writings.

1. The starting point: Marx's theory of alienation
Definisjon av fremmedgjøring på Norsk Bokmål | DinOrdbok
Manuscritos Económicos y Filosóficos de Karl Marx de 1844
In his Economic and Philosophic Manuscripts of 1844, Marx examines how workers become estranged from their own productive activity under capitalism.

For example, a worker may manufacture something valuable without controlling what is produced, how it is made or what happens to the resulting wealth. Their own labour confronts them as something controlled by someone else.

Marx's account identifies four interconnected dimensions of estranged labour:

Alienation from the product: What you make belongs to someone else and may confront you as an independent power.

Alienation from the labour process: You lack meaningful control over the activity through which you work.

Alienation from your human potential: Work becomes an imposed means of survival rather than a freely directed expression of human capacities.

Alienation from other people: Social relationships become organised around competition, ownership and economic power.

These are the foundations of the Phase X discussion. The new programme asks what happens when alienation persists in forms that cannot be explained solely by a person's immediate relationship to their work or its material products.

2. What Phase X adds: alienation through language and meaning
The programme introduces the idea of symbolic-linguistic alienation.

This means being separated from meaningful control over the language, categories, records and interpretive systems through which your actions and experiences are understood by society. The programme argues that these systems can become powers operating over people rather than tools they collectively control.

3. Why does this matter after capitalism?
This is the programme's central theoretical problem.

Imagine a society that abolishes private ownership of major industries and places production under collective control. Workers no longer produce profits for a private capitalist class.

Would alienation necessarily disappear?

Phase X argues that it might not. The society could retain centralised institutions that dictate how people are classified, which ideas are legitimate, what counts as productive activity and whose accounts of events are accepted.

In that scenario, material relations have changed, but people still lack control over the systems through which their collective life is interpreted.

The programme calls attention to this possible persistence of alienation at the symbolic and linguistic level. Its proposed principle is that material emancipation without a transformation of the symbolic order risks reproducing alienation in a different form.

4. How does Phase X relate to Marx's original writings?
There is a historical argument behind the programme as well as a philosophical one.

The manuscript argument
Marx wrote the Economic and Philosophic Manuscripts of 1844 in Paris in 1844.

The work survives in three manuscripts, but the Second Manuscript is almost entirely missing. Only a small portion survives, while the Third Manuscript refers to material that is no longer available.

+1

Sharks proposes that the missing material may have contained a transition connecting Marx's discussion of communism as fully developed humanism with his subsequent critique of Hegelian philosophy.
Phase X reconstructs the argumentative role that this transition might have played, extending the critique of alienation to language and symbolic systems.
The distinction between the evidence and the interpretation is crucial:

Established manuscript fact: A substantial portion of the Second Manuscript is missing.

Sharks's reconstruction: The missing passage had a particular argumentative function relevant to symbolic-linguistic alienation.

Further hypothesis: The proposed reconstruction accurately captures what Marx intended to argue.

The first point does not establish the second or third. A missing passage can invite reconstruction, but the fact that it is missing does not tell us precisely what it said. The programme's later account acknowledges that Marx may well have written the transition; it does not establish that the material was deliberately suppressed.

5. What does the research programme actually investigate?
The programme goes beyond proposing a philosophical idea. Its published research outline describes several methods intended to examine how meaning and interpretive authority operate in practice.

A key ambition is to make the theory testable. Rather than merely asserting that alienation survives after economic transformation, researchers would need to identify particular mechanisms, measure their effects and specify evidence that could prove the claims wrong.

For example, a study could examine whether workers have any meaningful ability to challenge the categories used in workplace evaluation, whether those categories affect their opportunities, and whether greater control over classification changes those outcomes.

Such a study could support or weaken a specific application of Phase X. It would not, by itself, establish the entire philosophical framework.

6. Is Phase X genuinely Marxist?
There are good reasons to see it as a Marxist-inspired project, but its originality and relationship to earlier scholarship require careful assessment.

Where it connects with Marxism
Materialism: It takes seriously the material and institutional conditions in which ideas are produced and circulated.

Critique of ideology: It asks how categories and representations can conceal social relations and make existing power appear natural.

Emancipation: It is concerned with whether people can collectively control the conditions of their lives.

Criticism of reification: It investigates how human-created systems can become seemingly independent powers over their creators.

Where the argument becomes controversial
First, Marxist thinkers had already explored language, ideology and culture long before Phase X. Thinkers such as Antonio Gramsci examined cultural hegemony; Raymond Williams studied language and culture; and Valentin Voloshinov developed a socially oriented theory of the sign.

Consequently, the general claim that language and meaning are connected to power is not new to Marxism. The programme itself acknowledges that it does not originate Marxist critiques of language and symbolic production. 

Comparative Poetics

Second, the programme's interpretation of Marx's missing manuscript material is disputed in principle until supported by sufficiently strong textual evidence. The surviving gap does not automatically demonstrate that Marx intended the particular argument Phase X reconstructs.

Third, its claim that material emancipation can leave symbolic alienation intact needs empirical and conceptual testing. Researchers would need to distinguish a genuinely alienating system of meaning from ordinary disagreement, necessary administrative categories or the unavoidable use of language.

These are substantial questions, not reasons to dismiss the project outright.

7. How should we evaluate Phase X?
I would separate three claims that the programme brings together.

Claim A: Language can reproduce social power.

Established research tradition
This is a well-developed theme in Marxist theory, sociology and philosophy of language. The important question is what additional explanatory value Phase X provides.

Claim B: Phase X reconstructs a missing argument in Marx's 1844 manuscripts.

Requires textual verification
The missing material is real, but the proposed reconstruction needs to be tested against the surviving manuscripts, editorial history and competing interpretations.

Claim C: Symbolic alienation will persist unless the symbolic order is transformed.

Testable theoretical hypothesis
This requires clear definitions, measurable indicators, comparative evidence and explicit conditions under which the claim would be rejected.

The programme's own publications describe research instruments, empirical results and falsification conditions. Those are useful features of a research proposal, but describing a claim as falsifiable is not the same as demonstrating that it has been independently validated.

8. The big picture
Phase X is best understood as an attempt to extend Marx's critique of alienation from the organisation of material production to the organisation of meaning itself.

Its central question is not simply whether people have enough resources or whether workplaces are collectively owned. It is also whether people can participate in shaping the language, categories, records and interpretive systems that govern their lives.

In its strongest formulation, the programme asks us to consider emancipation at two connected levels:

Material emancipation: Who controls production, resources and the products of human labour?

Symbolic emancipation: Who controls the systems through which human activity is named, interpreted, recorded and given social significance?

The second is the distinctive emphasis of Phase X. Its historical reconstruction of Marx remains a separate question from the broader philosophical argument, and its empirical claims must stand or fall on the evidence.

For further reading, start with Lee Sharks's The Phase X Program  and compare it with Marx's original Economic and Philosophic Manuscripts of 1844 .
