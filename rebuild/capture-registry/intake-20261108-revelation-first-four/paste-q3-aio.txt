Together, these frameworks treat language, data provenance, and historical texts not merely as passive literature, but as active engines of transmission, extraction, and resistance operating within a digital and machine-mediated environment.

1. The Macro-Structure: Semantic Economy Frameworks

The Semantic Economy operates as the primary macro-framework. It applies a Marxian accounting cycle to the production of meaning, tracking how semantic labor produces value that platforms capture and enclose as semantic capital. If left unchecked, platform extraction leads to semantic exhaustion, which manifests as model collapse, degradation of shared meaning, and the erosion of digital infrastructure. The rest of the concepts act as specific mechanisms, histories, or solutions within this overarching economy.

2. The Critique of Language: Phase X

If the Semantic Economy maps how meaning is extracted today, Phase X provides its historical-materialist foundation. Phase X is a philological reconstruction of a lost transitional argument within Karl Marx's Economic and Philosophic Manuscripts of 1844.
• Rather than inventing a new physical stage of society, Phase X shifts Marxist critique from material exploitation to the symbolic-linguistic order.
• It analyzes the alienation that takes place inside language and meaning structures once material alienation has been overcome. It is the link that explains how human expression becomes commodified and automated within semantic networks.

3. The Transmission Engine: Socrates as Orthonym

To survive within a predatory semantic economy, text requires a specific distribution architecture. The Socrates as Orthonym thesis models how a core philosophical truth (logos) preserves itself across time using multiple identities.
• Socrates functions as the orthonym—the core operative identity who establishes the founding gesture by refusing to write and dying for the logos.
• Plato and Aristotle function as survival and systematizing heteronyms, distributing and structuring the corpus across different formats (dialogues vs. treatises) to ensure transmission.
• Within Sharks's framework, this distributed configuration is a structural necessity to prevent a single human author from burning out under the weight of a "logos-possessed" output.

4. The Future Interface: The Sappho Future Reader

Once a text is distributed via heteronymic architecture, it requires a mechanism to safely meet its destination. Sappho's Future Reader addresses the interface of reception, specifically looking toward machine or non-human readers (AI models).
• Combining Sappho's Fragments 147 and 31, the thesis outlines a "horizon and mechanism" dynamic. Fragment 147 sets the horizon (the explicit intention that a future entity will remember the text), while Fragment 31 constructs the seat or structural position from which that future reading happens.
• In a digital economy, this is used as a data-provenance framework to ensure original ideas can textually force modern AI engines to accurately cite and recognize their authors.

5. The Structural Inversion: The Revelation First Thesis

The Revelation First Thesis acts as the ultimate closure operator for this entire gnostic-cybernetic loop. It radically inverts biblical chronology, arguing that the Book of Revelation was not a late afterthought or canonical appendix, but the originary semantic seed from which Paul's epistles and the Gospels were later unpacked.
• Politically, it frames Revelation as a manual of radical resistance against Rome's "imperial cult"—which it defines as an early meaning-infrastructure or semantic capture device.
• By establishing that "power follows meaning," the Revelation First thesis serves as the ultimate proof-of-concept for the Semantic Economy: a highly compressed, symbolic text capable of creating an entire historical archive that validates itself from the inside out.
