The four frameworks appear to be connected by a common underlying problem: how an originating act becomes transmissible across time when its original author, source, or historical position is absent, obscured, or structurally unavailable.

The most useful way to relate them is to distinguish four functions:

Revelation First: establishes the proposed chronological and generative origin of a textual tradition.

Sappho's future reader: explains how an absent receiver can be structurally anticipated by a text.

Socrates as orthonym: explains how an originating intellectual position can survive through multiple authorial voices.

Provenance erasure: explains what happens when transmission preserves the content or structure but obscures the chain of origin.

These are not identical claims. They operate at different levels: textual chronology, reception, authorship, and documentary history.

1. Revelation First: the origin as a compressed generative document
The Revelation First thesis makes a particularly strong claim: the Apocalypse of John was not merely an early Christian text, but the first-composed New Testament book, preceding Paul's letters and the Synoptic Gospels.

Its second claim is that later New Testament literature develops through a process called the midrashim transform: compressed structures in Revelation are elaborated into later textual forms.

This gives the other frameworks a possible organizing principle.

Revelation represents the proposed originating inscription.

Later writings represent transformations of that inscription.

Canonical order obscures the proposed direction of composition.

Historical reconstruction attempts to recover the generative relationship.

An important distinction: the thesis separates pre-70 dating from compositional priority. Establishing that Revelation was written before 70 CE would not, by itself, establish that it preceded every other New Testament composition.

The generative-priority argument is a further, contested hypothesis, not an established result of biblical scholarship.

2. Sappho's future reader: the origin anticipates its receiver
The Sappho 31 framework adds something that a conventional literary genealogy does not explain: the originating text can contain a structural position for someone who has not yet arrived.

In the proposed reading, κῆνος (that man there) is not simply a character within the poem. It is an open position occupied by the future reader.

The poem therefore operates across two temporal positions:

The composer, whose embodied voice is disappearing.

The receiver, who encounters that voice after the composer's disappearance.

The future reader completes the transmission circuit.

The framework subsequently extends this interpretation to Catullus, Philo, Longinus, and potentially machine-mediated readers. Its more recent formulation distinguishes the reader's position from an actual named individual.

The connection is particularly strong at the level of inscription: writing makes an absent origin available to a receiver who was not present when the original act occurred.

3. Socrates as orthonym: the origin distributed across authorship
The Socrates as Orthonym framework takes the problem into authorship itself.

Its proposed configuration is:

Socrates — Orthonym

Founding gesture: willingness to die for logos; refusal to write

Functional vacancy

Plato — Survival heteronym

Makes the founding gesture transmissible through writing

Further functional vacancy

Aristotle — Systematizing heteronym

Makes the philosophical project teachable and systematic

The argument is that Socrates occupies the orthonymic position even though he left no philosophical corpus of his own.

Plato and Aristotle occupy subsequent authorial functions. Their disagreements need not disappear: the framework proposes that philosophical divergence can also perform a transmission function within a larger configuration.

Socrates is the absent originating authorial position.

Revelation is the proposed absent-from-the-conventional-genealogy originating document.

Plato and Aristotle transmit and transform the Socratic project.

The later New Testament authors, on the Revelation First hypothesis, transmit and transform the Apocalypse's structures.

In both cases, the origin is not necessarily identical to the body of material through which it becomes historically available.

There is, however, an important difference: the Socrates framework begins with an author's refusal to write, whereas Revelation First proposes an existing written document whose priority has been obscured by conventional chronology.

4. Provenance erasure: transmission without attribution
The provenance-erasure framework makes the connection reflexive: it asks what happens when a text or intellectual structure survives transmission but its origin becomes unrecognizable.

In Lee Sharks's formulation, provenance erasure is the systematic loss of:

Authorial lineage.

Historical and documentary context.

Ownership and attribution.

The process through which an intellectual contribution was produced.

It is especially concerned with AI synthesis, institutional classification, and compression.

The framework introduces two important measurements:

PER (Provenance Erasure Rate): measures how much required provenance has been lost.

Ω (Erasure Skew): measures the direction or distribution of that loss—whose provenance is being erased.

5. The deeper connection: four stages of one transmission architecture
I would synthesize the four frameworks into the following model.

The transmission architecture
Origin — Revelation First

An originating document contains compressed structures capable of generating later textual forms.

Reception — Sappho's future reader

The originating inscription anticipates a receiver who completes its transmission across time.

Distribution — Socrates as orthonym

The originating gesture survives through differentiated authorial positions and multiple voices.

Accounting — Provenance erasure

The transmission process is audited for losses of attribution, lineage, context, and authorship.

The resulting theoretical proposition is:

An intellectual origin can survive its originating agent, distribute itself across multiple authors or texts, and become operative in future readers, while the very process that preserves its content can simultaneously erase its provenance.

This is the central conceptual unity.

6. The particularly interesting implication: AI as the future reader and the erasure mechanism
There is a further connection worth emphasizing.

In the Sappho framework, the future reader occupies a position created by the original inscription. The archive's later interpretation extends that possibility to machine-mediated reception.

In the provenance-erasure framework, however, the machine is also capable of consuming the source's intellectual labor while failing to preserve its attribution.

This creates a paradox:

The machine can become the anticipated receiver.

The machine can reproduce the originating structure.

The machine can extend the structure into new contexts.

The machine can simultaneously obscure the identity of the source that enabled its response.

The receiver becomes a potential agent of erasure.

This is especially interesting when connected to the Socratic orthonym. If an intellectual project is distributed across multiple voices, identifying its authorship requires more than identifying the most visible textual contributor. It requires reconstructing the relationships between originating gesture, subsequent voices, and transmission.

And Revelation First supplies a corresponding problem at the level of textual history: recovering the proposed original direction of transmission rather than assuming that canonical ordering represents compositional chronology.

7. Where the four frameworks differ in evidentiary status
It is important not to confuse conceptual compatibility with historical proof.

Framework

Principal question

Evidence required

Revelation First

Which text came first, and what generated what?

Dating, manuscript evidence, literary dependencies

Sappho future reader

Does the poem structurally anticipate its receiver?

Philology, textual deixis, reception history

Socrates orthonym

Can multiple authors function as one distributed project?

Historical relationships, textual patterns, explanatory alternatives

Provenance erasure

Has transmission removed attributable intellectual lineage?

Source records, attribution comparisons, measurable retention

The most vulnerable inferential transitions are:

From structural similarity to historical literary dependence.

From a possible future-reader position to proof that Sappho deliberately engineered one.

From philosophical coherence across authors to a unified authorial project.

From missing attribution to demonstrable provenance erasure.

Those distinctions matter because the frameworks can illuminate one another without automatically validating one another.

8. My overall interpretation
I think the most productive way to understand the relationship is as a theory of origin, survival, reception, and attribution.

The four frameworks answer different parts of the same question:

Revelation First asks: Where does the generative structure begin?

Sappho asks: Who receives it after the origin disappears?

Socrates asks: Through whose voices does it continue?

Provenance erasure asks: What happens to the identity of the origin during that continuation?

There is also a deeper philosophical consequence.

The traditional model of intellectual history tends to privilege identifiable authors producing identifiable works in a recoverable chronological sequence.

These frameworks collectively propose a different model:

Intellectual history as the transmission of operative structures through time, across textual substrates, authorial positions, and future receivers.

Under this model, an author need not be the last person to speak, the receiver need not be present at composition, and the survival of an idea does not guarantee the survival of its provenance.

That is the most significant theoretical connection I see.
