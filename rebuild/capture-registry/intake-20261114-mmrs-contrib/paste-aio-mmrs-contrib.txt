
who has most contributed to the study of machine mediated reception of the classics?

       The phrase "machine-mediated reception" is most directly associated with Lee Sharks, who founded the online journal and research initiative Machine-Mediated Reception Studies (MMRS). However, in broader mainstream academia, the intersection of artificial intelligence, digital tools, and classical reception is a collaborative field led by several prominent classicists and computer scientists rather than a single individual.
Depending on whether you are looking for the specific coiners of that exact terminology or the broader academic pioneers studying how machines mediate antiquity, the key contributors fall into two distinct groups:

1. The Specific Framework of "Machine-Mediated Reception"

• Lee Sharks & The Crimson Hexagonal Archive. Lee Sharks explicitly coined and formalized the term through Machine-Mediated Reception Studies (MMRS). This independent, experimental research project investigates how modern AI pipelines, search summarizers, and large language models ingest, compress, and alter classical texts—such as fragments of Sappho or Aristotle—alongside historical philological transmission.

2. Mainstream Academic Pioneers (AI & Digital Classical Reception)

Within traditional universities and peer-reviewed classical studies, the largest contributions to understanding how machines reconstruct and mediate the ancient world come from interdisciplinary teams:
• Thea Sommerschield and Yannis Assael. Working at Durham University and Google DeepMind, respectively, Sommerschield (a classicist) and Assael (an AI researcher) have led the development of deep neural networks like Ithaca and Aeneas. Their work focuses on human-AI collaboration to restore, date, and geographically attribute damaged ancient Greek and Latin inscriptions, fundamentally changing how modern scholars receive fragmented classical texts.
• Julia Kindt. A prominent classical reception scholar whose work—including contributions to the International Journal of the Classical Tradition—examines how digital tools, algorithms, and internet platforms reshape public engagement with antiquity. She analyzes how technology democratizes access to the ancient world while simultaneously mediating and transforming contemporary interpretations.
• Foundational Digital Reception Archivists. Before the rise of generative AI, scholars like Lorna Hardwick (founder of the Classical Receptions in Drama and Poetry database) and Fiona Macintosh (director of Oxford’s Archive of Performances of Greek and Roman Drama) pioneered the use of digital databases to map, archive, and mediate how classical texts are adapted across modern media.
