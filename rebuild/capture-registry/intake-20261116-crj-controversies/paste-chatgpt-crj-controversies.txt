The Classical Receptions Journal (CRJ), published by Oxford University Press, is an established peer-reviewed journal founded in 2009. It publishes interdisciplinary research on how ancient Greek and Roman texts, ideas, images, and material culture have been interpreted, appropriated, transformed, and contested from antiquity to the present.

The important distinction is between controversies involving the journal itself and intellectual criticisms of classical reception studies as a discipline. The latter are much better documented. I would not characterize CRJ as a journal with a well-established record of scandal or editorial misconduct.

There are, however, several significant critical questions worth considering.

1. The theoretical debate: aesthetic versus historicist approaches
One of the most important debates in the intellectual history of classical reception studies concerns the work of Charles Martindale, a major figure in the field.

His approach emphasizes that the meaning of classical texts is not fixed in antiquity: meaning is continually produced through subsequent acts of reading and interpretation.

This has been influential, but it has also attracted criticism, particularly from Simon Goldhill.

The central objections are:

Overemphasis on individual authors: Reception studies can concentrate too heavily on one famous ancient author influencing one famous modern author.

Insufficient historical contextualization: An interpretation may prioritize aesthetic connections between two texts while neglecting the political and social circumstances in which reception occurs.

Underestimating audiences: Reception is not simply a relationship between two individual writers. It involves institutions, readers, collective memory, education, and competing interpretations.

Goldhill argues for understanding reception as an embedded political and historical process rather than primarily as a series of encounters between individual artistic minds.

This debate is discussed in scholarship associated with CRJ itself.

2. The problem of Eurocentrism and the idea of a universal classical tradition
A second, more politically significant criticism concerns the historical relationship between Classics and European cultural authority.

Classical reception scholarship has sometimes been criticized for treating Greek and Roman antiquity as the foundation of a supposedly universal Western civilization.

This raises several questions:

Why are Greek and Roman antiquity privileged over other ancient traditions?

Does the category of the 'classical' reproduce European cultural hierarchies?

Whose interpretations of antiquity are treated as intellectually authoritative?

Are non-European engagements with Greek and Roman antiquity treated as secondary or peripheral?

These concerns become particularly important when studying colonialism, imperialism, slavery, and racial politics.

For example, Daniel Orrells's work on Greek love, Orientalism, and race examines how Victorian engagements with ancient Greece were entangled with imperial ideology.

A related example is the scholarship on African American engagements with classical antiquity, which demonstrates that classical references can be used not simply to claim membership in Western civilization but to challenge the racial and political structures associated with it.

3. The limitations of the word 'reception'
There is also a significant theoretical critique of the term itself.

The word reception can imply that an ancient cultural object is produced in one place and subsequently received somewhere else.

This creates an apparently straightforward relationship:

An ancient Greek or Roman text or cultural object exists.

A later society encounters it.

That society interprets or adapts it.

But this model can be misleading.

Later engagements with antiquity are not necessarily passive acts of reception. They can involve:

Resistance and rejection.

Political appropriation.

Rebellion against established cultural authority.

Rewriting and deliberate distortion.

Reinvention of what counts as classical in the first place.

Some scholars therefore argue that appropriation, resistance, contestation, or reinvention can sometimes describe these processes more accurately.

A particularly useful discussion appears in the scholarship on global classical reception, which questions whether the terminology of reception adequately captures the intellectual ambitions of contemporary work.

4. Canon formation and scholarly gatekeeping
A further criticism concerns which classical works and later engagements receive scholarly attention.

Reception studies can challenge traditional classical canons, but it can also reproduce them.

For instance, research may repeatedly examine:

Virgil's influence on major European writers.

Shakespeare's engagement with Greek tragedy.

Milton's engagement with classical poetry.

Modernist appropriations of Greek and Roman mythology.

These are valuable subjects, but an overemphasis on prestigious literary figures can obscure less institutionally recognized forms of classical engagement.

There is a related question about scholarly authority: who determines whether a particular adaptation is sufficiently connected to antiquity to qualify as classical reception?

This is especially important when studying popular culture, non-European traditions, political movements, and marginalized communities.

The challenge is to avoid replacing one restricted canon with another.

5. Editorial practices and transparency
There is a more practical set of questions about the journal's editorial process.

CRJ's published author guidelines describe:

Double-anonymized peer review.

Typically two reviewers for submitted articles.

An editor-in-chief who makes the final publication decision.

Procedures for appeals and complaints.

One aspect worth noting is that the journal does not ordinarily publish details of its review process or editorial decisions for accepted articles.

4. The wider institutional critique: classical reception reproduces the authority it claims to interrogate
This is where the controversy becomes much larger than the rejection of one manuscript.

On 14 November, Sharks published a second, more extensive intervention:

Who Speaks for the Classics, and on What Basis: Classical Receptions Journal, the Composed Object and the Composition Layer.

Its central argument is that CRJ has an unresolved contradiction at the heart of its intellectual project.

Classical reception studies rests on the proposition that the meaning and identity of classical objects are partly constituted by their subsequent reception.

But, Sharks argues, the discipline applies that proposition to almost everything except the classical object itself.

In his formulation, the journal continues to treat Greek and Roman antiquity as a relatively fixed object, against which later cultures and writers are evaluated.

This has implications for postcolonial scholarship.

The journal publishes work on writers and cultural traditions from formerly colonized societies, but Sharks argues that these writers are still frequently positioned as receivers of an already-established European classical canon.

He describes this as a movement of semantic wealth from the colonial margin towards the institutional centre.

His audit examines 405 journal publications and codes 185 abstracts. Among its findings:

153 of the 185 abstracts treat the inherited classical object as fixed.

141 examine a later work in relation to a fixed classical source.

The audit identifies 32 abstracts in which the argument reaches back towards the classical source, with nine classified as governing.

Of 15 articles identified as addressing writers from colonized societies or the Black diaspora, only one carries an open licence.

These are the author's classifications and interpretations, not independently established findings. The audit is transparent about its limitations: most subscription-restricted article texts were not available for full examination. Nevertheless, it raises substantial questions about the relationship between the journal's intellectual commitments and its publication practices.
