1. The cross-name stylistic results stop being anomalies
If Plato, Aristotle, and Theophrastus are three independent authors, then cross-name affinities require explanation.

If they're differentiated positions within one self-dividing corpus, however, you'd actually expect the boundaries to leak.

Likewise, a Theophrastan text continuing an Aristotelian argument isn't necessarily “influence” or “borrowing.” It can be the corpus changing registers while remaining the same corpus.

2. “Heteronym” is much better than “successor”
“Successor” implies chronology and independent agency:

A finishes → B inherits → C continues.

“Heteronym” implies something quite different:

One textual/intellectual subject differentiates itself into distinct voices.

That's why the Pessoa analogy—if one wanted an analogy—is actually closer than a history-of-ideas model. The heteronyms aren't simply pseudonyms. Their differences are productive differences within a larger authorial machine.

And in this case the machine is doing something especially interesting: it generates philosophical oppositions by dividing itself.

So perhaps:

Plato is not “before Aristotle” in the strongest sense.
Aristotle is not merely “after Plato.”

Rather, Plato/Aristotle is a division the corpus performs.

3. Aristotle's disagreements with Plato become internal differentiation
This also changes the significance of Aristotle's attacks on Plato.

Under the discrete-author model:

Aristotle rejects Plato.

Under the self-dividing-corpus model:

The corpus produces an anti-Platonic position within itself.

That doesn't make the disagreements unreal. Quite the opposite: the disagreements are how the differentiation occurs.

The corpus needs to be capable of saying:

Forms are separate / Forms are not separate.

Knowledge is recollection / knowledge is demonstration.

Dialectical division / syllogistic demonstration.

Mathematics as paradigm / biology as paradigm.

Dialogue / treatise.

One / many.

The heteronyms are the names under which these differentiated operations become legible.

5. This makes “one and many” almost literal
And this may explain why Many and One is more fundamental to the project than it initially appears.

The question isn't merely Aristotle's metaphysical question:

How can many things be one?

It's also a question about the textual corpus:

How can one logos be many without the many ceasing to be one?

The answer isn't “they are secretly identical.”

The answer is closer to:

The one differentiates itself.

And then the many are not external additions to the one. They are the one in its modes of self-differentiation.

That gives you a much more precise formulation of what “heteronymic” means here:

A heteronym is not another person speaking for the corpus. It is the corpus speaking differently from itself.

That, I think, is the formulation you're after.

And it has a major consequence for Socrates.

If we carry the same logic backward, the relevant sequence may not ultimately be

Socrates → Plato → Aristotle → Theophrastus

at all.

It may be:

logos → self-division into Socrates/Plato/Aristotle/Theophrastus.

Then “Socrates” is the first name of the operation, while “Plato,” “Aristotle,” and “Theophrastus” are increasingly differentiated ways in which the same corpus makes its own internal differences visible.

That would also explain why the Alexanarch notion of the orthonym is potentially more radical than merely saying “Socrates didn't write.” The orthonym/heteronym distinction could be describing the corpus's own mechanism of producing authorial multiplicity rather than a distinction between one genuine author and several fictional ones.
