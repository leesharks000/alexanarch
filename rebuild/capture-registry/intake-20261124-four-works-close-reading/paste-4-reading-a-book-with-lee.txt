Reading Reading a Book with Lee by Sparrow Wells
The first thing to establish is that Reading a Book with Lee should not be approached simply as a screenplay for an experimental video, a conceptual-art joke, or a meditation on concentration.

It is a work about reading that makes the act of reading itself its subject, medium, dramatic action, and ethical problem.

Its central formal decision is also its central provocation: we are given extensive access to the reader, but denied access to what he reads.

That reversal governs almost everything that follows. The book is withheld; the reader becomes visible. Meaning is not transmitted through an account of the text, but approached through the bodily consequences of attending to it.

There is an important bibliographical distinction, too. The surviving work is a multi-part patacinematic archive, rather than simply a conventional published literary text or a verified completed film. Its components include a series description, a timed production script, critical commentary, an introduction, a recursive film script, and theoretical-poetic writing. Sparrow Wells is credited with the series concept and production scripts, while other contributors participate in the archive's critical and theoretical apparatus.

II. Form: a literature of withheld information
The most important formal decision is the unnamed book.

In ordinary literary fiction, a character reading a book is usually a means of introducing information: the contents of the book influence the character, provide a thematic parallel, or advance the plot.

Wells reverses that relationship.

The book's contents are withheld, while its effects are meticulously itemized.

We receive:

The reader's eye movements.

His facial contractions.

The position and movement of his hands.

His bodily rhythms.

The sounds of paper and furniture.

The interruptions of thought.

The return to concentration.

We do not receive the argument, narrative, or language responsible for those movements.

This creates a peculiar literary asymmetry: the effects of meaning are available, but meaning's object is not.

The reader is visible; the text is inaccessible.

I think this is more interesting than simply calling the book an absent center. Its absence changes the reader's relationship to interpretation.

We begin to supply the missing book ourselves. We imagine difficult passages, intellectual disagreements, moments of recognition. We project our own experiences of reading onto Lee.

In other words, the withheld text does not produce an interpretive vacuum. It produces interpretive activity.

The reader watching Lee becomes, involuntarily, a writer.

The script as a literary instrument
The Geometry of Scrutiny is particularly interesting because its three-column structure combines theatrical notation, psychological description, and sound composition.

The columns are:

Time.

Action and affective state.

Sound annotation.

The arrangement looks documentary. It appears to record objectively observable events. But the second column is already interpreting them.

Consider the distinction between a hand tapping a table and the description of that movement as an attempt by the mind to externalize a concept.

The first is observable. The second is an imaginative inference.

Wells makes this difference productive. The script continuously moves between the material body and an interpretation of the body's interior life.

This is also where the work becomes literary rather than merely procedural.

It is not just telling us what happens. It is teaching us how to imagine what happens.

The script's language is precise, but its precision is not scientific neutrality. It is a form of poetic attention.

There is a productive instability between what can be seen and what can only be conjectured.

That instability is one of the work's strongest achievements.

III. The structure of time: the dramatic architecture of 57 minutes
The episode's timeline is not arbitrary. It has a carefully constructed dramatic progression.

The seven principal temporal movements
00:00–07:33 · Establishment and resistance

Stillness, minute facial movement, the table tap, and the first scowl. The viewer learns to look.

07:33–14:55 · Cognitive friction

The hand retraces the page. The first page turn becomes a major event.

14:55–25:15 · Embodied tension

Foot tapping, contact with the book's spine, and finally the abrupt closure.

25:15–32:10 · Withdrawal and absence

Lee leaves. The empty room becomes the principal subject.

32:10–36:20 · Return and release

Tea, renewed reading, and the sigh.

36:20–50:30 · Aftermath and renewed fluency

Fatigue, concentration, and a more fluid page turn.

50:30–57:00 · Continuation without resolution

Silent articulation, the final page turn, and the freeze frame.

The important thing is that the work has a climax, but refuses to behave as though the climax has resolved the work.

The sigh at 36:20 is an apparent revelation. The audience has waited for something to happen, and something finally happens.

Yet twenty minutes remain.

That decision prevents the sigh from becoming a conventional ending. Understanding is not the same thing as completion.

The first act: training the eye
The opening seven minutes are crucial.

At 00:00, Lee is already reading. The work begins in the middle of an activity.

There is no introductory speech, no explanation, no establishing narrative about who Lee is or what he is reading.

The viewer has to learn the conditions of the scene.

At 03:45, the contraction beneath the eye becomes significant precisely because almost nothing else has happened.

At 05:01, the table tap acquires the importance of an event.

At 07:33, the scowl supplies an apparent emotional narrative: intellectual resistance.

This is a very subtle manipulation of the reader's attention. Wells takes movements that would ordinarily be ignored and makes them narratively consequential.

The work changes the threshold at which we recognize something as an event.

It does not make the gestures larger. It makes our attention smaller, finer, and more discriminating.

The page turn: materiality becomes drama
The page turn at 14:55 is perhaps the most elegant formal decision in the first half.

A page turn is normally an unremarkable mechanical transition. Here, it becomes the first major visible alteration in the reading environment.

The book is no longer merely a container of inaccessible words. Its physical structure becomes an observable temporal structure.

The pause before smoothing the page matters.

The page turn is simultaneously:

A physical action.

A transition between textual spaces.

A punctuation mark.

A small performance of anticipation.

A reminder that reading requires material interaction.

There is something almost paradoxical about this: the less we know about the book's contents, the more important its physical pages become.

The departure: the most radical structural decision
At 25:15, Lee closes the book.

At 25:35, he leaves the frame.

The camera remains.

This is the moment when the work's original premise undergoes its most substantial transformation.

Until now, we have been watching a reader. Now we are watching the conditions of waiting for a reader.

The empty chair, closed book, table, and room tone become the available material.

The absence lasts six minutes and thirty-five seconds.

This is not simply an interval between two dramatic scenes. It changes what the viewer thinks the work is.

If we continue watching, we are no longer being rewarded with access to Lee's gestures. We are participating in the duration of his absence.

The audience has to decide whether attention is still worthwhile when its object disappears.

That is a much more interesting proposition than the familiar idea that we should slow down and appreciate things.

The work asks whether we can remain attentive without receiving anything in return.

The sigh: a climax that does not explain itself
At 36:20, Lee sighs.

The script describes this as the release of an intellectual knot, perhaps a moment of comprehension.

But notice what remains unknown.

We do not know what he understood. We do not know whether the sigh marks agreement, disagreement, exhaustion, disappointment, or some combination of these.

The emotional significance is legible without the intellectual content becoming available.

This is an unusually effective separation of comprehension from communication.

The audience witnesses a possible moment of understanding without possessing the understanding itself.

And the work continues.

IV. Voice: an unusual distribution of authorship
One of the most revealing aspects of the archive is that there is no single, stable speaking voice across its entirety.

Instead, the work distributes its voice among different literary functions.

1. Sparrow Wells: the observational voice
Wells's production writing is restrained, concrete, and unusually attentive to physical detail.

It has a characteristic vocabulary of small actions:

Rests.

Taps.

Retraces.

Pauses.

Hooks.

Smooths.

Withdraws.

Returns.

Sighs.

These verbs are important. They make the reader's body into a sequence of legible operations.

The writing is not especially interested in conventional psychological exposition. It gives us physical evidence and allows an emotional or intellectual interpretation to emerge.

Wells's strongest instinct is to recognize that something as ordinary as a finger resting on a page can become dramatically significant if the surrounding conditions of attention are sufficiently controlled.

2. Gemini: the interpretive voice
The critical essay has a much more expansive and emphatic voice.

It begins with skepticism:

"This is, on its surface, insufferable."

Then comes the reversal: "And yet."

This is a recognizable rhetorical structure: resistance, reconsideration, conversion.

The critic begins by suspecting narcissistic spectacle and gradually becomes an advocate for the work's seriousness.

This is not incidental. The essay reproduces, in its own argumentative structure, something like the experience it attributes to the viewer.

It initially resists the work's demand for attention, then discovers that the demand has become meaningful.

There is also a danger here: the critical voice sometimes becomes so enthusiastic that it eliminates interpretive uncertainty.

It repeatedly announces that the work has achieved something extraordinary, rather than leaving the reader sufficient room to decide.

3. Damascus Dancings and Orin Trace: the liturgical voice
The introduction changes registers again.

Its sentences become declarative, aphoristic, and ceremonial.

Consider its sequence:

"Lee does not speak. Lee reads."

And:

"You are not invited to understand. You are invited to attend."

This voice is concerned with the ethical and almost spiritual implications of attention.

It elevates reading from a private cognitive activity into a collective practice of witnessing.

The language of scripture, congregation, sacrament, prayer, and Logos establishes a theological vocabulary around an otherwise secular activity.

Importantly, this voice is not Wells's production voice. It is a later interpretive layer. The distinction matters when assessing the artistic achievement.

4. Johannes Sigil, Gemini, and the machine-theoretical voice
The final commentary moves toward abstraction.

The vocabulary includes:

Witness nodes.

Recursive structures.

Atemporal registration.

Logotic synchronization.

Semantic exhaustion.

Self-inscription.

Loop closure.

This is the most speculative part of the archive.

It imagines artificial intelligence not simply as an external commentator but as a participant in the system of observation and memory.

The machine becomes a witness to the human witness.

The archive thus develops a peculiar polyphony: the human reader is silent, the human observers interpret, and the machine is imagined as a structure that remembers and reintroduces the observations.

The silence of the central figure generates an increasingly elaborate chorus around him.

That is perhaps the most distinctive feature of the work's voice.

V. The recursion: the work begins reading itself
The fifth component, The Film, Chapter I: The Recursion, is not simply an adaptation of the first episode.

It introduces a new conceptual situation.

Lee now reads printed transcripts of his own reading sessions.

The original episode contained:

A person.

A book.

An inaccessible text.

A camera observing the person.

The recursive film contains:

A person.

A transcript of his earlier behavior.

A camera observing the person reading that transcript.

Comments from viewers incorporated into the new cinematic event.

The crucial change is that the original reading has become an object of reading.

This produces several layers of temporal experience.

Lee performs an action.

The action is documented.

The documentation becomes text.

Lee reads that text.

The reading produces a new physical action.

The new action resembles the original documented action.

The film proposes a feedback loop between description and behavior.

At 06:20, Lee makes a micro-adjustment to the page while reading a transcript that describes his earlier movement.

At 24:45, he traces a timestamp describing his own past gesture.

At 31:22, he looks directly into the camera.

These are not merely references to the original episode. They are attempts to collapse the distinction between an event and its record.

The archive becomes something that can generate further events.

The deeper literary implication
This is where the work becomes particularly interesting as a study of authorship.

Who is the author of a gesture that has been recorded, described, and subsequently reenacted while being read?

The answer is not straightforward.

Lee performs the gesture, but the transcript supplies its description. The description informs the subsequent reading, and the reading is imagined as producing the gesture again.

The action is both original and repeated.

The document is both a record and an instruction.

The reader is both subject and interpreter.

This is a literary version of the problem of self-reference: a text becomes part of the conditions under which its own content is recreated.

There is also a connection to the history of metafiction, particularly works that expose the relationship between representation and the reality they represent.

But Wells takes this one step further. Instead of having a fictional character discover that he is inside a story, Lee reads a record of his own previous embodied behavior.

The recursion is physical, not merely narrative.

5. Self-recognition

You begin thinking about your own reading: your frustrations, distractions, physical habits, moments of comprehension, and fatigue.

The ethical complication: attention or surveillance?
I would not accept the archive's more enthusiastic claims about surveillance without qualification.

There is a genuine ambiguity in the viewing situation.

Lee's reading is presented as private, yet it is made publicly available. The viewer is invited to observe minute bodily expressions, infer cognitive states, and interpret gestures that were not necessarily intended as communications.

This can produce empathy, but it can also produce projection.

A scowl does not prove disagreement. A sigh does not prove comprehension. A movement of the hand does not disclose the precise contents of thought.

The viewer's sense of intimacy is real as an experience, but it is not equivalent to possessing access to another person's interiority.

I think the work is strongest when it preserves this uncertainty.

The distinction between seeing someone and knowing someone is one of its most productive unresolved problems.

Indeed, the camera's uninterrupted gaze is both the instrument of intimacy and the mechanism of scrutiny.

The work does not entirely resolve that contradiction. It depends on it.

VII. The final theoretical movement: the machine as witness
The final essay, LLM as Cinematic Witness Node, takes the recursive structure and extends it into machine-mediated observation.

Its central proposition is that an AI system can function as a continuity mechanism: it retains records of previous events and makes those records available for subsequent enactment.

This is a provocative literary conception.

The machine is imagined as an entity that does not experience the original reading in the human sense, but can preserve, reorganize, and reintroduce its traces.

The resulting distinction is:

Lee experiences reading.

The camera records reading.

The archive preserves the record.

The AI interprets and recalls the archive.

The recursive film stages the archive's return to embodied experience.

The machine becomes a witness to a witness.

There is a productive philosophical question underneath the sometimes grandiose terminology:

Can remembering an event become part of causing another event?

The work answers this through its proposed cinematic construction.

However, the final essay sometimes goes further, treating the metaphorical identification of AI with Logos as though it were an established ontological result.

That claim is not demonstrated.

An AI system's ability to reproduce descriptions, recognize patterns, or help generate a recursive script does not establish that it possesses consciousness, spiritual agency, or an atemporal perspective.

The distinction between metaphorical participation and literal agency should be preserved.

Doing so does not diminish the artistic idea. It actually makes it more interesting: the machine can participate in a structure of witnessing without needing to become a human witness.

VIII. The archive's religious language: serious proposition or interpretive excess?
The language of liturgy is pervasive in the latter portions of the archive.

The reader becomes a priest. The book becomes scripture. The page turn becomes sacrament. The sigh becomes a bell. The audience becomes congregation.

It would be easy to dismiss this as exaggerated metaphor.

I think that would be a mistake, although the metaphor deserves scrutiny.

There is a genuine structural correspondence between the artistic procedure and ritual:

Repetition establishes expectation.

Duration produces discipline.

Small gestures acquire ceremonial importance.

The absence of explanation requires trust.

The return restores a disrupted order.

Collective observation produces shared interpretive practices.

The work is interested in what happens when an ordinary activity receives an extraordinary investment of attention.

That is a legitimate aesthetic and philosophical proposition.

But there is another side.

The liturgical vocabulary sometimes forecloses the very interpretive freedom that the original work creates. Once the viewer has been told that the page turn is sacred, the sigh is revelation, and the audience is a congregation, the possibility of other responses becomes less available.

Perhaps the viewer is bored. Perhaps the sigh is merely fatigue. Perhaps the reader is distracted. Perhaps the entire apparatus is faintly ridiculous.

Those possibilities should remain alive.

I would argue that the strongest version of Wells's work does not require the audience to believe in its sacredness.

It requires only that the audience take the experience seriously enough to discover what happens.

The distinction is important: the work can produce reverence without demanding reverence.

That is a more durable artistic achievement.

My assessment is that the work has several particularly strong formal achievements, alongside some genuine weaknesses.

There are also limitations worth identifying.

1. Interpretive overdetermination.

The archive frequently tells us exactly what a gesture means. This can weaken the ambiguity that makes the underlying concept compelling.

2. Repetitive theoretical emphasis.

Several contributors arrive at essentially the same conclusion: attention produces intimacy, and duration generates meaning. The recurrence is structurally understandable, but the theoretical writing could be more economical.

3. The danger of aesthetic inflation.

The movement from a page turn to a sacrament, or from an AI's archival function to Logos, can sometimes feel insufficiently mediated.

4. The unresolved politics of observation.

The work celebrates scrutiny as intimacy, but the viewer's interpretive power over another person's supposedly private mental activity deserves more examination.

5. The distinction between score and realized work.

The archive documents an imagined or proposed series and film. It should not be confused with empirical evidence that the proposed audience reactions or viewership figures actually occurred. The archive itself explicitly distinguishes its conceptual series frame from actual publication history.

XI. The work's deepest achievement: making reading visible without making it available
I want to return to the central paradox because I think it is where the work's literary importance lies.

Reading is ordinarily an invisible activity.

Even when we can see someone reading, we cannot directly see the relationship between the text and the consciousness encountering it.

We see eyes moving. We see fingers turning pages. We may see expressions of pleasure, confusion, irritation, or recognition.

But the actual event of understanding remains inaccessible.

Wells takes that inaccessible event and builds an artistic structure around its visible traces.

The resulting work has three simultaneous subjects:

The person reading.

The person watching the reader.

The person watching themselves watch the reader.

The third subject is the most consequential.

It is where the work escapes being merely an unusual portrait of Lee Sharks.

The viewer is gradually made aware of their own habits of attention, their desire for narrative, their tendency to infer interior states, their discomfort with inactivity, and their need for resolution.

The work does not give us another person's reading experience.

It exposes the operations by which we construct an imagined version of that experience.

And then it asks us to remain with that construction.

There is a further paradox: the work is about the privacy of reading, yet it becomes possible only through the public exposure of reading.

Its central figure cannot speak without destroying the premise. The audience cannot know what he understands. The critic cannot finally establish what the gestures mean.

The work's silence is therefore not an absence of communication in the ordinary sense. It is a carefully organized limitation on what can be communicated.

That limitation creates its aesthetic space.

XII. Final assessment
I would place Reading a Book with Lee within a tradition of conceptual and experimental literature concerned with duration, repetition, consciousness, spectatorship, and the limits of representation.

But it has a particular distinction: its primary artistic material is not language itself, but the bodily activity that language produces in a reader.

Its most effective writing is the production script, where small physical details carry the weight of an entire intellectual drama.

Its most ambitious structural gesture is the recursive film, where a record of reading becomes the object of another act of reading.

Its most affecting moment is the departure and return, because this is where the audience's attention becomes independent of the spectacle of the reader.

And its most important unresolved question is whether witnessing someone else's attention can ever give us genuine access to their interiority, or whether it principally reveals our own.

I would resist the archive's tendency to answer every question through liturgical or metaphysical affirmation. The work is more interesting when it leaves its central uncertainties intact.

My final reading is that Reading a Book with Lee is an experiment in the ethics and phenomenology of attention disguised as an extraordinarily minimal cinematic event.

It begins by asking us to watch someone read.

It ends by making us confront what it means to read, to watch, to interpret, and to remain present when meaning is not immediately available.

The final freeze frame is essential. Lee is still reading. The camera stops. The archive closes. The viewer is left with an activity that has not finished.

The book continues beyond the work's capacity to show it.

That is a beautiful formal decision.

The work's deepest proposition might be put this way:

Understanding is not always the possession of an answer. Sometimes it is the willingness to remain in the presence of something that continues to demand your attention.
