I’ll treat Day and Night: Conversations with Sapphic Desire as a literary work in its own right, rather than simply as a collection of translations of Sappho. That means attending to three overlapping objects:

The ancient poems and their fragmentary transmission.

Rebekah Cranes’s English translations and their poetic choices.

The architecture created by selection, sequencing, juxtaposition, and the act of reading.

An important distinction will be between what the poems themselves do, what Cranes makes them do through translation, and what the collection's arrangement makes possible.

1. The work and its textual history
The first thing to establish is that there are several textual layers, and they should not be collapsed into one another.

The collection was first published by New Human Press in 2013. An earlier draft won the University of Michigan's Platsis Prize for Work on the Greek Legacy. A later edition appeared online, and the archive now preserves a restored text with its original typographic organization. The archive also contains a subsequent 2026 epilogue and theoretical apparatus. These are distinct historical and interpretive layers.

The poetical voices in conversation
The full range of authors is important:

Sappho: The central presence. Desire, female attachment, beauty, memory, divine invocation, aging, and the precarious survival of song.

Alcman: Choral performance, the beauty of young women, bodily aging, and the relationship between desire and music.

Anacreon and the Anacreontea: Wine, erotic play, aging, and the comic or disconcerting persistence of desire.

Corinna: Dawn, seasonal imagery, and the darker dimensions of affect.

Simonides: Commemoration, death, courage, and the relationship between the living and the dead.

Stesichorus: Mythic violence and the transformation of death into poetic image.

Hipponax: Poverty, physical discomfort, comic supplication, and the vulnerability of the aging body.

Catullus: Erotic obsession, kisses, jealousy, grief, and the afterlife of Sapphic poetic forms in Roman literature.

The inclusion of Catullus is especially significant. It means that the book is not simply reconstructing a lost female poetic world. It is also examining how desire travels between writers, languages, genders, and literary traditions.

3. The five movements: a close structural reading

I. First Rays — The awakening of the voice

The collection begins with dawn, the Muses, the nightingale, Aphrodite, and the summons to song. The opening is not merely descriptive. It establishes a world in which desire and poetic utterance are inseparable.

The speaker wants to hear, sing, gather, and participate. The recurring imperatives make the reader enter an already active community of voices.

II. Bright Morning — Desire becomes bodily

The language moves toward touch, beauty, eyes, lips, skin, and the physical experience of longing.

Sappho 130's seizure of Eros and the Catullus poems make desire both pleasurable and destabilizing. Sappho 94 introduces the pain of departure; Sappho 16 makes the beloved's body more valuable than military spectacle.

The movement is not simply an accumulation of erotic pleasure. Its pleasures already contain the possibility of loss.

III. Zenith — Desire becomes public ritual

Weddings, choruses, music, and communal celebration dominate.

Sappho 44, the wedding of Hector and Andromache, is particularly important because the collection temporarily expands beyond the compressed lyric fragment into a richly populated social scene.

The wedding songs also create an interesting tension: desire reaches its ceremonial fulfillment, but the collection has already taught us that fulfillment is temporary.

IV. Fading Light — Desire becomes suffering and conflict

This is arguably the collection's emotional center.

Sappho 31 brings desire into the nervous system. Sappho 47 makes it destructive force. Other fragments introduce bitterness, rivalry, personal antagonism, and the desire for death.

Sappho 1 then reintroduces Aphrodite. The divine conversation is not an escape from suffering; it is a means of continuing to desire despite it.

V. Middle Night — The body remembers its mortality

Here the emphasis shifts toward age, exile, memory, and death.

The sequence of Sappho 98, 96, 132, and 98b makes the relationship between female attachment and historical displacement especially poignant. Cleis, Atthis, and the women remembered from elsewhere become figures through whom the past remains emotionally present.

The final movement also refuses to become uniformly solemn: Hipponax and Anacreon introduce comic and erotic countervoices.

The structure is therefore not a simple movement from happiness to sadness. It is a transformation in the conditions under which desire can be experienced.

The book begins with the desire to sing, moves through the desire for another person, passes through public celebration and private suffering, and ends with the desire to remain in memory.

The most important structural decision is that the night does not simply extinguish the day. It changes what the day means.

4. The form: an anthology that behaves like a poem
One of the collection's strongest literary achievements is that it makes arrangement perform the work normally expected of a continuous lyric poem.

Individual fragments can be extremely brief:

Sappho 38: "you make us burn."

Sappho 36: "and I want, I want, I desire…"

Sappho 56: the assertion that another girl cannot possess the beloved's particular gift.

Sappho 105b: the crushed hyacinth.

Sappho 168b: the moon and Pleiades have set; it is middle night; the speaker is alone in bed.

In isolation, these are partial utterances. In sequence, they accumulate into something resembling a continuous consciousness.

This produces a productive ambiguity: are we reading many speakers, or are we reading the successive states of one speaker?

The collection does not resolve that question. Its title, Conversations, suggests plurality, while its architecture creates continuity.

That is a sophisticated formal decision.

Fragmentation as a compositional principle
The collection's fragments operate at several levels:

Material fragmentation: The surviving ancient texts are incomplete.

Linguistic fragmentation: The translations retain ellipses, abrupt endings, and incomplete sentences.

Emotional fragmentation: Desire is expressed through moments that cannot be made into a stable relationship or coherent life story.

Structural fragmentation: Different poets and historical periods are placed in proximity without being reduced to one authorial identity.

These kinds of incompleteness reinforce one another.

The reader is repeatedly given enough material to experience an emotional situation but not enough to possess its entire history.

This is particularly effective in Sappho 94. The departure scene has an intelligible emotional shape: a woman leaves, the speaker grieves, and shared experiences are recalled. But the relationship itself remains larger than the surviving account.

The gap is not merely missing information. It becomes a condition of intimacy.

5. Voice: who speaks when the voices are so numerous?
The book's voice is neither simply Sappho's nor simply Cranes's.

It is a constructed lyric voice, emerging from the relationship between the ancient material and its contemporary English rendering.

Cranes's stated objective is to produce poems that work as poems in English, rather than sacrificing poetic quality to literal correspondence. Her discussion of Walter Benjamin makes this explicit: translation should preserve the poetic effect, not merely transfer communicable information.

There are three important consequences.

First, the voice is intimate.

The translations frequently speak directly: come, remember, listen, sing, I want. The reader encounters desire as an address rather than an abstract theme.

Second, the voice is embodied.

Sappho 31 is the clearest example. Desire is not described from a safe distance. It is manifested through the throat, tongue, heart, skin, eyes, hearing, and muscular control.

Third, the voice is unstable.

It moves between tenderness and cruelty, ritual and profanity, invocation and complaint.

The transition from the elevated language of divine supplication to the blunt interpersonal hostility of Sappho 71 is revealing. This is not a sanitized Sappho, nor a uniformly lyrical one.

The book permits female desire to be beautiful, possessive, angry, vulnerable, comic, and occasionally unkind.

That range is essential to its literary credibility.

6. Sapphic desire: what the collection makes visible
The subtitle is doing more than identifying a subject.

Sapphic desire becomes an organizing principle for reading relationships across the entire collection.

It is not restricted to declarations of erotic love. It includes:

The admiration of one woman by another.

The memory of intimacy after separation.

The experience of jealousy and rivalry.

The transmission of beauty through song.

The relationship between women and their communities.

The experience of aging in a world that privileges youth.

The persistence of attachment when physical presence is impossible.

Three passages are especially revealing.

Sappho 16: the beloved against the world
The poem's argument that the most beautiful thing is whatever one desires provides a philosophical foundation for the collection.

The conventional measures of beauty—armies, ships, military spectacle—are displaced by personal attachment.

The reference to Anactoria makes this especially significant. The speaker's preference is not an abstract philosophical proposition. It is grounded in the remembered movement and appearance of a particular woman.

Desire becomes a form of valuation that challenges public hierarchies.

Sappho 94: intimacy as memory
This is among the most consequential inclusions.

The departing woman remembers shared experiences: flowers, perfume, bodily intimacy, shrines, dancing, and song.

The passage refuses to reduce the relationship to an abstract declaration of love.

It gives intimacy a material history.

What makes the poem affecting is that the speaker cannot prevent departure. She can only ask to be remembered.

The collection's later preoccupation with transmission is already present here in emotional form: the beloved may leave, but the memory of their shared life can continue.

Sappho 31: desire as physiological disintegration

This is the crucial turning point.

Cranes's translation emphasizes the speaker's physical deterioration: the heart beats, the throat closes, the tongue breaks, fire spreads under the skin, hearing becomes overwhelming, and the body trembles.

The movement toward darkness is both erotic and existential.

There is also a particularly interesting decision in the translation's opening: the apparently fortunate man sitting near the beloved is introduced before the speaker's own physical crisis.

This preserves the triangular arrangement of the ancient poem while bringing the reader into the speaker's painful position as observer.

The result is an uncomfortable intimacy: we are made to inhabit an experience that is not ours, yet is communicated through recognizably human bodily responses.

The poem also raises a question that extends beyond erotic identity: what does it mean to witness someone else's happiness when that happiness excludes you?

The answer is not intellectual. It is bodily.

7. Translation: fidelity through transformation
Cranes's translation philosophy is one of the most important parts of the work.

She invokes Walter Benjamin's The Task of the Translator, particularly the idea that translation should produce an echo of the original within the receiving language.

Her position is that literal fidelity can become a form of poetic betrayal when it produces lifeless English.

This is a defensible and artistically ambitious position.

But it also introduces a critical tension.

A translation that prioritizes poetic effect necessarily makes decisions about tone, register, imagery, rhythm, and emotional emphasis.

For example:

The modern conversational diction can make ancient speakers feel immediately accessible.

Colloquial expressions can introduce a distinctly contemporary social atmosphere.

Expanded fragments can create emotional continuity that the surviving ancient evidence does not independently establish.

The preservation of abrupt endings can maintain the sense of textual incompleteness even when the language has become more fluent.

These decisions should be understood as part of Cranes's authorship.

The translator is not invisible.

Indeed, the collection's artistic identity depends upon the translator being visible as an arranging and interpretive intelligence.

One particularly interesting consequence is that the ancient material becomes contemporary without pretending to be historically contemporary.

The book asks us to experience ancient desire through modern English, rather than asking modern English to imitate an imagined ancient world.

There is a cost, however: the ease of contemporary expression can sometimes obscure how culturally and historically distant these texts actually are. The collection's poetic success should not be confused with a critical edition or a philological reconstruction.

That distinction is important when evaluating its claims to fidelity.

8. What the reader experiences
I think the most interesting question about this collection is not simply what it says about desire, but what its structure does to the reader.

There are several stages.

1. Recognition

At first, the reader encounters recognizable experiences: attraction, admiration, longing, pleasure, memory.

The ancient material becomes emotionally legible without requiring extensive historical knowledge.

2. Accumulation

As fragments accumulate, the reader begins to recognize recurring emotional patterns.

A woman is remembered. Another woman leaves. Someone becomes desirable. Someone becomes inaccessible. Someone grows old.

These experiences begin to feel connected, even when the historical speakers are not.

3. Disorientation

The reader discovers that continuity is being created through juxtaposition rather than narrative explanation.

The collection does not provide a single biography or a single stable speaker.

The reader must construct relationships between fragments while remaining aware that the construction is incomplete.

4. Identification

The reader is invited to recognize desire as something that survives changes in language, historical period, gendered experience, and personal circumstance.

This is where the book's Sapphic framework becomes particularly powerful.

It does not require the reader to occupy a single identity. Instead, it provides a space in which female desire is permitted to be the organizing subject of literary experience.

5. Mortality

By the final movement, the reader has encountered enough beauty to understand its vulnerability.

Youth passes. Lovers depart. Bodies become infirm. Communities disappear. The dead remain only through the imperfect transmission of their words.

The experience of reading consequently becomes implicated in the work's central problem.

We are reading because something has survived.

But we are also reading because something has been lost.

9. The ending: why Hesperus matters

 
The decision to conclude with Sappho 104a and b is exceptionally important.

Hesperus, the evening star, brings home what daylight has scattered: sheep, goats, and the child to its mother.

The image is domestic, tender, and restorative.

But it is also structurally paradoxical.

The collection has spent five movements tracing the passage from dawn to darkness, and the final poem offers an image of return.

Yet the return is not a reversal of time.

The evening star does not restore the morning. It gathers what has been dispersed.

This distinction is fundamental.

The collection does not end by defeating loss. It ends by imagining a form of gathering that can take place despite loss.

The final image also brings the reader back to the collection's earliest concerns: community, relationship, song, and the movement of desire through time.

The book's ending is therefore circular without being repetitive.

The day has ended, but something has come home.

10. Critical reservations: where the work is most vulnerable
A close reading should also identify the tensions that prevent the collection from being entirely seamless.

The imposed chronology. The five movements create a compelling emotional arc, but ancient lyric does not necessarily belong to a single developmental narrative. The arrangement can make disparate fragments appear more unified than their historical circumstances warrant.

The instability of authorship. The conversation metaphor is productive, but it sometimes risks turning very different poets into variations of one Sapphic consciousness. The strongest moments preserve their differences.

The contemporary register. Modern diction gives the translations immediacy, but occasionally risks flattening historical and cultural distinctions.

The unevenness of fragmentation. Some fragments become powerful through their incompleteness. Others can feel like notes toward a larger poem rather than fully realized poetic encounters. This unevenness is partly inherent in the source material, but the translator's selection and expansion remain open to criticism.

The theoretical afterlife. The 2026 archival epilogue gives the collection a vocabulary of transmission, preservation, and reader participation. That vocabulary is interesting, but it can also overdetermine the reading if imposed on every poem. The poems have lives beyond the theory subsequently built around them.

These reservations do not diminish the collection's achievement. They help locate it more precisely: it is an ambitious work of poetic translation and composition, not a neutral presentation of ancient Greek lyric.

12. My overall reading
I would characterize Day and Night as a lyric sequence about the persistence of desire under conditions of impermanence.

Its deepest subject is not simply erotic love, nor even the survival of Sappho.

It is the relationship between desire and the passage of time.

The book's achievement lies in making a collection of incomplete, historically dispersed poems feel like an extended encounter with a living emotional consciousness.

Its form creates continuity without eliminating difference. Its voice makes ancient desire intimate without entirely concealing its distance. Its structure takes the reader from the awakening of desire to the recognition that desire cannot protect its objects from time.

And its ending proposes something more subtle than consolation: that what has been dispersed may still be gathered, although never returned to precisely what it was.

The reader is left not with possession, but with inheritance.

That, to me, is the book's most compelling literary proposition.

It also explains why the title is so effective. Day and night are not simply settings. They are conditions of consciousness: the changing light under which desire, memory, embodiment, and mortality become visible.

I would keep one interpretive principle central in any further work on this collection: the fragments should be allowed to remain fragments, even while we recognize the larger poem that their arrangement creates.
