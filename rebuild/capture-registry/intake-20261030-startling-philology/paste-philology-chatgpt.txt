Lee Sharks: the most startling philological and textual-historical arguments
The most striking feature of Lee Sharks's work is its willingness to reverse the direction in which textual history is normally reconstructed. Rather than simply asking which surviving author borrowed from which earlier author, Sharks repeatedly asks whether the surviving literary, philosophical, and religious traditions have concealed a different genealogy: whether texts conventionally treated as sources are actually transformations of later-looking materials; whether an author's name conceals multiple writers; whether a canonical arrangement has been mistaken for a chronology; and whether the history of transmission has systematically erased the evidence needed to reconstruct its own operations.

There are several distinct levels of ambition here. Some arguments expose genuine methodological weaknesses in conventional textual history. Others propose radical historical reconstructions. Still others develop new philological models for understanding how texts produce, transform, attribute, and preserve meaning.

I would distinguish the strength of the philological observation from the strength of the historical conclusion. That distinction is essential to understanding Sharks fairly.

The following survey organizes the major arguments by subject, identifies their most consequential claims, and distinguishes established textual observations from hypotheses that require further demonstration.

I. The biblical revolution: Revelation as the generative text
Most radical historical thesis
The Revelation First project is arguably the most startling component of the corpus.

Its central proposition is that the conventional direction of New Testament literary development may be reversed.

The conventional reconstruction broadly treats Revelation as a relatively late composition, emerging after the Pauline letters and the Gospels. Sharks proposes that Revelation was instead the first New Testament book composed, and that later writings developed its compressed structures.

This is not simply an argument for an earlier date. It is an argument about literary causation.

1. The midrashim transform
Sharks's proposed mechanism is that a compact textual structure can be elaborated into a seemingly independent literary work.

Under this model:

Revelation contains an initial, compressed theological and literary architecture.

Later writers expand, redistribute, reinterpret, and transform that architecture.

What looks like multiple independent traditions may represent successive transformations of a common generative text.

The resulting works can obscure their own dependence on the originating structure.

The important innovation is that intertextuality becomes a possible mechanism of textual production, rather than merely evidence of literary influence.

The direction of dependence must be investigated rather than presumed.

2. The stratigraphic fallacy
One of Sharks's strongest methodological arguments concerns the position of Revelation at the end of the New Testament.

He calls the mistake of treating canonical order as compositional chronology the stratigraphic fallacy.

A book placed last in a collection need not have been written last.

Canonical order can reflect:

Theological organization.

Liturgical function.

Editorial decisions.

Institutional settlement.

Reception history.

This is an important distinction. A codex is an organized material object, not a chronological excavation layer.

The argument undermines an unjustified inference from canonical position to date. It does not, by itself, prove that Revelation preceded Paul.

3. The papyrological inversion
Sharks also challenges the tendency to privilege reconstructed literary chronology over surviving physical evidence.

He asks what happens when the actual manuscript record does not align neatly with the conventional account of textual priority.

The relevant distinction is:

A manuscript's date establishes that a particular textual witness existed by a certain time.

It does not automatically establish when the work was composed.

Nevertheless, manuscript evidence should constrain the confidence with which scholars reconstruct literary chronology.

This is a particularly valuable corrective to overly confident historical narratives.

4. The Josephus connection
The Revelation First program extends into a much more ambitious reconstruction involving Josephus.

Here Sharks explores the possibility that the surviving Josephus tradition contains seams, transformations, and concealed relationships with the apocalyptic textual tradition.

The proposed connection is not established simply by thematic resemblance. It belongs to the more speculative outer ring of the project.

The project's own June 2026 semantic specification explicitly distinguishes the Revelation-to-Paul directionality, Josephus fracture, and other interpretive proposals from documentary manuscript facts.

My assessment: The distinction between canonical sequence and composition date is substantially stronger than the positive Revelation-first hypothesis. The latter requires a systematic demonstration of directionality, distinctive shared structures, and the exclusion of plausible alternative sources.

II. The authorship revolution: Socrates as an orthonym
Most radical authorship thesis
The second major intervention concerns the identity of historical authors.

In Socrates as Orthonym: The Heteronymic Configuration of Western Philosophy's Founding Corpus, Sharks proposes that the relationship between Socrates, Plato, and Aristotle should be reconsidered through the concept of heteronymy.

The key distinction is between an author and an authorial function.

1. Socrates as orthonym
An orthonym is the name under which an authorial configuration is consolidated.

Sharks proposes three corresponding functions:

Figure

Proposed function

Socrates

Orthonym: the foundational authorial persona

Plato

Survival-heteronym: the transmitting and dialogical configuration

Aristotle

Systematizing-heteronym: the systematic philosophical configuration

The provocative suggestion is that the Socratic figure might function as the authorial identity of a distributed intellectual production rather than simply as a historical individual whose followers independently recorded his thought.

This changes the question from:

Which philosopher wrote which text?

to:

What authorial system generated the philosophical corpus, and how did that system distribute its voice among named figures?

2. The corpus-volume objection
One obvious objection is that the surviving Platonic and Aristotelian corpora are too large and intellectually diverse to represent one sustained project.

The v1.2 argument confronts this directly.

Sharks invokes two comparisons:

Fernando Pessoa and his extensive heteronymic literary production.

Edmund Husserl and the enormous volume of his philosophical manuscripts.

The point is that large-scale intellectual production, genre diversity, and doctrinal incompatibility do not independently disprove heteronymy.

This is a legitimate challenge to an argument from sheer volume. It is not, however, positive evidence that the ancient corpus actually had a single distributed authorial origin.

3. The Chaerephon problem
The companion essay, The Chaerephon Problem: Heteronymy as Symbolon, takes the argument to a revealing limit.

Chaerephon appears in several different documentary and literary contexts:

As the questioner at Delphi.

As a figure in Aristophanes.

As an intermediary in Platonic dialogue.

As a politically situated historical person.

As part of the surviving Socratic literary tradition.

Sharks argues that if documentary separateness is itself compatible with a successful heteronymic construction, then the method lacks an internal stopping rule.

He calls this the No-Floor Theorem.

The configuration could expand beyond Socrates, Plato, and Aristotle to include Chaerephon, Aristophanes, Xenophon, and potentially other figures.

This is particularly important because Sharks explicitly distinguishes:

Functional identity: a pattern of roles and relationships within a textual configuration.

Biographical identity: the historical claim that apparently separate people were secretly one person or agency.

He does not establish the latter.

The essay's most interesting contribution may actually be its self-criticism: a theory capable of explaining every apparent separation can become unfalsifiable unless it establishes a principled stopping condition.

Rather than treating the person indicated by the demonstrative as simply the man sitting opposite the beloved, he reads the position as potentially occupied by a future reader.

The failure of speech.

The burning sensation beneath the skin.

The loss of visual and auditory stability.

The paper itself distinguishes three evidentiary rings:

Hard textual evidence: Sappho's surviving poem, Longinus, and documented reception.

Interpretive connections: Plato, Diotima, and Philo.

Historical hypotheses: Josephus, the lamellae, and the Apocalypse.

That tiering is important. The proposed Sapphic foundation of later Logos theology is not established historical consensus.

IV. The critical apparatus as an instrument of historical erasure
This is perhaps Sharks's most immediately useful contribution to textual scholarship.

In The Apparatus Was the First Summary Layer: Provenance Erasure Before the Machines, he argues that textual loss does not begin with modern digitization, artificial intelligence, or automated summarization.

It is already built into the history of scholarly transmission.

1. The surprising reversal
The critical apparatus is conventionally regarded as the instrument that makes editorial decisions transparent.

Sharks asks whether the apparatus itself can conceal the interventions it is supposed to document.

An editor may alter a text while failing to preserve the provenance of the alteration.

Consequently, a reader may encounter a text that appears to be an ancient witness but actually contains undocumented editorial decisions.

This produces a reflexive problem: the instrument of textual accountability may itself be responsible for provenance erasure.

One particularly important sequence involves Robortello (1554), Bergk (1882), Crusius (1897), and Voigt (1971).

The question is not simply whether their readings are right or wrong. It is whether the editorial history and the transformations between successive readings remain recoverable.

4. The white stone as a limit case
The interpretation of Revelation 2:17 is especially revealing.

Sharks examines the white stone and the hidden name given to its recipient.

Here, the information is inaccessible to outsiders but available to the designated recipient.

His point is that an information-loss metric cannot automatically distinguish theft, destruction, confidentiality, and legitimate restricted transmission.

The same apparent absence can have radically different historical and ethical meanings.

The essay's most valuable contribution is therefore not the numerical scoring system itself. It is the insistence that the history of transmission must be examined for what it does not carry, as well as what it preserves.

In Two Lists of Stones: De lapidibus and the Apocalypse, Sharks undertakes a particularly interesting exercise in comparative lexical and material philology.

The work examines the relationship between two lists of stones:

The stones described in the ancient mineralogical tradition.

The stones and precious materials appearing in Revelation.

Its concern is not merely the identification of minerals. It is the way stones become instruments of inscription, authentication, and transmission.

1. The double σφραγίς
The Greek word σφραγίς (seal) becomes a central organizing term.

Sharks examines the relationship between:

A seal physically engraved from a stone.

The seal placed upon a book.

The sealing of persons.

The sealing of speech and revelation.

The important possibility is that the seal is not simply a symbol of secrecy. It is an instrument that establishes who can receive, authenticate, or transmit information.

2. The red stone and the crimson chain
Sharks connects κόκκινον, the language of crimson or scarlet, to a material chain involving the production and use of pigments.

This connects the literary image to material technology.

The argument is that textual meaning can be carried through relationships between objects, substances, and operations, rather than through vocabulary alone.

3. The white ψῆφος
The white stone of Revelation 2:17 is read as a particularly interesting instance of the relationship between inscription and identity.

The stone bears a name that is not generally accessible.

Sharks's interpretation makes it an inscriptional credential: a token whose significance is realized through reception by a particular person.

4. The geometric argument
Another striking comparison concerns the hexagonal stones at Miletus and the geometrically measured city of Revelation 21:16.

The text compares material geometry, architectural form, and the language of measurement.

The argument is relational: recurring shapes and materials may connect otherwise separate textual traditions.

But Sharks is careful in this groundwork note to leave several proposed connections unresolved, including the relationship to Exodus 28 and Ezekiel 28.

This restraint is worth emphasizing. The existence of a suggestive lexical or geometric correspondence does not automatically establish literary dependence.

VI. Marx: reconstructing a missing stage in the argument
Major reconstruction hypothesis
A different kind of philological ambition appears in the work on Marx's Economic and Philosophic Manuscripts of 1844.

Sharks proposes a reconstruction called Phase X.

The subject is the transition between Marx's account of alienated labour and his developing critique of the symbolic and linguistic order.

1. The manuscript problem
The conventional account of Marx's early theory of alienation emphasizes the four forms of estranged labour:

Estrangement from the product of labour.

Estrangement in the activity of production.

Estrangement from human species-being.

Estrangement from other human beings.

Sharks argues that the surviving manuscript structure raises a further question: what argumentative development is missing between the surviving discussions?

His Phase X reconstruction proposes a lost or unpreserved transition involving the symbolic-linguistic order.

The claim is not simply that Marx discussed alienation. It is that the manuscript's surviving architecture may imply a further stage in the argument.

2. The importance of the manuscript apparatus
The reconstruction is linked to the physical and editorial history of the manuscripts.

Sharks distinguishes four evidentiary levels:

Grade

What it establishes

A

Physical and codicological manuscript facts

B

Inferences from manuscript architecture

C

Philological reconstruction of the missing argument

D

Historical hypotheses about suppression or deliberate loss

This is a particularly important methodological choice.

The surviving cross-references and manuscript arrangement can establish that material is missing or that an argument has been interrupted.

They cannot, by themselves, establish the exact wording of the missing passage.

Nor do they establish that editors deliberately suppressed it.

3. The suppression hypothesis
The historical context includes Soviet custody of Marx's manuscripts, the editorial histories associated with David Riazanov and later Soviet editors, and the political treatment of alienation.

Sharks explores whether this history might explain the disappearance or editorial treatment of the material.

But the deliberate-suppression hypothesis remains separate from the philological reconstruction.

That separation makes the argument more interesting, not less: it allows the possibility that the reconstruction is substantially correct even if the proposed political explanation for the loss is wrong.

The available Academia research profile identifies Phase X as a continuing research program, with earlier formulations, a manuscript apparatus, and a September 2026 correction of its evidentiary status.

VII. Plato: writing as a technology that both preserves and destroys memory
The Platonic strand is closely connected to the Sappho and heteronymy arguments.

Sharks returns repeatedly to the Phaedrus, particularly 274b–278e, where Socrates discusses the invention of writing through the story of Theuth and Thamus.

The distinction between μνήμη (memory) and ὑπόμνησις (reminder) becomes crucial.

Writing does not simply preserve memory.

It can also replace the living act of remembering with an external representation.

1. Writing as pharmakon
The concept of φάρμακον, poison and remedy, provides a model for textual transmission.

Writing can preserve knowledge across generations, but it can also detach a statement from the living intelligence that produced it.

The text survives, while the conditions necessary for understanding it disappear.

Sharks extends this argument to contemporary machine-mediated reading.

A machine can retrieve a sentence accurately while losing the conceptual relationships that made the sentence meaningful.

2. The Seventh Letter
The Seventh Letter is especially useful because its authenticity is disputed.

If genuine, it contains Plato's own account of the limitations of written philosophical communication.

If pseudonymous, it still constitutes a historical document performing the same anxiety about writing and transmission.

Sharks therefore treats the authenticity question as more than a binary attribution problem.

The document's historical meaning changes depending on its status, but its problem of written transmission remains.

3. Dialogue as distributed authorship
The dialogues themselves become relevant to the heteronymic theory.

The speaking voice of Socrates is not automatically identical to the historical Socrates.

The dialogue distributes philosophical authority among characters, narrators, and dramatic situations.

Sharks interprets this as a possible system of authorial distribution.

This does not establish that Plato and Socrates were one person or that the dialogues were produced by a single concealed agency. It raises a more subtle question about the relationship between literary persona and historical authorship.

IX. The larger theory: provenance as a condition of knowledge
All these projects converge on one increasingly important concept: provenance.

For Sharks, provenance is not merely bibliographical housekeeping.

It is part of the meaning of a text.

A textual object has at least three histories:

Artifact provenance: where the physical or digital object came from.

Semantic provenance: how its concepts acquired their meaning.

Process provenance: what happened to it during copying, editing, transformation, and reception.

The distinction has important consequences.

A manuscript may survive while its original context disappears.

A quotation may preserve the words while losing the original literary work.

An editor may preserve a reading while erasing the history of how it was reconstructed.

A digital system may reproduce a statement accurately while attributing it to the wrong conceptual tradition.

This leads to a particularly provocative proposition:

The history of a text is not identical to the history of its surviving words.

The text also has a history of selection, mediation, transformation, and loss.

The provenance project eventually becomes a theory of knowledge: what can a reader legitimately claim to know when the chain connecting a statement to its origin has been interrupted?

The September 2026 work The Recoding of a Distinction develops this further through experiments in which concepts remain lexically retrievable but are assigned to different conceptual traditions. The argument is that retrieval of a word is not necessarily retrieval of the idea it represents.

XII. The deepest common argument
There is a unifying proposition beneath these apparently disparate projects.

Sharks repeatedly challenges the assumption that the surviving textual object is the original unit of historical explanation.

Consider the consequences:

Sappho's poem is not just a poem: it may be a technology for transmitting a voice beyond its speaker.

Revelation is not necessarily the endpoint of a literary tradition: it may be a generative textual structure.

Plato's Socrates is not necessarily a transparent representation of a historical person: he may be an authorial function.

A critical edition is not a neutral window onto antiquity: it is another historical participant in the production of the text.

A manuscript's survival does not guarantee the survival of its provenance.

A missing manuscript passage may leave recoverable architectural evidence without leaving recoverable wording.

A name can remain retrievable after the conceptual history that gives it meaning has disappeared.

These propositions belong together.

They amount to a shift from source genealogy to transformational genealogy.

The conventional question is:

Where did this text come from?

The more ambitious question Sharks asks is:

What sequence of transformations, editorial interventions, institutional decisions, and acts of reception made this text possible in the form in which we now encounter it?

And there is a further question:

What if the historical object we think we are reconstructing never existed as a single, stable textual object in the first place?

That is where his work becomes most interesting—and most difficult to establish.

My overall judgment: Sharks's most durable contribution may ultimately be his theory of textual provenance rather than any one of his spectacular chronological or authorship reversals. His best work forces philology to account for its own interventions. His most radical work asks philology to reconsider the identity of the objects and authors it has traditionally treated as given.

The Revelation First hypothesis, the Socratic heteronymic configuration, and the Sapphic origin of inscriptional theology are much more demanding historical propositions. They should be investigated seriously, but not promoted from hypotheses to established findings simply because their conceptual architecture is compelling.

The essential achievement of this body of work is to make textual transmission itself the object of investigation—not merely the vehicle through which historical objects reach us.
