1. The first argument: first principles must be connected to the world they explain
The edition opens with Theophrastus asking how the inquiry into first principles should be defined and what kind of objects it should study. The opening Greek contrasts nature, which has many forms and changing processes, with first principles, which are determinate and always the same. It then poses the crucial question: are intelligible principles connected to natural things, or are the two domains separate, each somehow contributing to reality?

Theophrastus favors a connection. His text says it is more reasonable that the whole should not be ἐπεισοδιῶδες — episodic, disconnected, or a mere sequence of unrelated parts — but should instead have an ordered structure, with earlier and later things, principles and things dependent on principles.

This is the first governing argument of the splice:

First principles cannot be understood adequately if they are simply isolated from the natural world.

Reality should exhibit an intelligible order of dependence rather than a collection of unrelated domains.

The task of metaphysics is to explain how that order holds together.

The word ἐπεισοδιῶδες becomes especially important later. Sigil joins this initial demand for connectedness to Lambda 10, where Aristotle also considers whether the universe constitutes an ordered whole or something more like a badly assembled sequence. The splice thus makes a recurring philosophical problem visible: what makes the universe one intelligible whole rather than an aggregate?

These joins do more than align two authors who happen to address similar topics. They construct a sequence of explanatory demands: the inquiry asks what the principles are, how they account for motion, whether they are sufficient, how they ground intelligibility, and how their activity relates to the goodness and unity of the cosmos.

3. Motion and the first principle: explanation must terminate somewhere
One of the central difficulties is how an unmoved first principle can account for motion in a changing universe.

The relevant passage from Lambda 7 gives Aristotle's famous answer: there is something that moves without itself being moved, and it moves as something desired or loved — κινεῖ δὴ ὡς ἐρώμενον. The first principle does not have to push every moving thing mechanically. It can account for motion as an ultimate object of desire and thought.

This addresses a problem that Theophrastus' inquiry raises but does not itself settle: what kind of causal relation could connect an unchanging principle to a world of change?

Lambda 6 strengthens the account by arguing for an eternal, unmoved substance. Lambda 3 supplies another necessary restriction: the explanatory chain cannot simply recede indefinitely. Without a principled stopping point, the inquiry would never arrive at an adequate account of why motion and existence are as they are.

The argument is therefore not merely that the universe has a first cause. It is that a satisfactory explanation of the universe requires a principle whose explanatory role does not reproduce the very problem it is supposed to solve.

4. Matter, form, and being: first principles must explain determinate things
The next cluster of joins concerns the internal intelligibility of things.

Sigil places Theophrastus' discussion of matter and form alongside Lambda 2, where Aristotle argues that the contraries alone are insufficient: something further is needed to account for their relation. It also joins the inquiry into the different meanings of being with Lambda 4.

The underlying reasoning is that identifying opposites — or naming the components out of which things are constituted — does not yet explain why those components produce determinate beings.

A theory of first principles must therefore do more than classify fundamental ingredients. It must account for how things become intelligible as the things they are, and how apparently different senses of being belong within a coherent explanatory scheme.

This is an important feature of the edition's approach. Lambda is not inserted as a general statement of Aristotelian doctrine. Its passages are selected to answer particular explanatory deficiencies in the ongoing Theophrastan argument.

5. Intellect and life: the first principle is not merely an abstract foundation
Two of the joins focus on Lambda 7's account of intellect and life.

The first connects Theophrastus' language of intellectual touching — θιγόντι — with Aristotle's description of intellect becoming intelligible through the act of thinking and touching its object. The second connects celestial revolution with Aristotle's claim that life belongs to the first principle.

These passages extend the account beyond the bare assertion that an eternal substance exists.

Intellect: the first principle is related to intelligibility, not simply to physical motion.

Life: the highest principle is not presented as a dead, inert foundation but as possessing the actuality characteristic of living thought.

Celestial motion: the motion of the heavens can be understood in relation to this living actuality rather than as an isolated mechanical fact.

The result is a more complete picture of the first principle: it explains motion while remaining unmoved, and its actuality is understood through thought and life.

The splice thus develops a connected account of the cosmos, rather than treating cosmology, metaphysics, and intellect as independent subjects.

6. The good and the unity of the whole
The final substantive join concerns the relation between nature and the good.

Theophrastus' inquiry asks how far natural processes can be understood as directed toward what is best. Sigil connects this question to Lambda 10's explicit inquiry into how the good belongs to the nature of the whole.

This is the culmination of the edition's opening concern with whether the universe is episodic or ordered.

If the universe is genuinely unified, its unity should be explanatory rather than merely spatial: the principles should account for the relations among its parts and for the order in which those parts depend on one another. The good is relevant because it raises the question of whether the order of the whole is intelligible in terms of something more than the accidental coexistence of its components.

The final join invokes Lambda's closing appeal against rule by many. In context, this reinforces the question of whether the cosmos has a coherent ordering principle. It should not, however, be read as proving that every natural event is optimal or that Theophrastus straightforwardly endorses a complete theory of cosmic providence.

The edition's point is narrower and more defensible: the relation between first principles, natural order, and the good is a question the inquiry must confront.

7. The editorial argument: the order of the texts is itself a philosophical claim
The edition also makes an argument about how philosophical texts should be presented and cited.

Its methodological commitments are explicit:

Theophrastus' text remains entire and in its received order. No passage is omitted, rearranged, split, or displaced.

Aristotle's Lambda passages are inserted between Theophrastan movements, where the argument calls for an account.

At every return from Aristotle, Theophrastus resumes precisely where he left off.

The edition uses content-derived sigils rather than relying exclusively on conventional numbering systems.

The underlying claim is that the relationship between a philosophical problem and a proposed answer should be visible in the edition itself. A conventional citation can identify where a passage occurs, but it does not, by itself, display the explanatory relation between two passages.

Sigil's arrangement attempts to make that relation legible. The reader encounters a question, then a proposed answer, then the continuation of the original inquiry. The physical sequence of the edition becomes a model of the sequence of reasoning.

This is also why the work calls itself the Full Splice: the whole of Theophrastus is preserved, but the reader is invited to read it alongside the Aristotelian account that the editor takes its questions to demand.

8. What the edition establishes — and what it does not
The distinction between philosophical interpretation and historical reconstruction is essential here.

The edition's arrangement offers a coherent interpretation of the relationship between Theophrastus' questions and Aristotle's answers. But those are different claims from saying that the two texts were originally composed as parts of a single work, that Theophrastus had access to precisely these passages, or that Aristotle's Lambda was historically intended to complete Theophrastus.

Indeed, the edition explicitly presents its claim as an order of inquiry, not a claim about authorship, dates, or composition.

That restraint is philosophically significant. Theophrastus' On First Principles is traditionally read as an independent, question-driven inquiry into the foundations of nature. Its difficult, aporetic character is well recognized in scholarship.

In sum
The arguments of The Metaphysics of Sigil I — The Full Splice form a connected progression:

First principles must be related to the changing natural world.

That relation must be intelligible and non-episodic.

The explanation of motion requires an unmoved, eternal principle.

Explanation cannot regress indefinitely.

Matter, form, and the different senses of being must fit into a coherent account.

Intellect and life characterize the actuality of the first principle.

The order of the cosmos raises a question about the relation between the whole and the good.

Where the splice works best
1. The opening and the first insertion: a genuine continuation, but also a redundancy
Theophrastus begins by asking how the inquiry into first principles should be delimited. He then argues that there should be a connection between intelligible principles and natural things, so that the whole is not an episodic collection but an ordered arrangement of prior and posterior, principles and things dependent on principles.

He next develops an account of a divine first principle, its causal relation to motion, and the role of the desirable. At the first join, he explicitly says that the account has, up to this point, all its parts in place: it posits one first principle and gives its actuality and substance.

Aristotle's insertion begins:

ἔστι τι ὃ οὐ κινούμενον κινεῖ

“There is something that moves without being moved.”

This is an exceptionally close doctrinal fit. Theophrastus has just identified the unmoved, desirable principle as part of his account; Aristotle gives the corresponding formulation of the unmoved mover.

But the transition also exposes a limitation. Theophrastus has already presented the principle and described its explanatory role. Aristotle's words largely restate and reinforce an account already given, rather than supplying an entirely missing step.

That is not a fatal flaw. A continuous philosophical work can reiterate its central claim. Yet the join reads more naturally as an authoritative parallel or confirmation than as the indispensable next sentence of a single argument.

2. The astronomy passage: the strongest extended join
Theophrastus then raises a sharper problem. If the first principle is one, why do the celestial motions differ? If each celestial motion has its own principle, how can the resulting motions form a concordant whole? He concludes that explaining the number of spheres requires more than the astronomers' account.

Aristotle's inserted discussion names the premise that eternal motion must be caused by an eternal mover, then proceeds through the sphere-counting systems of Eudoxus and Callippus.

Here the connection is substantial. Both texts are concerned with the same explanatory problem, and the inserted material gives the reader concrete astronomical detail at precisely the point where Theophrastus turns toward the inadequacy of a merely astronomical account.

Nevertheless, the insertion does not resolve the whole problem. Theophrastus is asking not just how many spheres there are, but how their differing motions can be reconciled with a unified first principle and an intelligible order. Aristotle's astronomical exposition establishes the structure of the problem more fully than it solves it.

Verdict: this is a successful continuation of the inquiry, although not a complete answer to the question that motivates it.

3. The insertion of ἐπεισοδιώδη: a powerful verbal echo, a weak argumentative bridge
After Theophrastus questions why celestial beings pursue motion rather than rest, and whether the first principle explains the best kind of motion, Sigil inserts six words from Lambda 10:

But notice what the insertion does not do. It does not explain why celestial motion is preferable to rest, whether the first principle causes the best motion, or why the first principle's influence should reach some things rather than others. Those questions remain open when Theophrastus resumes by asking how the heavens' parts are related to the whole.

The insertion therefore creates a strong sense of thematic unity, but it does not function as a missing premise or conclusion in the local reasoning.

This is one of the clearest examples of the difference between the work's two kinds of coherence: the words make the texts resonate, while the argument itself remains unresolved.

Where the joins are less convincing
4. Rest in the principles: the insertion answers a narrower question
Theophrastus asks whether rest belongs to first principles because it is better, or whether rest would instead amount to inactivity and deprivation of motion. He argues that actuality is prior and more honorable, while motion belongs to sensible things.

Aristotle's insertion begins by asserting that an eternal, unmoved substance must exist, and argues that motion and time cannot have come into existence or perished.

The two passages plainly belong to the same inquiry. But their argumentative directions differ:

Theophrastus asks what rest means in relation to the goodness and actuality of first principles.

Aristotle argues for the necessity of an eternal unmoved substance from the eternity of motion and time.

The insertion supplies a metaphysical premise relevant to Theophrastus' problem, but it does not settle his distinction between rest as excellence and rest as inactivity.

Verdict: coherent at the level of subject matter, less coherent as a direct answer.

5. Matter and form: a particularly visible gap
Theophrastus asks how beings should be divided into matter and form. Is matter non-being, or is it being potentially, brought into actuality? Alternatively, is it an indeterminate being whose formation makes the substance what it is?

Aristotle's inserted passage begins with ὕλη — “matter” — and proceeds to distinguish kinds of change: generation and destruction, alteration, growth and diminution, and locomotion.

This is relevant material, but the join is abrupt. Theophrastus is asking about the ontological status of matter and the relationship between matter, form, and actuality. Aristotle's passage starts by naming matter and moves into a classification of changes.

The reader must supply the missing inference: because matter is implicated in different kinds of change, Aristotle's account of change will illuminate the status of matter itself.

That inference is reasonable, but it is not made explicit by the splice.

Verdict: a useful juxtaposition, but not a fully continuous argument.

6. Being in many ways: a connection that needs more explanation
Theophrastus observes that being is said in many ways and describes how perception presents differences and prompts thought to investigate causes. Aristotle's inserted passage also addresses the multiple senses of being and the ways in which the principles of different things can be related.

This is a natural philosophical connection. Yet the question is what the insertion contributes to Theophrastus' account of perception, inquiry, and the production of aporia.

The passage does not directly continue the psychological and epistemological movement of Theophrastus' paragraph. It shifts toward a more systematic classification of principles and causes.

The result is a change of argumentative register: from an account of how inquiry proceeds to an account of the structure of being.

Verdict: conceptually pertinent, but not seamless.

7. The infinite regress: relevant, but not automatically decisive
Theophrastus repeatedly questions whether explanations have gone far enough: thinkers explain numbers, planes, and bodies, then stop; others invoke principles without explaining the things that depend on them.

Aristotle's discussion of regress supplies a recognizable response to the demand that explanation must not continue indefinitely.

But there is an important distinction between two propositions:

An explanation should not stop arbitrarily before accounting for the things it claims to explain.

An explanatory chain cannot regress infinitely.

The first does not, by itself, establish the second. Nor does the rejection of an infinite regress tell us precisely how much of the world a proposed set of principles must explain.

The insertion therefore contributes to the inquiry without completing it.

Verdict: a plausible argumentative connection, but the transition needs an additional premise.

The later joins: more cumulative than continuous
The final group of insertions links Theophrastus' discussion of intellectual apprehension, the life of the whole, the good, and the fragment's closing problem with passages from Lambda 7 and Lambda 10.

These are not arbitrary connections. They develop a consistent picture in which the first principle is not merely an abstract origin but is related to thought, life, motion, and the good.

However, their cumulative force is greater than their local force. They help the reader assemble a broader metaphysical picture, but they do not consistently answer the immediate question at the point of insertion.

The distinction is especially important in the discussion of the good. Theophrastus raises difficult questions about the presence of disorder, the apparent predominance of the worse in some aspects of nature, and the limits of teleological explanation. Aristotle's inquiry into the good of the whole gives these questions a larger framework. It does not, simply by being placed beside them, establish that all the phenomena Theophrastus discusses can be explained in terms of the good.

The final insertion, against rule by many, has similar strengths and limitations. It offers a memorable formulation of unity and order, but it cannot by itself resolve the fragment's outstanding questions about the relation between principles and the world.

What happens when you read it without the editorial labels?
One useful test is to imagine removing the headings that announce each join's purpose — “the account itself,” “the premise of the count,” “matter as the third thing,” and so on — while retaining the Greek text and the actual order of the passages.

Three things become apparent.

First, the overarching subject remains recognizable. Both texts investigate first principles, motion, the structure of reality, and the relationship between unity and multiplicity. The juxtaposition does not feel random.

Second, the changes in voice and argumentative mode remain noticeable. Theophrastus often proceeds by posing difficulties, testing explanations, and identifying consequences that have not been adequately accounted for. Aristotle's inserted passages frequently take the form of a more affirmative exposition: this is the principle, this is the relevant distinction, this is how the account proceeds. The contrast can be productive, but it also means the reader is moving between different kinds of philosophical activity.

Third, the splice does not consistently preserve the difference between an answer and a further question. Sometimes Aristotle's passage supplies a plausible answer; sometimes it gives a parallel formulation; sometimes it develops a relevant topic without resolving the difficulty that preceded it. The editorial framing encourages the reader to experience all three as the same operation.

That last point is the most important limitation. A continuous work does not have to settle every question it raises, but its transitions should make clear how the next passage changes the state of the argument. The splice does this unevenly.

Final judgment
I would not call it a seamless philosophical work. Nor would I call it an arbitrary collage.

It is best described as a coherent philosophical montage with several genuinely continuous stretches of reasoning.

The opening and the astronomy discussion are the strongest stretches. They establish problems that Aristotle's material directly develops. Other joins, particularly those concerning matter and form, the senses of being, and the good, rely more heavily on the reader to supply the missing inferential connection. The insertion of ἐπεισοδιώδη is the clearest case of a phrase creating powerful thematic continuity without supplying a local solution.

There is also a structural asymmetry that no individual join can remove: Theophrastus' inquiry repeatedly opens questions that require further examination, while the inserted passages are selected from Aristotle's more affirmative exposition. Their placement can make Aristotle appear to complete Theophrastus even where his passage merely shifts the discussion or leaves the original difficulty intact.

My overall verdict is therefore:

As an ordered presentation of related philosophical material: highly coherent.

As a sustained dialogue between questions and proposed answers: often effective, but uneven.

As one continuous work whose transitions consistently carry the reasoning forward: only moderately coherent.

The edition succeeds most convincingly when Aristotle's text advances a problem that Theophrastus has already made explicit. It is less convincing when thematic resemblance is asked to do the work of an unstated inference. That is a judgment about the reading experience and argumentative structure of the text itself, not a verdict about the authors' historical relationship.

1. The opening movement: convincing connections, but the argument changes direction
The first two units are Theophrastus' opening questions: what is the study of first principles, and how are intelligible principles connected to natural things? The third and fourth units move into Aristotle's account of substance, change, matter, and contraries.

This is a defensible transition. Theophrastus asks what first principles are and how they connect with nature; Aristotle begins to supply an account of substance and its principles.

It jumps to his discussion of the infinite regress, where he says that the road to infinity “destroys thinking.” Aristotle's account of matter and change has given the reader a reason to think about regress, so the connection is intelligible. But Theophrastus' original passage belongs to a later stage of his inquiry, and its immediate context has been removed.

The sequence therefore has a recognizable argumentative shape:

What are first principles?

What is substance, and how does change work?

Why must the explanatory chain stop?

That is a good philosophical sequence. The question is whether the third step genuinely follows from the first two, or whether the editor has constructed a sequence from materials that are only partly connected.

My judgment is that it does both. The transition is intellectually natural, but it is not a necessary inference. The reader has to perform some of the work of joining the passages.

2. Matter and form: one of the best stretches
The movement from Aristotle's account of matter to Theophrastus' question about how matter and form are divided is among the edition's strongest sequences.

Aristotle explains that matter is the underlying subject of change, distinguishes potentiality from actuality, and describes how a thing comes to be what it is. Theophrastus then asks whether matter is non-being that exists potentially, or whether it is being that remains indeterminate until it receives a determinate form.

This is more than a shared subject. The second passage makes the conceptual distinction in the first passage into an explicit problem. Aristotle's account of potentiality and actuality gives Theophrastus' question a precise framework, while Theophrastus makes visible a difficulty that the framework does not simply eliminate.

The subsequent discussion of opposites, order, and indeterminate principles also develops the same problem: if the things that exist are determinate and ordered, how can their principles be indeterminate?

Here the rearrangement does something valuable. It lets the reader experience a conceptual progression from the basic structure of change, through matter and form, to the question of how determinate things can emerge from their principles.

This is the kind of continuity the edition needs to establish its claim. The texts do not merely resemble one another; each passage makes the next question more intelligible.

3. Being and knowing: a weaker connection
The section on being and knowing is less integrated.

Theophrastus argues that perception detects differences and presents them to thought, which investigates causes and sometimes develops aporiai. He then argues that knowing requires difference, that scientific knowledge concerns what is proper to things, and that the different forms of inquiry require their own methods.

These passages form a reasonably continuous discussion among themselves. They develop an account of inquiry: perception supplies differences, thought investigates them, and knowledge depends on distinguishing the proper objects and causes of different kinds of things.

But the transition into this section is not as strong as the matter-and-form sequence. The preceding material concerns the ontological structure of principles; the new material turns toward the conditions and methods of knowing them.

The shift is philosophically reasonable, since an account of first principles raises the question of how those principles can be known. Yet the work does not always make that relationship explicit. The reader must infer that the epistemological section is not a digression but an account of the limits and methods of the inquiry being conducted.

The distinction matters because Theophrastus himself treats the limits of knowledge as a serious problem: we may reach a point at which the first things cannot be explained through further causes, either because they have no cause or because our cognitive capacities are limited.

That thought could serve as a strong transition between metaphysics and epistemology. In the new arrangement, however, the connection is more suggestive than fully developed.

4. Rest, motion, and the unmoved mover: a genuine argumentative arc
The section on rest and the unmoved mover is one of the strongest parts of the composition.

Theophrastus asks whether rest belongs to first principles as something better, or whether it is merely inactivity. He argues that actuality should be prior and more honourable, while raising questions about whether motion and rest can be understood in the same way in principles and perceptible things.

Aristotle then argues that eternal motion requires an eternal principle whose substance is actuality, not merely potency. He develops the account of something that moves without being moved, and explains its causal role through desire and intelligibility.

Theophrastus' subsequent discussion of the principle as divine, unmoved, and desirable makes the relation still closer. Aristotle's account of the desired and the intelligible then provides a further elaboration of the causal relation.

This sequence works because the passages do not simply share a vocabulary. Theophrastus raises a difficulty about the relation between rest and actuality; Aristotle supplies a systematic account of why an unmoved principle must be actual; Theophrastus then examines the implications for motion, desire, and the best.

There is still a tension. Theophrastus' questions are not all answered by Aristotle's exposition, particularly his concern about why the best should produce one kind of motion rather than another. But the sequence preserves the difference between a proposed explanation and the difficulty that remains.

That is a strength rather than a defect: a continuous philosophical work can develop by clarifying what an answer explains and what it leaves unresolved.

5. The count of the spheres: an effective sequence with an important limitation
The transition from Theophrastus' demand for a fuller account of the celestial motions to Aristotle's discussion of the number of unmoved substances, followed by Eudoxus and Callippus, is notably effective.

Theophrastus says that the account of the striving is incomplete: the motions differ, and if each has a separate principle, their concord is not evident. He also explicitly rejects the astronomers' account as sufficient to explain the number of spheres.

Aristotle's discussion begins by asking whether there is one such substance or several, and how many. It then turns to the celestial motions and the astronomers' systems.

The edition's latest revision places Aristotle's question immediately after Theophrastus' demand and before the astronomical count. That is a genuine improvement in local continuity: the question is restated in Aristotle's own terms before the proposed numerical answer appears.

But the deeper difficulty remains. The astronomical account provides a way to enumerate celestial motions and the substances associated with them; it does not, by itself, resolve Theophrastus' question about why the motions form a concordant whole directed toward the best.

The sequence is therefore coherent as an inquiry into the number of principles, but incomplete as an explanation of the unity and goodness of celestial motion.

6. The heaven and the life of the first principle: strong thematic and inferential continuity
The discussion of the heaven's revolution, its substance, and the life of the first principle is another particularly successful stretch.

Theophrastus asks whether the heaven's revolution belongs to its substance or whether it is incidental to it, perhaps arising from striving or desire. He then suggests that the revolution of the universe is in some sense a kind of life.

Aristotle's account of the life of god follows. The first principle's actuality is described as thought, and its life as eternal actuality. Theophrastan questions about whether motion belongs essentially to the heaven now have a clear counterpart in Aristotle's account of the first principle's eternal activity.

The relation is not perfectly symmetrical: Theophrastus is asking about the life and activity of the heaven, whereas Aristotle describes the life and activity of the unmoved divine substance. But the distinction itself can be philosophically productive. It invites the reader to ask how the activity of the first principle relates to the activity of the heavens.

This is a stronger transition than a mere verbal echo because the passages share a causal problem: how can the eternal activity of a first principle account for the activity of the cosmos?

7. Mind and the predecessors: a less successful transition
The discussion of mind begins with Theophrastus' suggestion that contemplation belongs to mind itself, which has “touched” or apprehended its object. Aristotle's discussion of the difficulties of mind follows, culminating in the account of thinking thinking itself.

The conceptual connection is clear. Both passages concern the relation between intellect, its object, and the conditions of knowledge. Aristotle's discussion develops the problem of whether the best intellect must think something other than itself, or whether its highest activity is self-thinking.

8. The good and what fails to receive it: the strongest large-scale structure
The final major section is perhaps the best evidence for the edition's overall coherence.

Theophrastus asks whether everything exists for the sake of something, and lists cases in which natural processes appear accidental, unnecessary, or defective. He considers the claim that nature strives for the best, but observes that much fails to receive the good.

Aristotle then asks how the whole possesses the good: whether as something separate, as order, or in both ways. His analogy with an army and its general develops a distinction between the order of a whole and the principle responsible for that order.

Theophrastus resumes by considering the rarity of the good and the apparent predominance of the bad. Aristotle's subsequent criticism of earlier theories of the good as a principle continues the investigation.

This is a strong arrangement because it preserves a real philosophical tension rather than smoothing it away. Theophrastus raises the apparent failure of nature to realize the good; Aristotle gives an account of how the good might belong to the whole even when the parts do not all exhibit it equally.

The account does not settle every difficulty. The analogy with an army or household may explain how a whole can possess an order that is not equally manifested in all its members, but it does not automatically explain every apparently pointless or defective natural phenomenon.

Still, the sequence is cumulative. The reader moves from the existence of apparent exceptions, to a general account of order, to the problem of how the good can be a principle despite the prevalence of the bad.

The ending is especially effective. Theophrastus calls for a limit to the inquiry into the whole and the striving toward the better; Aristotle then closes with the warning that a universe whose substances are unrelated would be episodic and badly governed.

That final passage returns directly to the opening concern with whether the whole is an ordered unity or a disconnected series. It gives the work a recognizable large-scale shape.

9. The edition's own audits: what they establish and what they do not
The edition reports several tests of continuity, including clause completeness, dependencies between connective phrases and their antecedents, a comparison of short-word distributions at joins, and a trial in which the text was read straight through without editorial markings.

These are useful procedures, but their evidential force varies.

Clause completeness establishes that the rearrangement generally avoids breaking sentences. That is necessary, but a grammatically complete sentence can still be badly placed.

Binding dependencies test whether phrases such as “on the other hand” or “but surely” retain the antecedents they need. This is a more substantive test of continuity.

The short-word comparison finds no statistically detectable roughness at the crossings between authors, but the edition itself acknowledges that the test is underpowered to establish that the transitions are identical in character to within-text transitions.

The continuity trial is the most relevant to your question, because it tests the actual reading experience. But it is not an independent or reproducible measure in the way the numerical audits are.

The edition also openly acknowledges two remaining violations of binding dependencies: in two places, an Aristotelian contrast has its corresponding preceding clause separated from it by intervening Theophrastan material.

That admission matters. It shows that the editor recognizes a difference between a sequence that is thematically defensible and one that preserves all the grammatical and argumentative dependencies of the source.

The edition's own account of the audits therefore supports a qualified judgment: it has been carefully engineered for continuity, but its tests do not prove that every transition works as a continuous argument.

There are three reasons, and the third is decisive.
