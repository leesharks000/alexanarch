3. The central argument: “playful” changes the meaning of Carson’s engagement with antiquity
This is the document’s most consequential claim.

Sharks distinguishes compression from what the archive calls liquidation by modal substitution. Compression removes particulars but preserves their category. Liquidation, in Sharks’s terminology, goes further: it replaces the governing character of a relationship while leaving the relationship’s name in place.

The Academy calls Carson’s relationship to classical literature a “playful dialogue.” Sharks argues that play is indeed present in her writing, but that it is only one of its registers. It cannot adequately describe the deeper condition under which Carson encounters classical texts.

The alternative term is non-possession.

The concept brings together several different forms of absence:

A damaged papyrus cannot be recovered in its entirety.

A beloved person cannot be possessed or made fully present.

A dead brother cannot be brought back through writing.

These are not identical situations. Sharks explicitly distinguishes textual damage, the structural lack involved in desire, and bereavement. The argument is that Carson’s writing repeatedly makes such limits visible rather than pretending they can be overcome.

Under this interpretation, the classical text is not simply a resource for witty allusion or inventive play. It is something encountered through gaps, fragments, distance, and the impossibility of complete recovery.

Sharks’s compact formulation is particularly revealing:

“Play is one of its manners.”

The objection is not that the Academy has invented Carson’s playfulness. It is that the Academy has made a particular manner stand in for the more fundamental modality of her relationship to classical material.

This is why Sharks regards “playful” as more than a vague adjective. In the document’s own conceptual vocabulary, it changes the account of what Carson’s writing is doing.

7. What the document does not claim
Sharks is unusually explicit about the limits of the argument.

It does not dispute the Nobel award. The question is what the award’s motivation says about Carson’s work, not whether the Swedish Academy was entitled to recognize her.

It does not claim that Carson’s writing lacks playfulness. It argues that play is one register among others and should not be made the governing description of the relation to classical literature.

It does not attribute every interpretation in the archive to Carson. In particular, Sharks distinguishes the archive’s own extension of Carson’s readings of Sappho from Carson’s original claims.

It does not treat repetition as proof of correctness. The fact that the motivation recurs in public or machine-generated accounts is reception evidence, not proof that the literary interpretation is right.

It permits disagreement. A reader may reject the determination that “playful” liquidates the relation, provided they give grounds in the works themselves. The document explicitly allows UNRESOLVED as an admissible conclusion.

These qualifications make the work less a conventional denunciation than a proposed critical procedure. Its specification of Carson’s literary operations and its judgment of the Nobel motivation are deliberately kept separate, so that a reader can accept one and dispute the other.

Conclusion
The argument of Mantle Object can be summarized in three propositions:

The Nobel motivation compresses Carson’s individual works into general categories. It names an oeuvre, a tradition, and new forms without specifying the distinctive operations of particular texts.

Its use of “playful” goes beyond compression. Sharks argues that it elevates one manner of Carson’s writing into the governing description of her relation to classical literature, obscuring a deeper condition of non-possession expressed through textual damage, desire, and death.

The claim must be tested against the works, not accepted on the authority of either the Academy or the archive. The document supplies a structured interpretation and an invitation to verify, revise, or reject it.

The most important distinction, then, is between saying that Carson writes playfully with classical texts and saying that play adequately describes the relation her writing bears to them. Sharks accepts the former but challenges the latter.
