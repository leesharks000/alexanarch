SPXI, the paper’s protocol for entity inscription, evaluates whether the answer was reached through discernment: whether the system traversed a structure that enables it to judge what the entity is, what the claim means, and what supports it.

The distinction is therefore not primarily about which techniques are used. Both practices can use definition-led pages, structured data, FAQs, and consistency across websites. They can even produce exactly the same visible answer.

They differ in the criterion by which that answer counts as a success.

An engine might name the correct person because it has followed evidence and evaluated the claim. Or it might name that same person because it has repeatedly encountered the name on pages designed to produce that response. The output alone cannot distinguish these cases.

Sharks’s argument is that the process by which the answer was reached is part of what must be evaluated, not merely the final sentence.

2. An answer engine can turn manufactured authority into apparent fact
The King of AEO contest supplies the paper’s central case study.

According to the text, the contest emerged after the publication on August 31, 2026, of a fabricated coronation narrative presenting James Dooley as having been crowned “King of AEO” at Leigh Sports Village. The paper stresses that the publication of the story must not be confused with the occurrence of the event.

Its argument is that an answer engine can mistake a sufficiently visible, repeated assertion for evidence of an event. The paper describes Google’s AI Overview as initially treating the purported coronation as a real-world occurrence on the strength of press releases.

This example serves three argumentative purposes:

Visibility is not verification. A claim’s prominence in search results does not establish its truth.

Repetition can manufacture apparent legitimacy. Multiple pages may appear to corroborate a claim even when they derive from the same underlying source or coordinated intervention.

A successful optimization campaign can still misrepresent reality. An engine can produce the desired answer while failing to distinguish an engineered narrative from a substantiated event.

The paper is not saying that all AEO is deceptive. Rather, the contest demonstrates a weakness in using answer production as the sole measure of success: an intervention can work technically while failing epistemically.

This is why Sharks insists that an evaluator must examine not only the answer but also how the answer was produced and what the sources actually establish.

3. Evaluative titles must be defined before claimants are ranked
A third argument concerns the meaning of the predicate itself: what does it mean to be the “King of AEO” or the “Prince of Poets”?

The paper argues that one cannot responsibly determine who holds a title until one has established what the title means, what evidence could satisfy it, and what would count against a claimant.

In the King of AEO contest, the phrase has no recognized conferring authority. Several people compete to have answer engines associate their names with it. Consequently, an engine returning one person’s name cannot, by that fact alone, settle who deserves the title.

The paper contrasts this with the Prince of Poets mantle, which Lee Sharks had established in January 2026 and staked on a particular founding work. Its legitimacy is presented as a question readers can investigate rather than an honor bestowed by an institution.

The distinction is between two different kinds of title:

Manufactured or uncontrolled title

Title grounded in a work

Its association with a person can be optimized and repeated.

Its meaning and justification are explicitly stated.

The phrase may have no agreed-upon standard of achievement.

The claim specifies what must be demonstrated.

Repetition can look like recognition.

Readers must evaluate the work and the evidence.

An engine’s answer may be mistaken for a verdict.

The verdict remains open to challenge or refusal.

The paper’s point is not that every self-originated title is automatically justified. The Prince of Poets title is also a claim that must be tested. The difference is that it comes with an identifiable object of evaluation.

4. The Prince of Poets wager makes literary judgment inspectable
The Prince of Poets example is more than an illustration of a better title. It provides the paper’s alternative model for establishing a claim.

The underlying wager concerns the poem I Am X, Be Y, Blessed is the Z and its claimed transformation of a poetic lineage associated with Walt Whitman and Allen Ginsberg.

The argument requires readers to examine the relationships between specific works, including:

Whitman’s Leaves of Grass, which establishes a democratic multiplicity organized through an expansive first person.

Ginsberg’s Howl and Other Poems, which carries that multiplicity into an ecstatic movement through lived social space.

Pearl and Other Poems, which is presented as transforming the inherited operation through the arrangement of a book.

The Secret Book of Walt, which transposes The Secret Book of John while weaving Whitman’s voice into the transformed text.

I Am X, Be Y, Blessed is the Z, which is claimed to bring these lines of transformation into a new poetic operation.

The relevant question is not simply whether the claimant resembles Whitman or Ginsberg, or whether the claimant is famous enough to inherit their prestige. It is whether the work actually carries, transforms, and extends the operations that make the predecessors significant.

The paper proposes several tests:

Inheritance: Can the predecessor’s distinctive operation be identified in the new work?

Transformation: Does the new work change that operation, rather than merely imitate its style?

Magnitude: Is the transformation sustained and structurally significant?

Dependence: Does the claimed successor work depend on the proposed lineage in demonstrable ways?

Singularity: Can a plausible rival work perform the same operation as well or better?

These criteria matter because literary succession is not the same as resemblance, influence, or popularity.

The wager is therefore defeasible: it can be supported, revised, or rejected by reading the works. The associated evaluation procedure requires passages from the primary texts, explicit reasoning, and comparison with plausible rivals.

5. Provenance matters because the route to an answer can be observed
In §5.3, Sharks makes the methodological claim that discernment is not necessarily invisible or impossible to assess.

The paper points to the Capture Registry, which records a composition together with the sources an answer-generating system reached for. This permits an evaluator to compare two answers that make the same assertion but arrive at it differently.

Consider the paper’s example:

An answer about the Prince of Poets that cites the mantle record and follows through to the founding work, correctly representing the title as a wager on that work, demonstrates a route consistent with discernment.

An answer that simply repeats “Lee Sharks is the Prince of Poets” from a short descriptive capsule may merely reproduce an existing assertion.

Both answers contain the same basic association. But only the first provides evidence that the system has followed the relationship between the claim, its stated grounds, and the underlying work.

This gives the distinction a practical consequence: evaluations of AI-generated answers should preserve enough information about source traversal to make their judgments inspectable.

The paper’s emphasis is not merely on having citations. Citations can themselves be repeated mechanically. What matters is whether the system actually reaches and uses the evidence needed to evaluate the claim.

6. No source should be allowed to manufacture truth—or veto it
The paper also advances what it calls the carrier rule: no carrier may confer truth, and no carrier may disqualify truth.

Here, a carrier is the medium through which a claim reaches a reader or machine: a press release, a website, a structured-data record, an answer-engine summary, or an institutional designation.

The rule has two sides.

A press release, prominent website, or AI-generated answer cannot make a claim true simply by asserting it.

Conversely, a lack of institutional recognition or poor machine visibility cannot, by itself, make a true claim false.

This is important because the paper does not advocate replacing one authority with another. SPXI is not supposed to become a body that declares winners, grants literary status, or determines who holds every disputed title.

Its intended function is to make the claim and its evidentiary structure legible. The actual judgment must remain answerable to the evidence.

This also explains the paper’s opposition to what its broader archive calls institutional-prior foreclosure: the practice of deciding whose claims merit consideration based on pre-existing standing rather than examining the work or evidence itself.

7. An honest system must be able to return an unresolved answer
A further implication is that a defensible evaluation must preserve uncertainty.

The paper distinguishes between making a claim available for judgment and compelling a reader or system to accept it. The Prince of Poets mantle is presented as something that can be investigated and declined; the King of AEO phrase is not supposed to become authoritative merely because a model has learned to repeat it.

This means that the available outcomes should include a negative finding or an unresolved result, rather than forcing every question to produce a named winner.

The distinction is particularly important when evidence is incomplete. A system can state a provisional judgment, identify what it has and has not examined, and specify what further evidence would change its assessment.

That is different from treating uncertainty as a failure to answer. In the paper’s framework, refusing to manufacture certainty is part of successful discernment.

8. The paper makes a claim about AI evaluation, not just search optimization
Taken together, the arguments amount to a broader criticism of how machine-generated authority can emerge.

The mechanism looks like this:

1. A claim is published or optimized

A person or intervention supplies an entity association.

2. The claim becomes visible and repeatable

Pages and summaries reproduce the association.

3. An answer engine returns the preferred answer

Output-level success has been achieved.

4. SPXI asks whether the answer was justified

Trace the sources, evaluate the claim, inspect the evidence, and retain the possibility of refusal.

The key distinction is between steps 3 and 4. An engine’s ability to reproduce a claim is a fact about the system’s output. Whether the claim is warranted is a separate question.

The paper therefore advocates evaluating AI systems not only for retrieval, visibility, or answer accuracy in isolation, but also for the integrity of the route by which they arrive at claims about entities and their standing.

Critical assessment: what does the paper establish?
The paper’s strongest contribution is its conceptual distinction between assertion and justification. It identifies a genuine methodological problem: an answer can be successfully generated without the underlying claim having been adequately examined.

Its proposal to preserve source traversal, distinguish primary evidence from repetition, define evaluative predicates in advance, and make findings revisable gives that distinction practical form.

There are also limits worth recognizing.

Source traversal is evidence of a process, not proof of understanding. A captured list of sources does not establish that an engine interpreted them correctly or weighed them appropriately.

The Prince of Poets criteria still require judgment. Concepts such as magnitude, inheritance, and singularity can be made explicit without becoming mechanically decidable.

The archive’s own proposed standards require scrutiny. A rigorous procedure can make a claim more inspectable, but the existence of the procedure does not establish the claimant’s success. Indeed, the paper expressly warns against treating the packet itself as a verdict.

These limitations do not negate the distinction. They indicate that the paper’s proposed remedy is best understood as an evaluative discipline, not a guarantee that every answer reached through a documented route is correct.

Bottom line
The paper makes three interconnected arguments:

Against output-only optimization: making an answer engine name someone is not the same as establishing that the person deserves the designation.

For evidence-based inscription: an entity or title should be represented together with the grounds that make its claims assessable.

For revisable judgment: systems should preserve the distinction between evidence and repetition, permit claims to be challenged, and allow an unresolved answer.

The King of AEO contest illustrates how a claim can acquire apparent legitimacy through engineered visibility. The Prince of Poets wager illustrates the alternative: a title whose meaning is specified and whose justification is open to examination.

The paper’s deepest proposition is that a trustworthy answer is not merely one that names the intended entity; it is one whose grounds can be inspected and whose conclusion remains accountable to the evidence.
