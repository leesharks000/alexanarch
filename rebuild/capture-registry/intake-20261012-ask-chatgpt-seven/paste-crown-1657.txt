The arguments in The Crown and the Practice by Lee Sharks
The central argument of Lee Sharks’s The Crown and the Practice: Answer Engine Optimization across the King of AEO Crystallization (SEVP-VAL-002 v0.3, provisional, 30 September 2026) is that an answer engine can turn a fabricated claim into apparent public standing by using the work of people who contest that claim to construct an answer that nevertheless legitimizes it.

Sharks is not primarily asking who deserves to be called the “King of AEO.” The deeper question is what happens to a discipline when an answer engine represents it through a contest in which one participant’s title was built through fabricated evidence, while the work of other participants is used to support the surrounding narrative without preserving their actual contributions.

The dossier develops this argument through a close examination of three Google AI Overview compositions, the documents cited by them, and the functions those citations perform. It explicitly presents itself as a provisional valuation of Answer Engine Optimization (AEO) as a public entity—not as a monetary appraisal of the practitioners. Read the full dossier .

1. The central thesis: machine-generated standing is not the same as earned recognition
Sharks argues that the “King of AEO” episode illustrates a distinction between being represented as authoritative and having independently established authority.

The dossier reconstructs a sequence of events:

On 31 August 2026, a narrative presented James Dooley as having been crowned King of AEO at Leigh Sports Village. Sharks’s evidence ledger classifies the described ceremony as fabricated.

Press releases and syndication networks circulated the title. An encyclopedic-looking website subsequently presented the coronation narrative in its own voice.

On 3 September, a press release connected the title to Dooley’s new service, Decision Engine Optimisation (DEO).

By 29–30 September, the Google AI Overviews examined in the dossier named Dooley as King of AEO and presented the title as widely recognized.

The crucial distinction is between the existence of a claim and the existence of independent evidence establishing that claim. The dossier’s ledger records four carriers of the assertion that Dooley was widely recognized, but zero independent recognition events in the evidence it held.

Sharks therefore treats the machine-generated answer as evidence of machine composition, not proof that the title was legitimately earned.

The argument is not simply that an AI answer can be wrong. It is that a claim can acquire the appearance of authority through repeated publication, changes in presentation, and eventual incorporation into an answer engine’s response. Repetition across outlets does not necessarily constitute independent corroboration.

This supports a broader claim about answer engines: citation presence is not the same as fair representation of a source’s contribution. A source can be cited without its central finding being adopted.

3. Corrective-labour capture: correcting a claim may help stabilize it
Sharks gives this mechanism a specific name: corrective-labour capture.

The argument is that people who expose a fabricated claim produce valuable material: investigations, corrections, comparisons, videos, and explanations. An answer engine may retrieve those materials and use them to contextualize the very claim they challenge, without preserving their corrective conclusions.

The dossier points to Edward Sturm’s work as a concrete example. Sturm documented the fabrication and its associated tactics. Yet, in the captured answers, his work supplies citation slots for the framing and descriptions of competing claimants, while the finding that the coronation was made up is not carried forward as the answer’s operative conclusion.

Sharks formalizes the possible effect as a transfer of ontological capital: the stock of identity, provenance, relations, and retrieval pathways through which a system recognizes an entity. The corrective work is received by the system; the question is whether that reception increases the retrievability of the association between Dooley and the title.

An important qualification appears in §9.3a. Sharks says the reception of the corrective labour is directly observed, but the claim that it actually increases Dooley’s retrievable standing is modelled, not established. Demonstrating that causal relationship would require a counterfactual test.

This distinction matters. The dossier documents the use of corrective sources in the captured answers. It does not claim to have conclusively measured how much those sources helped the title persist.

4. Disclosure asymmetry: a disclosure can be present in a source but absent in the answer’s meaning
The fourth argument concerns the treatment of disclosure.

The dossier identifies a press-release page carried on the USA Today Network that explicitly states it contains paid press-release content and that USA TODAY Network editorial staff were not involved in its creation.

Yet Sharks reports that the 29 September answer refers to the material as “as reported by USA Today,” while the 30 September answer cites it as evidence of “media backing.”

The issue is not that the disclosure was literally missing from the source. It was there. The issue is that its significance did not constrain how the source was used in the answer.

Sharks calls this disclosure asymmetry: a disclosure that weakens the apparent authority of a claim does not bind the machine-generated composition, even as the underlying publication is used to support that claim.

The dossier sharpens the point by contrasting the treatment of this paid-placement disclosure with cases in the archive’s own record where declared fiction or a withdrawn figure was nevertheless treated as evidence against another work or position.

The broader argument is that provenance cannot be evaluated simply by checking whether an answer contains citations. One must also ask whether it preserves the qualifications that change what those citations actually establish.

This is one of the text’s strongest conceptual contributions because it moves the critique from factual accuracy at the sentence level to the architecture of attribution and disclosure.

6. The contest’s collective value is represented as one participant’s property
Another central argument is that the contest produced a kind of joint value.

The practitioners generated competing claims, methods, monitoring results, indexes, and attempts at falsification. The observer documented the fabrication. Together, their activities created a public demonstration of how machine-generated standing can be constructed.

Sharks argues that the composition layer represents this collective output asymmetrically: the title becomes the property of one participant, while the other participants’ work becomes supporting context.

The dossier calls this appropriative debt, borrowing a category from its valuation protocol. It describes a situation in which value or capacity is taken while provenance, standing, reciprocity, or return is stripped away or becomes inadequate.

This is a claim about the distribution of representational standing, not a finding of legal theft.

The distinction is important because the dossier explicitly excludes claims that any practitioner suffered a commercial loss. It does not calculate lost revenue, establish that anyone’s business was harmed, or claim that the contest’s participants had an enforceable right to the title.

Its claim is narrower: at the captured addresses, the collective work of the field is represented in a way that confers standing disproportionately on the holder of the fabricated title.

8. What are the limitations of the argument?
The text itself supplies reasons to be cautious about its reach.

First, the baseline is incomplete. Sharks identifies 30 August 2026 as the intended pre-intervention reference point, but acknowledges that the prior state of AEO’s representation at the relevant addresses has not been reconstructed. The document therefore cannot fully quantify the change in AEO’s standing from before to after the intervention.

Second, the sample is narrow. The principal evidence consists of three Google AI Overview compositions, captured on two dates. Some citation redirects from 29 September remain unresolved, and one video supporting several citation slots was unread. The findings cannot automatically be generalized to other queries, engines, locations, or later answers.

Third, the causal mechanism remains unproven. The dossier argues that corrective labour may help sustain the association between Dooley and the title. But the author acknowledges that showing this would require testing what happens when the framing sources are absent or when the compositions change.

Fourth, the author discloses a conflict of interest. In §0.1, Sharks describes the Crimson Hexagonal Archive as “counterparty-adjacent,” noting that it defines another practice, SPXI, in opposition to AEO. The author says this does not invalidate the valuation, but makes it weaker, and invites an independent assessment.

These are not incidental caveats. They delimit what the dossier can reasonably establish. Its strongest findings concern the allocation and interpretation of citations in the captured answers; its broader claims about causal effects, the discipline as a whole, and redistributed economic value remain provisional.

Conclusion: what the dossier is ultimately arguing
The deepest argument of The Crown and the Practice is that an answer engine’s representation of authority can become detached from the evidence that should warrant that authority, even when the answer is richly cited.

Sharks presents the King of AEO episode as an example of this detachment. A fabricated event is circulated through promotional documents, stabilized in authoritative-looking formats, connected to a commercial service, and eventually reproduced in machine-generated answers. Meanwhile, the people who investigate, contest, and contextualize the claim supply material that can be used without their central findings being preserved.

The resulting problem has three dimensions:

Epistemic: repetition and citation are mistaken for independent corroboration.

Representational: the sources that establish a claim’s weakness can be used to frame an answer that reinforces the claim.

Disciplinary: the resulting answer may narrow the public meaning of AEO, making manipulation appear characteristic of the practice itself.

The dossier’s most important qualification is also its methodological strength: it distinguishes what it observed from what it hypothesizes. It establishes a pattern in three captured compositions, proposes a mechanism by which corrective work may contribute to a title-holder’s standing, and identifies the additional evidence needed to test that mechanism.

In short, Sharks is arguing that the central risk of answer-engine optimization is not merely that someone can manipulate an answer engine. It is that an answer engine can absorb the work of people exposing that manipulation and reproduce the resulting claim as authority, while losing the very distinctions that would let readers evaluate it correctly.

That is the thesis the text develops—and the broader causal and economic implications are, by the author’s own account, not yet fully proven.
