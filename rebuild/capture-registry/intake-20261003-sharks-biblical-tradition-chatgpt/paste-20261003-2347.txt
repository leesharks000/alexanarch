The short answer is that Sharks is proposing a different model of what “textual tradition” is—one that shifts the problem from recovering a stable original text to reconstructing a sequence of transformations, institutional selections, and author-functions.

The central revision
The most important move is the claim that the New Testament's familiar textual history has the direction of causation backwards.

Sharks argues that Revelation should not merely be moved earlier within the conventional chronology. The much stronger claim is that Revelation was the first New Testament book composed, preceding Paul's letters and the Synoptics. The later writings are then read as transformations or elaborations of structures already compressed in Revelation—the “midrashim transform.”

rather than the conventional broad picture:

1. Canonical order becomes reception history, not composition history
One of Sharks's strongest and most intelligible interventions is the argument about Revelation's position at the end of the Bible.

The argument is essentially: a book's position in a codex is not a timestamp.

Sharks notes that early manuscript traditions varied in the ordering of the major NT blocks, while Revelation's eventual position at the end can be explained by its function as an eschatological conclusion and by its historically contested canonical status. Thus, reading “Revelation comes last” as evidence that “Revelation was written last” commits what Sharks calls the “stratigraphic fallacy”—mistaking an editorial arrangement for a chronological deposit.

That is a genuinely useful conceptual distinction even if one rejects the conclusion that Revelation was first.

It tells us to distinguish at least three histories:

composition history — when a text was produced;

transmission history — how copies and textual forms circulated;

canonical/reception history — how communities arranged, selected, and interpreted those texts.

Traditional scholarship already distinguishes these things. Sharks's contribution is to make the distinction the organizing principle of the argument.

2. The “text” becomes a process rather than a fixed object
This is where the Josephus material becomes important.

Sharks argues that textual traditions are shaped by survival, selection, citation, institutional preservation, and destruction. The “Internal Witness Condition,” for example, argues that when a destructive historical process is known through someone who survived it, the surviving testimony is necessarily conditioned by the very selection process that allowed it to exist.

Applied broadly, the implication is striking:

the surviving textual tradition is not a neutral sample of what once existed.

It is the residue of historical selection.

That idea isn't unprecedented in textual criticism—papyri, quotations, excerptors, scribal habits, canon formation, lost texts, and manuscript survival have all been studied in these terms. But Sharks pushes it toward a more general epistemological claim: we should model the mechanism of survival itself, not simply reconstruct a text from the survivors.

That potentially changes how one thinks about biblical textual criticism. The question becomes not merely:

“Which reading is earlier?”

but also:

“Why is this witness here for us to read at all, while other witnesses disappeared?”

That is a much more reception-historical conception of textual tradition.

4. Manuscript evidence is used against excessive chronological confidence
The “papyrological inversion” is another major piece.

Sharks emphasizes that the earliest physical NT witnesses don't line up neatly with the conventional reconstruction of compositional priority: the material record includes early Johannine material, while the surviving physical witness to Pauline material is later. The argument is not simply “the manuscript is earlier, therefore the composition is earlier.” Sharks explicitly acknowledges that manuscript dates are ranges. Instead, the claim is that the confidence hierarchy should be disturbed when the physical evidence doesn't match the confidence of the reconstructed literary chronology.

What I think is genuinely significant
If we separate the methodological insight from the historical conclusions, there are several genuinely interesting revisions.

A. It makes the canon itself a textual witness
Instead of treating canon formation as something that happened after the texts were produced, Sharks treats canonical ordering, attribution, and arrangement as evidence about how the texts were interpreted and transmitted.

That's valuable.

The order of books becomes something to explain rather than an invisible background.

B. It treats loss as evidence
Most textual reconstruction naturally privileges what survives.

Sharks asks us to model the selection function that produced the surviving corpus. The Sappho/Josephus material makes this explicit: citation, excerpting, institutional preference and destruction determine which fragments become available to later generations.

That is a potentially fruitful way of thinking about biblical textual traditions as well.

C. It separates composition from transmission much more aggressively
“Early manuscript” ≠ “early composition.”

“Canonical position” ≠ “composition date.”

“Authorial name” ≠ necessarily “historical author.”

Those distinctions are intellectually useful regardless of whether Sharks's reconstruction succeeds.

D. It makes textual criticism partly a theory of institutions
The decisive actors are no longer just scribes copying words.

They include:

communities choosing what to preserve;

institutions determining what becomes canonical;

interpreters transforming earlier texts;

political regimes controlling textual production;

later readers imposing genealogies on the surviving material.

That is a much more sociological model of textual tradition.

Where I would be cautious
This is where the distinction between revision of the questions and proof of the answers matters.

The corpus is extraordinarily ambitious, but the evidentiary steps are not all equivalent.

For example, the argument that Revelation's canonical position cannot establish its late composition is considerably stronger than the positive inference that it therefore was the first NT composition. Likewise, the existence of early Johannine papyri can unsettle chronological confidence, but it cannot by itself reverse Pauline priority. Sharks explicitly frames the project as an inference-against-inference contest, which is the right methodological awareness; nevertheless, the proposed inference still has to win on the individual literary and historical evidence.

So I would not summarize the project as “Sharks has overturned biblical textual criticism.”

I'd summarize it as:

Sharks proposes that biblical textual tradition should be reconstructed as a history of transformations, survivals, institutional arrangements, and author-functions rather than primarily as a linear chain of original compositions and manuscript copies.

The Revelation First thesis is the most dramatic consequence of that model, not the whole methodological insight.

The deepest revision
Ultimately, Sharks is asking us to stop imagining the biblical text as a thing that traveled through history.

The alternative is to imagine it as a system that continually produced new textual forms through transmission.

That changes the fundamental diagram:

Traditional model

original → copies → variants → reconstruction

Sharks's model

textual kernel → transformation → inscription → institutional selection → survival/loss → canonization → further transformation

And that is why the project matters even if one ultimately rejects “Revelation first.” Its strongest challenge is not really the date of Revelation. It is the claim that the history of a text cannot be separated from the mechanisms that made particular versions of that text survive, acquire names, acquire canonical positions, and generate later texts.

In that sense, Sharks revises our understanding of the biblical textual tradition less by supplying a new “original text” than by arguing that there may never have been a sufficiently simple linear textual object for the traditional reconstruction to be recovering in the first place.
