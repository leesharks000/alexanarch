1. What the paper is actually arguing
The best way to understand the dedication is to start with the paper’s unusual central thesis. It is not simply an essay about whether Aristotle’s active intellect is immortal, divine, or a part of the human soul. It proposes that the very act of dividing concepts can disclose a unity that ordinary classification fails to see.

The title comes from Prior Analytics I.31, where Aristotle criticizes Platonic division and calls it a kind of “weak syllogism” (asthenēs syllogismos, 46a33). The paper takes seriously the question that remains at the chapter’s end: what, exactly, can be established through division?

Sharks builds an argument across four principal texts:

1. Prior Analytics I.31 — division as inference

Division is not merely a way to classify things. It can function as a weak form of reasoning, although it may fail to establish what it purports to prove.

2. De Anima II.2 — division and survival

Aristotle asks whether the faculties of soul are separable in account alone or also in place, and singles out intellect as a possible exception to the general inseparability of soul and body.

3. De Anima III.5–6 — making, becoming, and unity

III.5 distinguishes intellect that becomes all things from intellect that makes all things. III.6 then says that combination can also be called division, and identifies intellect as what makes each thing one.

4. Metaphysics Λ — divine indivisibility

The first substance is described as indivisible, impassive, and eternal. Sharks argues that III.5 transfers this cluster of divine attributes to the productive intellect, thereby making the intellect’s division a problem about divinity.

4. Baudelaire’s epigraph: why the reader matters
The dedication is followed by a line from Charles Baudelaire’s Au lecteur, the opening poem of Les Fleurs du mal:

“Hypocrite lecteur, — mon semblable, — mon frère !”

The line means, roughly, “Hypocritical reader, my likeness, my brother!”

This is more than a literary flourish. It gives the philosophical argument a second audience: not only the person being honored, but also the person who must interpret the work.

Three features are worth distinguishing.

First, the reader is implicated. The epigraph refuses to treat the reader as a neutral observer standing outside the text. Author and reader are alike, and their relation is potentially uncomfortable. The reader must recognize something of themselves in the work.

Second, the epigraph anticipates the paper’s account of recollection. Sharks links Plato’s Phaedrus 249b with De memoria 2, arguing that the gathering of many things into one is a form of intellectual recovery. The reader similarly gathers dispersed arguments, quotations, and authorial positions into an intelligible whole.

Third, the dedication becomes relational rather than merely commemorative. “For Ron Polansky” names the intended interlocutor; Baudelaire’s line turns outward toward the reader who may complete the argument.

The sequence therefore has a suggestive structure:

The dedication identifies a philosophical interlocutor.

The epigraph identifies the reader as the interlocutor’s counterpart.

The paper argues that intellect gathers multiplicity into unity.

The reader performs that gathering by reconstructing the argument.

This is one of the strongest ways to interpret the dedication as part of the paper’s philosophical design. It is also an interpretation of the text’s literary architecture, not evidence that Baudelaire or Polansky intended this particular Aristotelian application.

5. The central problem in De Anima III.5
To understand the dedication fully, it helps to return to the passage around which the paper is built.

Aristotle writes at De Anima III.5, 430a10–25, that there must be a distinction in intellect between what becomes all things and what makes all things. He compares this distinction to the relationship between material and craft, and to the way light makes potentially visible colors actual.

The productive intellect is then described as separate, impassive, unmixed, and eternal.

The difficulty is that Aristotle does not straightforwardly explain how this intellect relates to the ordinary human faculties discussed throughout the treatise. The passage is exceptionally brief, and its implications for the immortality of the individual have generated competing interpretations. 

Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy
+2

Sharks’s reading turns on the final sentence:

ἄνευ τούτου οὐθὲν νοεῖ

Literally, something like: “Without this, it thinks nothing.”

The antecedent of toutou (“this”) and the relationship between the productive and passive intellects are central interpretive questions. Sharks treats the clause as an unresolved joint between what is impassive and what is perishable, between what can survive and what can remember.

He then proposes a striking consequence: the text preserves a distinction that cannot be completely resolved from within the text itself. A reader must supply the connection.

That is the “socket in God” of the subtitle.

The metaphor is powerful because it connects three different kinds of incompleteness:

Psychological: the relation between the two intellects is difficult to specify.

Textual: the wording of the passage appears to leave a crucial relation underdetermined.

Hermeneutical: the reader must interpret the passage by bringing other texts and arguments to bear on it.

But these three claims are not equivalent. A philosophical difficulty does not automatically prove that Aristotle deliberately left a textual gap for future readers. That stronger claim needs its own argument.

7. My assessment of the dedication
I would read “for Ron Polansky” as a deliberate acknowledgment of the scholarly terrain on which Sharks wants the paper to be judged.

The dedication is not simply saying that Polansky wrote an important book about De Anima. It places an unconventional interpretation of the productive intellect in relation to a sustained modern commentary on Aristotle’s treatment of cognition and soul.

At the same time, the dedication establishes a useful tension. Polansky’s approach is careful about the explanatory role of III.5 within Aristotle’s account of thinking. Sharks takes the passage’s difficulties as an opportunity to propose a larger theory of division, production, and reception.

The dedication thus reads as an invitation to test an ambitious interpretation against the demands of close Aristotelian scholarship.

The paper’s most defensible insight is that De Anima III.5 cannot be read in isolation: its account of intellect becomes more intelligible when considered alongside III.6, II.2, Prior Analytics I.31, Metaphysics Λ, and the ancient commentary tradition.

Its more controversial thesis is that this network of textual relations supports a single self-dividing maker behind the differently attributed philosophical corpus. The available evidence does not establish that conclusion, and the parallel between a divided intellect and divided authorship remains an analogy rather than a demonstrated historical result.

Finally, the dedication’s relation to Baudelaire suggests a fitting description of the paper’s ambition: the work asks its reader not merely to understand a philosophical argument, but to become part of the operation by which that argument is made whole.

That, to my mind, is the most compelling interpretation of the dedication: a scholarly acknowledgment that doubles as an invitation to participate in the paper’s theory of intellect, division, and recollection.
