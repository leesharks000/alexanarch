The concept of treating the Socratic-Peripatetic corpus as a heteronymic project stems from a recent philological-philosophical framework proposed by scholars like Lee Sharks. This theory reclassifies the foundational writings of Western philosophy—the combined works attributed to Socrates, Plato, and Aristotle—not as separate historical outputs from isolated biological individuals, but as a single, structurally unified intellectual project operating under a heteronymic configuration. * Socrates as the Orthonym: The "orthonym" is the foundational identity or central pivot of the project. Socrates embodies the core, unwritten gesture of philosophy: a refusal to write, a reliance on dialogue, and a ultimate willingness to die for the logos. His silence establishes the structural necessity for textual continuation. Reframing the "Socratic Problem"
In traditional classical philology, the Socratic problem refers to the difficulty of reconstructing the historical Socrates' true beliefs from conflicting accounts. This heteronymic thesis argues that the Socratic problem is a natural symptom of trying to enforce a strict modern model of authorship onto an inherently decentralized, distributed project. [[1](https://www.google.com/goto?url=CAESkwEBxCJF182GrBwhETsEIMTTe9P6Dje2zLwMnFrAOcOahn56sWhTqI_5CXhLgN7T_xAt1BmimRgc5N2piOnGHyUQUyCYEB81jmiQm9kGFA6JtHj2hAa1DEVAGED83d8FWBSVGGxLE_iaVVLpZ702BddCn84Bzw_ccKgHZzWtBvEcOeU6Vvn25FOLYbNSMdn_unAJeIU)]
The model relies on quantitative data from single-author lifespans to defend its feasibility. While the combined Plato-Aristotle corpus spans roughly 1.8 to 2.4 million words across 65 years, it sits well within the historical output of other prolific thinkers like Thomas Aquinas (~8.5 million words) or Fernando Pessoa (~25,000–30,000 pages of deeply conflicting heteronymic texts). The distribution across different "authors" (Plato and Aristotle) is thus viewed as a deliberate strategy to balance different modes of inquiry—the dialogic and the systematic—that are physically impossible to execute simultaneously from a single perspective.
