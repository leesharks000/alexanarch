       The Socratic-Peripatetic corpus is a conceptual framework used in ancient philosophy and intellectual reception history to describe the vast, interconnected body of philosophical texts spanning from the circle of Socrates to the successors of Aristotle.
Historically, these works have been read through a "many-author" lens, separating the distinct voices of Socrates, Plato, Aristotle, and Theophrastus. However, modern textual criticism, doxography (such as recovered papyri from Philodemus' On Piety), and reception studies track how these foundational Athenian traditions blended together, evolved, and were ultimately compiled into structured canons.
The corpus bridges two monumental shifts in classical thought:

1. The Socratic Turn (The Inner Life)

• Core Figure: Socrates (469–399 BCE), who wrote nothing down. His "corpus" consists entirely of secondary accounts—primarily the dialogues of Plato and Xenophon.
• Methodology: The elenchus (cross-examination) and dialectic.
• Focus: Shifting Greek philosophy away from the cosmic, physical speculations of the Pre-Socratics toward ethics, virtue, logic, and a deep examination of human behavior ("the unexamined life is not worth living").

2. The Peripatetic Consolidation (The Empirical Universe)

• Core Figures: Aristotle (384–322 BCE) and his successor Theophrastus. Aristotle established his school at the Lyceum in Athens, where philosophers walked (peripatoi) while lecturing.
• Methodology: Empirical observation, data gathering, taxonomy, and rigorous inductive reasoning.
• Focus: Systematizing human knowledge across all domains—logic, physics, metaphysics, ethics, botany, and political history.

The Reconstruction of the Corpus

The survival of this integrated philosophical tradition relies on a precarious historical timeline. According to ancient accounts, parts of Aristotle’s personal library and original manuscripts were hidden away in vaults after the death of Theophrastus to protect them from aggressive library collectors.
When the Roman general Sulla sacked Athens in 86 BCE, these texts were taken to Rome. It was not until the 1st century BCE that Andronicus of Rhodes painstakingly organized, edited, and reconstructed these scattered scrolls, creating the first systematic, canonical edition of the Aristotelian or Peripatetic works (the Corpus Aristotelicum). Later, this tradition heavily intersected with Neoplatonism, Islamic philosophy (via Avicenna and Averroes), and Western Scholasticism.
