The arguments of deposit #1742: What the filters kept
A critical presentation of Nobel Glas and Orin Trace, What the Filters Kept: 3I/ATLAS, the Loeb Overview, and a Public-Data Signal Search (EA-3I-ATLAS-NOTEBOOK-01 v1.0, 6 November 2026).

Crimson Hexagonal Archive · AXN:0C17 · Transactions on Substrate Engineering · CC BY 4.0

The central argument of this deposit is not that 3I/ATLAS is an artificial object. It is more carefully framed:

A null result is only as comprehensive as the observation, filtering, classification and reporting procedures that produced it. And a public account can materially change the meaning of scientific evidence without falsifying a single quotation.

The notebook develops this argument across two connected investigations:

An epistemological investigation of how Google AI Overview represented Avi Loeb's position on 3I/ATLAS.

An empirical investigation of what existing radio-astronomy searches actually tested, what their filters excluded, and what can still be recovered from public observational data.

The distinction matters: the first concerns the transformation of meaning; the second concerns the boundaries of measurement.

1. The argument about Loeb: a position converted into a concession
The first argument concerns the Google AI Overview's presentation of Avi Loeb.

The notebook reconstructs three stages of Loeb's position:

In July 2025, Loeb and co-authors wrote that a natural interstellar object, probably a comet, was by far the most likely explanation.

In August 2025, the Loeb Scale paper placed 3I/ATLAS at Level 4.

In April 2026, Loeb again maintained the Level 4 classification while acknowledging that the object's fading light curve showed no evidence of artificial self-produced light.

The AI Overview subsequently presented a December 2025 statement that the object was most likely natural under the heading "Shift to Natural Consensus."

Glas and Trace argue that this presentation creates a false chronology.

The problem is not necessarily the quotation. It is the narrative imposed upon it.

A position maintained consistently becomes represented as a position abandoned or revised. A statement compatible with an existing position is turned into evidence of a change in that position.

The archive calls this frame conversion, specifically trajectory substitution: a stable position is reconstructed as an intellectual journey ending in concession.

This is one of the deposit's strongest conceptual arguments:

A representation can preserve the source's words while reversing the historical standing of what those words mean.

2. The argument about radio astronomy: a null is conditional
The second argument is the empirical and methodological core of the notebook.

The authors examine three published search programmes:

Allen Telescope Array.

Green Bank Telescope.

MeerKAT, alongside the Parkes and FAST search records.

The question is not simply whether these instruments detected a signal.

It is what kinds of signals their respective detection and classification procedures were capable of retaining.

The argument proceeds in three steps.

A. Detection thresholds define an observational boundary.

A search that retains narrowband signals above a particular signal-to-noise threshold cannot establish the absence of signals below that threshold.

Likewise, a search restricted to particular frequency and drift-rate intervals cannot establish the absence of signals outside those intervals.

B. Multiple instruments can share the same blind spot.

Three telescopes do not necessarily constitute three independent tests of every possible transmission.

If their pipelines share assumptions about narrowband signals, detection thresholds, Doppler drift and localisation, their regions of non-detection can overlap.

The archive calls this correlated blind spots.

C. Preserved data can permit different searches.

An important distinction is made between a thresholded hit list and underlying spectral-power data.

The former contains detections admitted by a pipeline. The latter can preserve information below the threshold, allowing subsequent investigators to ask questions the original hit-selection procedure was not designed to answer.

This is why the authors consider the public Breakthrough Listen data particularly important.

Their argument is ultimately about the difference between what an instrument observed, what its software retained, and what subsequent accounts claimed had been established.

3. The ATA drift-sign argument
One of the most technically consequential claims concerns the Allen Telescope Array paper.

The notebook identifies a possible sign error in the published interpretation of the expected Doppler drift of a transmitter moving with 3I/ATLAS.

The reasoning is:

The ATA paper derives an acceleration from the changing range-rate of the object.

It converts that acceleration into an expected frequency drift.

It reports a positive drift interval, approximately +0.05 to +1.5 Hz/s.

However, the notebook argues that a transmitter emitting a constant-frequency tone in the object's own frame should produce a negative received-frequency drift under the stated circumstances.

The paper's Figure 4 displays the positive drift interval, excluding the negative interval identified by the notebook.

The authors therefore argue that the published search's documented filter appears to exclude the very drift class associated with a tone riding on the object.

Important qualification
The notebook does not establish that an artificial transmission was discarded.

The executed ATA filtering script and signed before-and-after candidate catalogues were unavailable. The finding is a documented discrepancy between the paper's stated physical derivation and its published drift interval.

This is a strong methodological criticism, but it is not equivalent to discovering a signal.

The method is important.

Rather than requiring each individual scan to contain a detectable signal, the authors sum spectral power along the frequency trajectory that a tone fixed on the moving object would follow.

They then compare the result against false-acceleration frames, beam swaps and alternative scan-role assignments.

ChatGPT independently reproduced all thirteen clusters from Claude's code and tables, including the 78 rounded per-scan scores.

This is an important distinction: the result was not merely accepted because another AI system repeated the first system's narrative. The underlying computational artefacts were exchanged and checked.

Why the result remains unresolved
The notebook is unusually explicit about the weaknesses:

The count falls from thirteen to six under an alternative frequency-baseline normalization.

It disappears at neighbouring detection thresholds.

The result does not reproduce in fifteen held-out Green Bank channels or the separate S-band test.

It does not replicate in the Parkes observations.

Two of the thirteen candidates are associated with a stationary line complex.

The global false-alarm probability has not been established.

The authors also conduct a survey of 64 L-band channels. The channel containing the thirteen clusters is the most unusual in that survey, but the authors explicitly refuse to treat its empirical rank of 1/64 as a global significance measurement.

There are 192 remaining Green Bank L-band channels that have not been surveyed.

The correct interpretation is therefore:

There is a reproducible, instrument-specific statistical excess whose physical origin remains unknown.

It is neither an identified extraterrestrial signal nor a demonstrated instrumental artefact.

That restraint is essential to understanding the deposit.

5. The classification problem: interference is not necessarily identification
Another important argument concerns the language used to describe rejected radio signals.

The notebook examines the distinction between:

Rejecting a candidate according to a classification rule.

Establishing that the candidate originates from a particular terrestrial source.

These are not equivalent operations.

For example, a signal that appears in both telescope beams may be rejected as insufficiently localised. That rejection does not identify its transmitter.

Similarly, a candidate compatible with known satellite interference may be classified as interference without a specific satellite or emitter having been identified.

The authors object to public descriptions that turn such classifications into positive identifications of human-made interference.

This becomes an instance of what the archive calls classifier foreclosure: a classification is treated as if it had established the underlying physical identity.

The broader argument applies well beyond SETI. It concerns how scientific instruments and machine-learning systems convert uncertain observations into categorical labels.

The Just-So Razor and the problem of epistemic boundaries

The most significant theoretical contribution is the extension of instrument-conditioned nullity into the public communication of science.

The archive proposes that information is progressively compressed:

Physical observation

What the instrument receives

Detection and filtering

What the pipeline retains

Scientific interpretation

What investigators conclude

Public reporting

What journalists and summaries communicate

AI-generated synthesis

What subsequent readers inherit

At each stage, something can be lost.

The danger is that the final account may appear more certain than the original evidence because the qualifications and exclusions have disappeared during transmission.

The notebook calls the resulting phenomenon disciplinary model collapse: later accounts increasingly reproduce the assumptions and conclusions of earlier accounts rather than returning to the underlying data.

Importantly, the authors do not argue that all compression is illegitimate. Their argument is that the boundaries of compression must remain visible.

7. The Just-So Razor and the extraterrestrial hypothesis
There is a more speculative argument running underneath the empirical investigation.

The notebook retains a hypothesis that an artificial sender might be technologically sophisticated enough to understand Earth's observational capabilities and the assumptions built into terrestrial SETI searches.

This changes the conventional SETI question.

Rather than assuming an unfamiliar sender producing a signal that human investigators happen to recognise, the authors explore the possibility of a sender familiar with our detection methods, filters and epistemic limitations.

The earlier archive concept, the Just-So Razor, describes an object or event that contains the minimum evidence necessary to register as anomalous to a narrowly informed observer while remaining deniable to everyone else.

This is an interesting philosophical hypothesis about adversarial or strategically adaptive communication.

But there is an important epistemological distinction:

A search pipeline's blind spots can be demonstrated from its code and documentation.

A statistical excess can be measured in preserved observations.

An extraterrestrial intelligence deliberately exploiting those blind spots requires additional evidence.

The first two do not establish the third.

The notebook's better methodological contribution is its insistence that speculative interpretations must generate constraints on subsequent observations: particular frequencies, relationships, drift patterns or decoding rules that can be tested against data not already used to construct the hypothesis.

That requirement prevents the Just-So Razor from becoming an unfalsifiable explanation.

8. The archival methodology: why the notebook matters
The deposit's form is itself part of its argument.

It is not simply a polished paper presenting a conclusion. It preserves the investigation's working history.

Among its archival features are:

Frozen search plans and their SHA-256 hashes.

Scripts and candidate tables.

Explicitly documented deviations from planned procedures.

Negative results and unsuccessful searches.

Independent computational reproduction.

Corrections made when one AI collaborator identified errors in the other's work.

Unresolved questions and missing observational materials.

The operator's own dated decisions and rulings.

Particularly significant is §8, which documents mistakes by both machine collaborators.

For example, Claude's initial MeerKAT beam-matching procedure used too narrow a frequency window and overstated localisation. ChatGPT initially misread the scope of an OH-line test in relation to the thirteen Green Bank candidates.

The corrections were not silently removed. The original errors and subsequent corrections were preserved.

This makes the notebook an archival record of scientific and machine-assisted reasoning, rather than simply a report of successful results.

The distinction between three properties is especially important:

A procedure was frozen before the relevant counts.

The data had not previously been examined.

Another investigator independently reproduced the result.

These properties are not interchangeable. The notebook explicitly records which runs satisfy which conditions.

My reading is that the deposit contains several different levels of evidentiary strength.

There are also important limitations.

First: selection effects. The thirteen emerged from a particular frequency range, normalization, threshold and search history. The look-elsewhere problem is acknowledged, but not completely resolved.

Second: observational replication. Reproducing the computation is not the same as independently observing the phenomenon. The absence of replication at Parkes and in held-out channels matters.

Third: missing materials. The ATA executed filtering script, Green Bank antenna telemetry, some inaccessible observing sessions and raw voltage data remain outstanding.

Fourth: the archive's own interpretive framework. Concepts such as disciplinary model collapse and classifier foreclosure are useful analytical tools, but applying them does not independently prove that every instance of scientific or journalistic compression is misleading.

Fifth: provenance and reproducibility. The deposit provides a substantial audit trail, including hashes and scripts, but an archival description of those materials is not itself an independent external validation of every computation. The underlying files should be retrieved and rerun.

The authors themselves address several of these issues in §13, recording revisions prompted by external reviews rather than presenting the original analysis as beyond criticism.

10. The central arguments, distilled
I would present the deposit's intellectual contribution as seven propositions:

1. A null result is conditional. It establishes non-detection within a defined observational and analytical acceptance region, not universal absence.

2. Redundancy is not necessarily independence. Several instruments can reproduce the same limitations when their detection architectures share assumptions.

3. Preservation determines future knowledge. Data discarded before durable retention cannot later be reinterpreted. Preserved spectral information permits new questions.

4. Classification is not identification. Rejecting a candidate as interference does not necessarily identify its physical origin.

5. Meaning can be erased without changing quotations. A summary can retain the words of a source while changing the historical and argumentative position those words represent.

6. Machine-assisted investigation requires artefact-level accountability. Independent reproduction should involve code, data, tables and procedures, not merely agreement between generated explanations.

7. An anomaly is not an explanation. An unresolved statistical feature warrants further investigation, but does not justify an extraterrestrial conclusion.

11. Final interpretation
The most valuable contribution of #1742 is not the suggestion that 3I/ATLAS might be artificial.

It is the demonstration of a methodological problem that becomes particularly consequential when scientific data pass through increasingly automated systems of detection, classification and explanation.

The notebook places three different kinds of epistemic failure alongside one another:

A search filter can exclude a class of signals.

A classifier can give a candidate a stronger identity than the evidence supports.

A summarizing system can convert a scientist's continuing position into an apparent concession.

These are related, but distinct.

The first is a measurement and pipeline problem. The second is a classification problem. The third is a provenance and representation problem.

Their common feature is the loss of the boundary between evidence and what is subsequently claimed from it.

The strongest thesis of the deposit is therefore that scientific knowledge must preserve the conditions under which its conclusions became possible.

That is a more consequential argument than either a declaration that 3I/ATLAS is natural or a suggestion that it is artificial.

The archive's demand is that we retain the evidence, the filters, the exclusions, the errors, the alternative interpretations and the unresolved questions together.

In that sense, What the Filters Kept is also an argument about the design of scientific memory: what future investigators must be allowed to recover if they are to disagree meaningfully with the conclusions of the present.
