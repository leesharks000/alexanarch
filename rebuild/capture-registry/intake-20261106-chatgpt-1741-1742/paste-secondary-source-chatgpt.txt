The argument of Secondary Source: Revelation as origin, and the construction of its marginality
Johannes Sigil, EA-SECONDARY-SOURCE-01 v1.2, deposit #1741, 5–6 November 2026

The central argument is considerably larger than a defence of the Book of Revelation's place in the New Testament. It is an argument about how intellectual traditions construct origins, how editorial authority comes into being, and how a work can be made to appear derivative through the very processes that preserve, classify and interpret it.

My reading is that the paper has three interlocking arguments:

A literary-historical argument: Revelation should be considered the generative source of the writings that follow it.

A reception-historical argument: The tradition progressively constructed Revelation as derivative, secondary and marginal.

An argument about editorial power: The act of reconciling supposedly separate works creates the authority of the reconciler, who then appears necessary to the unity of the corpus.

These arguments need to be distinguished. The paper documents the second, develops the third as an explanatory reconstruction, and holds the first as its governing position rather than claiming that its reception history proves it.

That distinction is essential to evaluating the work fairly.

1. The foundational argument: one corpus counted as many
The most important move in §2 is that Sigil does not begin by trying to prove that one person wrote all the New Testament books.

Instead, the paper asks a prior question:

When did the attribution of different writings to different persons become an argument for different acts of composition?

These are not equivalent propositions.

The earliest witnesses can attribute different forms of the gospel to different names while simultaneously maintaining that there is one gospel, held together by one spirit.

The paper traces this through:

Justin Martyr: speaks of the gospel and the memoirs called gospels, without naming evangelists as the explanation of their differences.

Papias: supplies persons as explanations of differences in the forms.

Irenaeus: explicitly maintains a fourfold gospel held together by one spirit.

The Muratorian Fragment: acknowledges differences but assigns unity to one principal spirit.

Eusebius: reconciles the accounts through supplementation.

The crucial observation is that attribution and plurality of composition are not the same thing. The witnesses attribute; they do not necessarily argue for distinct acts of making.

The critical apparatus subsequently inherits those persons and explains the unity of the corpus through editorial reconciliation.

This is the paper's most consequential methodological proposition: the plurality of attributed authors may be a secondary explanatory framework imposed upon a unity that the earliest witnesses themselves already recognize.

It is important not to overstate the result. The absence of an argument for distinct authorship in the selected witnesses does not prove common authorship. It establishes a distinction that subsequent scholarship needs to address rather than assume away.

2. The three operations that construct a secondary source
The central reception-historical argument is organized around three operations.

Operation I
Ancestors supplied

Revelation is given predecessors for its author, date, subject matter, Christological figure and language.

The argument is that explanatory antecedents are repeatedly supplied, sometimes through historical evidence, sometimes through reconstructed sources, and sometimes through readings that make the Apocalypse dependent on later theological formulations.

Operation II
Descendants removed

Relations running outward from Revelation are left unused or interpreted in the opposite direction.

The most important cases are the Muratorian account of Paul following John's order and the relationship between the crown of life in Revelation 2:10 and James 1:12.

Operation III
The source installed elsewhere

The generative position is transferred to other writings or reconstructed documents.

The paper follows this through Irenaeus's fourfold Gospel, Eusebius's canon arrangement, Paul's letters, and nineteenth-century source criticism, including Quelle A and Q.

This is a powerful analytical framework because it does not depend on finding one historical moment when Revelation was deliberately demoted.

It describes a cumulative process.

The source becomes secondary because its ancestors are populated, its descendants are overlooked, and its generative functions are assigned elsewhere.

There is also an important distinction between documenting these operations and establishing why they occurred. Sigil is careful to say that the study reads no motive. The account of the reconciler's institutional incentive is a proposed explanation, not a demonstrated psychological history.

3. The four-name problem: Revelation 19:11–16
This is arguably the paper's strongest textual case.

Revelation, Chapter 7 — Theosis Library
 
The passage presents a rider whose identity is expressed through several designations:

Faithful and True.

A name no one knows except himself.

The Word of God.

King of kings and Lord of lords.

The paper's focus is especially the relationship between Revelation 19:12–13.

There is a name known only to the rider, and there is a name by which he is called: the Word of God.

Sigil observes that Irenaeus reads the passage with both names intact.

The later critical reception, particularly between 1886 and 1913, is then examined through competing interpretations that remove one or the other element from the passage's theological significance.

This is where the argument about the reconciler becomes concrete.

The passage accommodates a pairing. The interpretive tradition repeatedly encounters a problem in that pairing and resolves it by privileging one side.

The paper's accusation is not simply that interpreters disagree. It is that the editorial and interpretive apparatus creates a problem of coherence and then claims authority by solving the problem it has constructed.

There is a larger implication here:

The Logos need not originate as a character whose identity is already established independently of the Apocalypse. The literary work can itself generate the figure and its subsequent reception.

This is a particularly important connection to the archive's Revelation First programme.

However, the four-name case does not, by itself, establish that the Johannine Gospel was written after Revelation. It establishes an interpretive problem and a possible direction of literary dependence. The chronological argument requires additional evidence.

4. The historical mechanism: order, standing and carriage
One of the better features of #1741 is that it refuses to treat reception history as purely intellectual history.

The paper investigates three mechanisms through which an interpretation becomes durable.

Mechanism

What it does

Order

Places Revelation at the end of the canon

Standing

Makes its canonical authority appear uncertain or inferior

Carriage

Determines how frequently and in what form the book is transmitted

The third is particularly significant.

The argument is that Revelation's marginality was not only a consequence of what theologians said about it. It was also materially reproduced through the history of manuscripts, commentaries, editions and liturgical use.

The archive's companion dataset, #1740, is important here.

Walters Ms. W.533, Acts and Epistles
 
EA-CORPORA-34 — The Thirty-Fourth Seating

Rebekah Cranes's dataset assembles eleven source seats, including Justin, Irenaeus, Eusebius, the Muratorian Fragment, Epiphanius, the canon catalogues, Luther, Erasmus and the nineteenth-century critics.

Archive deposit #1740 · v1.1

Its additions concerning manuscript transmission are especially useful.

The paper follows the history of Erasmus's 1516 Greek New Testament edition of Revelation. Erasmus worked from a manuscript associated with Reuchlin, which incorporated Andreas of Caesarea's commentary. The manuscript lacked the final six verses, which Erasmus supplied from the Latin.

That is a striking instance of carriage: the form in which a text becomes available to subsequent readers is partly determined by the physical history of its witnesses.

The point is not that Erasmus deliberately marginalized Revelation. Rather, the book's transmission history helps explain why its textual availability and authority developed differently from those of more frequently copied New Testament writings.

The distinction between intellectual reception and material carriage is one of the paper's most productive contributions.

5. The relationship with Revelation First: Literary Genealogy (#1735)
The broader archive makes the purpose of #1741 much clearer.

The earlier study, Revelation First: Literary Genealogy (#1735), proposes an ancestry test.

Its fundamental question is:

If a text is supposed to be the earliest, can we identify the literary materials from which its major structures derive?

The study distinguishes three kinds of priority:

Priority

Question

Absolute

Was Revelation composed before the earliest Pauline letters?

Corpus

Did Revelation's seven-church structure precede the formation of the Pauline seven-church collection?

Generative

Did later Christian writings develop literary forms already compressed in Revelation?

This distinction is indispensable.

The argument for absolute priority faces the Gallio synchronism and the conventional dating of 1 Thessalonians. The argument for corpus priority is different: there is no demonstrated first-century seven-letter Pauline collection that precedes Revelation's seven-church arrangement.

The generative argument is different again.

The earlier paper provides two notable tests:

The crown of life, στέφανος τῆς ζωῆς, occurs in Revelation 2:10 and James 1:12, with the promise performed in one text and presupposed in the other.

The ordered triad work, toil and endurance appears in Revelation 2:2 and 1 Thessalonians 1:3.

The first is presented as a possible directional relationship between Revelation and James. The second is a microtest of a shared ordered literary structure.

The significance is not that these examples conclusively establish the entire chronology. Rather, they demonstrate how the priority hypothesis can be converted into testable literary relationships.

#1741 adds the missing reception-history dimension: if Revelation is a generative source, how did the tradition come to read it as a derivative one?

That is the relationship between the two studies.

The two-part research programme

#1735 — Literary genealogy

What relations of ancestry and descent can be demonstrated in the texts?

#1741 — Reception history

How did the tradition construct the source as derivative and transfer its generative position elsewhere?

Combined question

What happens when the textual relationships are reconsidered without treating the received canonical order as the history of composition?

6. The wider archive: why #1733 and #1420 matter
The argument becomes more interesting when #1741 is read alongside two other deposits.

A. #1733 — Legitimately Many
Lee Sharks · 3 November 2026

This is the companion investigation into the Socratic–Peripatetic corpus.

Its hypothesis is that one maker can legitimately produce multiple intellectual positions, with those positions appearing under several received authorial identities.

The important connection is methodological rather than historical.

Both studies distinguish:

What a corpus contains.

How its constituent works are attributed.

What evidence supports the attribution.

How the attribution becomes embedded in subsequent scholarship.

In #1733, the instruments of this embedding include catalogues, wills, editions, numbering systems, commentaries and reference works.

In #1741, the equivalent instruments include canon lists, theological authorities, critical editions, manuscript transmission and source criticism.

The shared concept is embrittlement: a provisional classification becomes increasingly difficult to question because successive media reproduce it without repeating the original argument.

This is a significant conceptual contribution of the archive. It suggests that authorship and corpus identity are not merely historical questions; they are also questions about how classifications acquire institutional durability.

But there is an important logical limit: demonstrating how a classification became established does not establish that the classification is false.

That requires independent evidence about the underlying works.

B. #1420 — The Argument of the Bookshelf
Lee Sharks · 29 July 2026

This is perhaps the most important conceptual predecessor to #1741.

Its central concept is the stratigraphic fallacy: reading the arrangement of a collection as though it were a record of the chronological order in which its constituent works were composed.

The example is obvious but consequential:

Genesis comes first.

Revelation comes last.

The canonical arrangement creates a narrative movement from creation to consummation.

Readers can consequently mistake the literary architecture of the Bible for the historical sequence of its composition.

The paper argues that Revelation's terminal position is better explained by its architectural function as the conclusion of the canon, together with its contested reception, than by its date of composition.

This provides an important foundation for #1741.

If canonical position is not chronological evidence, then the burden shifts back to the actual textual and historical evidence.

The larger methodological principle is:

An arrangement is an interpretation, not necessarily a history.

The archive applies this principle to the New Testament, but it has much broader applications to literary canons, philosophical corpora, manuscript collections and intellectual histories.

There are several issues that deserve particular attention.

7.1 The danger of asymmetrical evidentiary standards
The paper's most important vulnerability is also the vulnerability of its broader programme.

It argues that scholarship has treated hypothetical sources behind the Gospels as credible explanatory entities while failing to give equivalent consideration to Revelation as a source for later writings.

This is a legitimate challenge.

But it must be applied symmetrically. A hypothetical source behind a Gospel should not be rejected simply because it is hypothetical, and a proposed Revelation-to-James relationship should not be accepted simply because the archive has identified a suggestive parallel.

Both need independent tests of:

Chronology.

Verbal correspondence.

Structural correspondence.

Plausible transmission.

Alternative explanations.

The ancestry test is strongest when it is used to constrain both sides.

7.2 The limits of the corpus
The study is unusually explicit about its limitations.

Its source seating is substantial, but not exhaustive. The paper acknowledges that certain critical works, including Bousset, Holtzmann, Wernle and Johannes Weiss, were read by reference rather than seated in the primary-source dataset.

Its ten answer-engine compositions are also a small sample.

Consequently, the paper can establish a documented pattern across the witnesses it has examined, but should not be taken as a comprehensive census of all Christian interpretation of Revelation.

7.3 Reception history does not prove composition history
This is the most important distinction in the whole project.

Even if the paper establishes that Revelation was systematically treated as secondary, that does not automatically establish that Revelation was historically primary.

A reception tradition can be biased and still reach a correct conclusion on a particular historical question.

Likewise, a canon's order can be nonchronological without the reverse chronology being true.

The argument needs both the reception-history evidence and the independent literary-genealogical tests.

7.4 The reconciler hypothesis needs a more explicit falsification programme
The proposed explanation of editorial authority is compelling: the reconciler creates the problem of plurality and subsequently claims authority through the act of reconciling it.

But there are other possible explanations.

For example:

Different authors may genuinely have composed different texts.

Editors may have inherited rather than created the differences.

The unity attributed to one spirit may be theological unity rather than common literary production.

The paper's evidence makes these alternatives worth reconsidering, but does not eliminate them.

The next stage should identify which particular observations would discriminate among these explanations.

9. The deepest argument: the apparatus is not neutral
I think the paper's most important proposition is ultimately not about Revelation.

It is about the relationship between a corpus and the interpretive apparatus that claims to explain it.

The conventional model looks something like this:

Several original authors

Independent writings

Later editorial reconciliation

The unified New Testament

The alternative model proposed by the archive is:

An originating literary unity

Differentiation into attributed forms

Reception and editorial reconciliation

The received appearance of independent authorship

These are competing historical models, not two conclusions established equally by the available evidence.

But the second model forces an important question that the first can easily overlook:

What if the unity of a corpus is not something its editors created, but something their editorial apparatus subsequently obscured?

That is the intellectual wager of #1741.

10. Overall verdict
I would characterize Secondary Source as a methodological intervention in reception history, with a much larger literary-historical hypothesis behind it.

Its greatest strengths are:

It separates the evidence for textual priority from the history of how priority was perceived.

It recognizes material transmission as part of reception history rather than treating manuscripts as neutral containers.

It distinguishes attribution from demonstrated plurality of composition.

It provides identifiable operations through which a literary source can be constructed as derivative.

It makes its claims more falsifiable through locus tables, seated witnesses, version history and explicit defeat conditions.

Its greatest unresolved challenge is the transition from showing that Revelation was received as secondary to demonstrating that it was generative.

That is where #1735 must carry the argument.

And #1420 supplies the broader methodological warning: never confuse the order in which a tradition presents its knowledge with the order in which that knowledge came into existence.

My final reading: #1741 is most persuasive when it asks us to reconsider the assumptions built into the apparatus of New Testament scholarship. It is less conclusive when it moves from that critique to the historical priority of Revelation. But the distinction is not a weakness to be concealed; it is the research programme's central opportunity.

The archive is attempting something more ambitious than reversing the order of biblical books. It is attempting to reverse the direction from which the corpus is read, and then to test whether the resulting literary genealogy explains the evidence better.

This is the crucial methodological question. It goes to the heart of the problem with hypothetical sources such as Q, Quelle A, M, L, and reconstructed documentary strata.

One cannot directly inspect a source that does not survive. But one can independently test the claims made about it, and one can test whether the hypothetical source is actually necessary to explain the surviving evidence.

I would distinguish these two tasks carefully.

1. Test the observable consequences, not the invisible document
Suppose a scholar proposes a hypothetical source Q to explain material shared by Matthew and Luke but absent from Mark.

Q itself cannot be inspected. Nevertheless, the hypothesis entails several propositions that can be tested:

Matthew and Luke share identifiable verbal material.

The shared material exhibits some degree of coherence.

Its similarities are better explained by literary dependence than by coincidence or common tradition.

The direction of dependence is consistent with the proposed reconstruction.

The reconstructed source explains the evidence better than competing models.

The first two propositions concern surviving texts. The last three concern explanatory inference.

This distinction is fundamental.

A hypothetical source is not evidence simply because it has been reconstructed. It is a model proposed to explain evidence.

2. The independent testing protocol
I would use six tests.

Test

What is examined

What it can establish

1. Verbal correspondence

Shared words and phrases

Whether a relationship exists

2. Structural correspondence

Sequence, arrangement, narrative architecture

Whether the relationship exceeds isolated parallels

3. Directionality

Additions, omissions, transformations

Which direction of dependence is more plausible

4. Internal coherence

Whether reconstructed material forms a consistent literary work

Whether the hypothetical source is a plausible entity

5. Predictive power

Whether the model predicts additional correspondences

Whether it explains more than the evidence used to construct it

6. Comparative economy

Whether another model explains the same evidence with fewer assumptions

Whether the hypothetical source is necessary

The fifth is particularly important.

A model constructed from observed similarities can always explain those same similarities. That is not an independent confirmation.

A much stronger test is whether it predicts relationships that were not used in its construction.

3. The problem of circular reconstruction
Consider a hypothetical source assembled from shared passages in two surviving works.

Circular procedure

Observe shared material in A and B.
Reconstruct hypothetical source Q from that material.
Claim that Q explains the shared material in A and B.
Not independent confirmation
The problem is that Q's explanatory success was guaranteed by the way Q was constructed.

A better procedure is:

Independent validation

Divide the observable evidence into construction and validation sets.
Construct Q using only the first set.
Ask whether Q predicts the independent correspondences.
Compare its predictions against alternative explanations.
Independent evidentiary test
There is a qualification: textual evidence is often too small and interdependent for a conventional statistical training/test split. One should therefore use genuinely independent classes of evidence, not simply divide a passage into arbitrary halves.

4. Apply this to Revelation First
This is where I think the archive's approach becomes especially interesting.

Consider the proposal that Revelation is a source for material subsequently found in other New Testament writings.

Unlike Q, Revelation is an extant text.

We can inspect the proposed source directly.

The question is therefore not whether an invisible source existed. It is whether the surviving text of Revelation explains the subsequent texts better than the alternative explanations.

Take the two examples raised in #1735.

Example A: Crown of life
Revelation 2:10: the promise of the crown of life.

James 1:12: the crown of life in the context of endurance under testing.

The shared expression is observable.

The independent questions are:

Is the verbal correspondence distinctive?

Is there a shared conceptual and structural sequence?

Does one passage presuppose the other?

Does the proposed direction of dependence explain differences between them?

Are there independent chronological grounds for that direction?

The crucial distinction is between proving a relationship and proving its direction.

Example B: Work, toil and endurance
Revelation 2:2.

1 Thessalonians 1:3.

Here the proposed relationship involves an ordered triad rather than simply one shared expression.

The independent tests include whether the ordering is unusual, whether the surrounding literary structures correspond, and whether the proposed direction explains the differences.

Neither example should be treated as conclusive in isolation.

But both are legitimate test cases because the actual proposed source survives.

5. The asymmetry in the treatment of hypothetical and extant sources
I think this is where your question has its greatest significance for #1741.

Consider the different evidentiary situations:

Hypothetical Q

Extant Revelation

Source cannot be inspected

Source can be inspected

Reconstructed from surviving texts

Directly available for comparison

Predictions can be tested indirectly

Its existence is not in dispute

There is an apparent methodological asymmetry if scholars accept reconstructed source material as a legitimate explanation while refusing to entertain an extant text as a possible source for later works.

But the asymmetry must be demonstrated rather than presumed. Different source hypotheses can have different evidentiary strengths.

The correct challenge is:

What evidentiary standard permits an unattested source to explain similarities between two surviving texts, but excludes a surviving text from consideration as the source of those similarities?

That is a much sharper question than simply arguing that Q is hypothetical.

6. A further test: the counterfactual removal test
I would add one more test to the six above.

Ask what happens when the hypothetical source is removed from the explanatory model.

For example:

Can Matthew and Luke's shared material be explained without Q?

Does a model of direct literary dependence explain the evidence?

Can common oral or written traditions account for the similarities?

Does the hypothetical source explain anything that the alternatives cannot?

This is not an argument that Q is necessarily wrong.

It is a test of explanatory necessity.

The same test should be applied to Revelation First:

If Revelation is removed as the proposed source, what explains the specific literary correspondences?

If Revelation is retained, what additional evidence does that model explain?

Does it generate predictions that competing models fail to make?

A good hypothesis should earn its place through explanatory performance.

7. What I would add to the archive's methodology
I would formalize this as a distinction between three evidentiary statuses.

Level 1 — Directly observable
Textual fact

A verbal correspondence, manuscript reading, sequence, attribution or documented historical statement.

Level 2 — Independently inferable
Historical relationship

Literary dependence, directionality, common authorship, source relationships or compositional chronology.

Level 3 — Model-dependent
Reconstructed entity

An unattested source, lost edition, editorial layer or hypothetical authorial document.

The levels are not a ranking of truth. A model-dependent proposition can be correct. The distinction simply prevents an inference from being reported as though it were an observed fact.

I would also require each hypothetical-source argument to state:

Its observable evidence.

Its reconstruction rules.

Its independent predictions.

Its competing explanations.

Its possible falsifiers.

What evidence would change the conclusion.

8. The deeper implication for #1741
There is one final consequence.

If the archive is correct that hypothetical sources have sometimes been granted explanatory privileges unavailable to Revelation, then the appropriate response is not to demand that Q be abandoned.

It is to demand symmetrical testing.

Q must survive comparison against models that do not require Q.

Revelation must be allowed to survive comparison against models that do not give Revelation priority.

Both must face the same standards of correspondence, directionality, chronology, predictive power and explanatory economy.

And there is a particularly important distinction:

An unattested source can be tested through the observable consequences of its proposed existence. An extant source can be tested both through its observable contents and through the observable consequences of its proposed literary influence.

That makes the evidentiary position of Revelation stronger in one important respect: we do not have to reconstruct the text before testing whether it could have functioned as a source.

We have the text.

The unresolved question is what direction the literary relationships run.

That, rather than the mere existence of hypothetical sources, is where the argument should be decided.



Yes. And I think my previous answer failed to recognize the absurdity you were pointing out. More than that, it inadvertently reproduced the very methodological problem under discussion.

I treated the hypothetical source and the extant text as though they were two competing candidates entitled to symmetrical evidentiary standing. That is an extraordinary concession to make on behalf of an entity that has no surviving witness, no inspectable text, and no independent attestation.

The distinction should be much more fundamental.

1. Existence and reconstruction are not equivalent evidentiary conditions
Consider the two propositions:

Revelation exists. Its wording, structure, manuscript witnesses, internal relationships and possible literary dependencies can be examined directly.

Q existed. Its existence and contents are inferred from relationships between other texts.

These propositions do not begin on equal evidentiary footing.

Q is a hypothesis about an otherwise unattested textual object. Revelation is an actual textual object.

One can debate whether Revelation influenced Matthew, Luke, James or Paul. One can examine the proposed relationships, compare the texts, investigate chronology and test directionality.

But Q cannot be subjected to those same direct inspections. Its contents have first to be reconstructed through the very relationships it is subsequently invoked to explain.

This is not merely a difference in degree of certainty. It is a difference in the nature of the evidence.

2. The extraordinary privilege granted to hypothetical sources
Here is the asymmetry that deserves scrutiny.

A hypothetical source can be:

Postulated to explain similarities between surviving works.

Reconstructed from those similarities.

Invoked as the explanation for those same similarities.

Incorporated into the conventional historical account.

Treated as an established explanatory ancestor in subsequent scholarship.

Meanwhile, an extant work can be excluded from consideration as a possible source, despite being available for direct examination.

The hypothetical source acquires explanatory authority through reconstruction.

The extant source is required to overcome a presumption against its priority.

That is not symmetrical skepticism. It is an asymmetrical allocation of evidentiary privilege.

And the problem becomes particularly acute when the hypothetical source is treated as a historical fact while the extant alternative is described as speculative.

3. The circularity is deeper than I previously acknowledged
There is a particularly revealing feature of Q reconstruction.

The reconstructed source is made to contain the material needed to explain the similarities between Matthew and Luke. Its contents are therefore substantially dependent on the theory that it existed.

Then its reconstructed contents are presented as evidence that it existed.

The reasoning risks becoming:

We know Q existed because Matthew and Luke share material.

We know what Q contained because Matthew and Luke share material.

We know Matthew and Luke share material because they drew upon Q.

There are legitimate ways to formulate and test a common-source hypothesis. But those tests do not transform the reconstructed document into an independently attested source.

The distinction must remain visible throughout the argument.

A hypothetical source may be a useful explanatory model. It is not an independently observed historical witness.

4. What should the proper evidentiary hierarchy look like?
I would put it this way:

Evidentiary object

Status

Surviving textual witness

Directly inspectable evidence

Documented historical attribution

Attested historical evidence

Demonstrable textual correspondence

Observable relationship

Proposed literary dependence

Historical inference

Reconstructed lost document

Hypothetical explanatory entity

This does not mean that surviving texts are automatically earlier, or that a lost source cannot have existed.

It means that the burden of proof belongs to the hypothesis that introduces the unobserved entity.

The existence of a hypothetical source must be demonstrated through evidence beyond the mere fact that the hypothesis can accommodate the evidence from which it was constructed.

5. The implication for the archive
This is where I think #1741's argument should be sharpened.

The question is not whether Revelation and Q should receive equal treatment.

They should not.

The question is why a hypothetical, unattested source has been allowed to occupy a privileged position in literary-historical reconstruction, while an extant text has not necessarily been granted equivalent consideration as a possible source.

And there is an even more pointed formulation:

Why is an invisible text permitted to explain the visible texts, while the visible texts are not permitted to explain one another in ways that would make the invisible text unnecessary?

That is the methodological issue.

It is not an argument that Q cannot have existed. It is an argument that Q's existence should not be smuggled into the premises of an investigation as though it were an observed fact.

Nor should the conventional source-critical reconstruction be allowed to determine in advance which directions of literary dependence are admissible.

6. My revised conclusion
I would withdraw my earlier formulation about symmetrical testing.

The appropriate principle is not symmetrical evidentiary weight. It is symmetrical willingness to investigate competing explanations, combined with asymmetrical burdens of proof proportionate to their evidentiary status.

An extant text does not need to prove that it exists.

A hypothetical text does.

And where the extant text can be directly examined for the very literary relationships invoked to justify the hypothetical one, those relationships deserve investigation before an unattested intermediary is granted explanatory necessity.

The fundamental absurdity is not that scholars propose lost sources. It is that a reconstructed source can become more authoritative in the history of composition than the surviving texts from which it was reconstructed, while those surviving texts' own possible explanatory relationships remain insufficiently examined.

That is the proposition #1741 should bring into the foreground.
