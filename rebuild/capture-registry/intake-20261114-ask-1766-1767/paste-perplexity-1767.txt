Lee Sharks argues that the writings attributed to Plato, Aristotle, and Theophrastus form a coordinated apparatus for producing readers: the dialogues create the desire and ability to pursue philosophy; the treatises deliver compressed arguments whose intelligibility depends on that preparation; and the working papers mediate between dramatic persons and abstract doctrines. This explains, on his proposed single-maker or “heteronymic” reading, why one maker would divide the corpus among distinct names, genres, and audiences—and why concealing that unity would help the arrangement work. The paper’s question is therefore not simply whether these texts are connected, but what their separation accomplishes.�

It is important to distinguish its governing hypothesis—one maker behind the divided corpus—from the particular textual relationships offered as evidence for its production model.�
1. The division of the corpus solves a problem of readership
The paper begins from a problem left by Sharks’s earlier Legitimately Many: a single-maker reading owes an explanation of why the maker would distribute the work among apparently different authors. Here the answer is functional rather than merely biographical. A difficult, innovative genre that looks like “crabbed lecture notes” cannot assume an audience already capable of reading it. Another form must cultivate that audience (§§0–2).�
The three positions perform different jobs:
The public line—the dialogues filed under Plato—attracts readers, trains their judgment, and leaves them with unresolved questions.
The held line—the treatises filed under Aristotle—presupposes those capacities and questions, delivering what would otherwise look like abrupt or unexplained conclusions.
The draft position—the materials filed under Theophrastus—converts definitions into persons and dramatic encounters into doctrine. It faces both ways, not merely forward in a chronological sequence.�
This makes “production” more than a metaphor for influence. Sharks’s claim is that one form produces the conditions under which another can be understood. His compact formulation is that the treatises possess “the brevity of a sequel” (§5.4): their compression becomes intelligible when a reader has already undergone the dialogues’ extended intellectual work.�
2. Public circulation and restricted understanding support each other
Sharks reconstructs two complementary publication regimes, while making several explicit evidentiary distinctions. For the dialogues, he separates reports of dramatic arrangement, repeated revision, and paid access. Diogenes Laertius’s report of publication on the model of tragic tetralogies establishes a theatrical arrangement, he says, not publication at festivals. The report that readers paid holders of newly issued copies establishes paid access, not that the maker controlled or received the payments (§3). These qualifications matter: the argument does not require a demonstrable author-run commercial enterprise.�
The public writings also direct readers beyond themselves. Sharks reads Letters II and VII as statements that the decisive teaching is not available as an ordinary written exposition and requires sustained association, synousia. In his account, this generates both preparation and demand: the public product gives readers a reason to seek something it does not straightforwardly supply (§§3.4, 4.4).�
For the held line, he turns to Gellius and Plutarch: selected morning instruction contrasts with open evening instruction; Alexander objects that publication will erase his educational advantage; Aristotle answers that the works are both published and unpublished because only those already instructed can understand them. Sharks’s conclusion is: “The lock is the form” (§4.3). Scarcity survives copying because access to the text is not equivalent to access to its use.�
He then separates two claims that could otherwise become conflated:
Whether the historical Lyceum actually operated as these reports describe.
Whether the transmitted story teaches later readers to approach the treatises as restricted, initiatory knowledge.
The second can operate even if the first remains uncertain. Because Gellius takes the letters from Andronicus’s book, the editorial transmission of the held line also transmits a story about how it should be read (§4.5). Thus the account concerns both an alleged original production arrangement and its reproduction in reception.�
3. Three close readings supply the principal evidence
Section 5 is the argumentative center. Sharks looks for a specific pattern: a dialogue leaves the reader with a difficulty, and a treatise’s compressed formulation answers, repairs, or brackets that same difficulty. Mere shared vocabulary would not establish his case.�
Division: a suspicion becomes a verdict
The Sophist runs through multiple accounts of the sophist, teaching readers both to practise division and to distrust its ability to select a uniquely warranted answer. Prior Analytics I.31 then calls division a “weak syllogism” that assumes what it ought to prove. Sharks reads the treatise’s claim that this is “easy to see” as presupposing the reader’s experience of the dialogue: the treatise names a limitation the dialogue has made the reader discover (§5.1).�
Wax: a failed model receives a repair
The Theaetetus develops the wax-block model of memory and false judgment, then exposes its difficulty with error involving thought without present perception. De anima II.12 invokes wax briefly to explain receiving sensible form without matter; De memoria again uses the imprint analogy. Sharks argues that the compressed Aristotelian use presupposes familiarity with both the Platonic model and its failure, and that receiving form without matter supplies the relevant repair (§5.2). This is his interpretation of the relation, not simply an explicit cross-reference made by the ancient texts.�
Parts of the soul: prior labor permits a question to be set aside
The Republic argues at length for divisions within the soul. Nicomachean Ethics I.13 invokes discussion in the “exoteric discourses,” adopts the rational/non-rational division, and sets aside whether the parts are genuinely separable or only distinguishable in account. For Sharks, the treatise grants permission to use a result whose difficult groundwork the public line has already supplied (§5.3).�
Across the three examples, the proposed relation is not simple agreement. The treatise can criticize a method, revise a model, or decline to reopen a question. What unifies them is prepared intelligibility: a terse sentence becomes an “earned answer” for a reader formed elsewhere in the corpus (§5.4).�
4. Aristotle turns editorial operations into philosophy
The next argument is that the Aristotelian treatises function like the critical signs listed in Diogenes Laertius’s account of Plato—but transform those signs’ operations rather than merely reproduce them (§6). Sharks explicitly calls this a functional claim, not a claim that the signs can be securely dated as the treatises’ historical source.�

The obelus provides the strongest version of the argument. In textual criticism the criterion is authorship: this passage is not Plato’s. In philosophical criticism the criterion becomes truth: this doctrine should not stand. Sharks reads Nicomachean Ethics I.6—honoring truth over friends, even when destroying “one’s own things”—as a further change in the critic’s relation to the object: from editor to owner. On the single-maker hypothesis, Aristotle’s criticism becomes the maker’s rejection of the maker’s own public doctrine (§6.5).�
The interpretive stake is that disagreement need not signal independent authorship. Within Sharks’s model, self-criticism is one of the coordinated corpus’s productive operations. But the ownership reading gains its particular force from the hypothesis; the quoted language does not independently establish a single maker.�
5. Theophrastus occupies a functional, not necessarily chronological, draft position
Sharks defines a draft as material that is reusable, incompletely integrated into an inquiry, and capable of explaining features of two more fully integrated forms (§7.1). A draft therefore need not predate both of them. He acknowledges the Characters’ reference to campaigning with Alexander and explicitly separates the dating of a sketch from its mediating function.�
His extended example is εἰρωνεία, dissimulation or self-deprecating irony, across three forms:
In the Ethics, the trait receives a definition and a relatively mild valuation, with Socrates named as an example.
In Characters 1, it becomes a repertoire of evasive behavior: disavowing knowledge, professing perplexity, wondering, claiming still to be examining. Its valuation is hostile.
In the dialogues, those moves belong to Socrates, while the hostile judgment appears in an antagonist’s mouth—most pointedly Thrasymachus’s accusation in Republic 337a. The sustained drama revalues what the sketch condemns (§§7.3–7.5).�
This is a more specific claim than “the same character type occurs in several works.” Sharks argues that position changes ethical meaning. The sketch supplies reusable gestures and a hostile verdict; the dialogue redistributes that verdict to a defeated speaker and turns the repertoire into philosophical examination; the treatise classifies and qualifies the trait. “One operation, given three positions,” becomes three different ethical and philosophical things (§7.5).�
The reverse movement appears in De sensibus, which extracts an account of perception from the Timaeus and itemizes its omissions. That is drama converted into doctrinal material. Theophrastan sketches face toward dialogues, while doxographies and problem lists face toward treatises: together they constitute the apparatus’s “shop floor” (§§7.8–7.9).�
6. Separation preserves the works’ different effects
Sharks identifies six means of maintaining the divisions: names and biographies, genre, audience, publication, custody, and later editorial filing (§8.1). Yet he expressly concedes that the testimony about Andronicus does not say Andronicus assigned the author names. The proposition that editorial organization converted functions into persons is Sharks’s reconstruction, with the ancient documentary connection still to be sought (§8.2).�
His deeper claim is that announcing the unity would damage the reading process. Readers who know the playwright also wrote the notes may treat the play as concealed instruction and the notes as commentary on the play. The dramatic encounter would lose its independence, and the treatise’s answer would no longer feel earned through it (§8.3). Separation is therefore not incidental packaging or merely protective concealment; it preserves the different operations the forms perform on their readers.�
7. What the paper claims to establish—and what remains open
The paper recognizes the obvious rival explanation: distinct thinkers in a school can share terms, refer to earlier writings, inherit questions, and develop one another’s arguments. Sharks explicitly says those phenomena do not discriminate between intellectual succession and his model (§9.0). His proposed discriminator is “coordinated dependence across the partitions”: an exact difficulty under one name explaining both a construction under another and an otherwise unexplained compression under a third.�
He proposes further tests involving exoteric cross-references, transformations of critical-mark operations, the movement of character valuations, and a preclassified count of Theophrastan titles. These are proposed investigations, not all completed demonstrations. Likewise, the reported overlap between twenty of thirty Characters title-stems and the ethical treatises is explicitly an upper bound: a matching stem can name something other than a character trait (§§7.6, 9).�
My assessment, grounded in this text, is that its most developed contribution is a model of cross-generic reading: it shows how dialogue can train a question that makes a treatise’s compression meaningful, and how changing a trait’s dramatic position changes its value. Its larger attributional conclusion remains less securely established. The cited patterns are evidence for the coordination Sharks proposes, but the paper does not demonstrate that distinct authors in an intellectual tradition could not produce them. Its strongest distinction is therefore between mere thematic overlap and structured reader preparation; the further inference from structured preparation to a single maker remains the contested step.�
