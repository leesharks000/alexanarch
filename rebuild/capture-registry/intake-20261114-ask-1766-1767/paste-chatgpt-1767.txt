Lee Sharks's central argument in “The Production of Readers: The Public Line, the Held Line and the Draft Position in the Socratic–Peripatetic Corpus” is that the division of the Socratic–Peripatetic corpus between Plato, Aristotle and Theophrastus is not adequately explained as the successive literary preferences of three historical authors.

Instead, Sharks proposes that the division is a coordinated system of intellectual production. Its genres, modes of circulation and authorial identities work together to produce the readers capable of receiving its philosophy.

The thesis is stronger than the claim that Plato popularised ideas later developed by Aristotle. The separation itself has a productive function: it creates the conditions under which the corpus can be read.

The argument has three principal components:

The public line: Plato's dialogues produce the desire, questions and interpretive capacities needed for philosophical reading.

The held line: Aristotle's treatises preserve and communicate knowledge in a more restricted form, relying on capacities developed by the dialogues.

The draft position: Theophrastus's works mediate between the two, converting dramatic representations into doctrine and doctrinal dispositions into dramatic characters.

This is the organising thesis of the deposited text, particularly §§0–1, 3–4 and 7–8.

1. The public line: Plato produces the reader
The first argument is that the Platonic dialogues are not simply accessible versions of philosophical doctrine. Their function is pedagogical, but in a more fundamental sense than instruction.

They produce the conditions of philosophical inquiry.

Sharks argues that the dialogues do this by:

Staging problems rather than simply resolving them.

Making the reader experience uncertainty and aporia.

Creating desire for answers that the dialogue itself does not supply.

Training readers to follow distinctions, divisions and arguments.

Establishing an expectation that philosophical understanding requires further work.

The dialogues therefore function as a kind of preparation for the held line.

This is why Sharks emphasises the publication evidence in Diogenes Laertius III.56. Thrasyllus reports that Plato's dialogues were arranged according to the tragic tetralogy. Sharks reads this as evidence of a deliberately dramatic public form, rather than merely a convenient bibliographical classification.

The further evidence in Diogenes III.37 is also important: the opening of the Republic was reportedly revised repeatedly, while the Laws remained on wax tablets. Sharks uses these testimonies to suggest that the public line was a worked, composed and revised product.

The important distinction: Plato does not simply tell readers what to think. He produces readers who want, and are increasingly capable of, the kind of thinking that the treatises demand.

2. The held line: Aristotle preserves the knowledge that the dialogues cannot directly deliver
The second argument concerns the Aristotelian treatises.

Sharks argues that the treatises have a different relationship to their audience. They are compressed, technically demanding and dependent on knowledge that cannot be assumed of an unprepared reader.

Their difficulty is functional.

The dialogues have already established the questions, distinctions and intellectual expectations that make the treatises intelligible. Aristotle's compressed arguments can therefore presuppose work that the dialogues have performed.

Sharks makes this argument particularly through three sets of textual comparisons in §5.

THE THREE DEMONSTRATIONS OF READER PRODUCTION

A. Division
Plato's Sophist stages the difficulty of division and classification.

Aristotle's Prior Analytics I.31 employs a compressed treatment of division.

Sharks argues that the Aristotelian treatment presupposes an intellectual problem that the Platonic dialogue has already made available to its reader.

B. The wax and memory
Plato's Theaetetus 191c–196b develops the wax-block analogy for memory and knowledge.

Aristotle's De anima II.12 and De memoria treat related problems in a more compressed theoretical register.

The argument is that the dialogue develops the reader's capacity to understand a problem whose more technical treatment subsequently appears in the treatises.

C. The parts of the soul
Plato's Republic IV investigates the division of the soul.

Aristotle's Nicomachean Ethics I.13 treats the parts of the soul in relation to ethical inquiry.

Again, Sharks sees a relationship between the dialogue's exploratory treatment and the treatise's more economical theoretical handling.

These comparisons are the paper's most important textual demonstrations. They are intended to show something stronger than shared subject matter: that the treatises presuppose intellectual work performed by the dialogues.

3. The held line has an economic and institutional dimension
Sharks also argues that the distinction between public and held writing is sustained by restricted circulation.

Here the paper draws on two important sources:

Aulus Gellius, Attic Nights XX.5.9.

Plutarch's testimony concerning the two regimes of Aristotelian instruction.

The distinction is between material available to a wider audience and material whose value depends on participation in a course of instruction.

The paper connects this with Gellius's statement:

neque editos scito esse neque non editos

Sharks interprets this as a problem of publication that cannot be reduced to the simple alternatives of published and unpublished.

A text can circulate without being made freely available in the sense that matters.

The argument is that restricted circulation preserves the value of the held line. Its form works with readers who have already undergone the requisite preparation.

This produces a reciprocal relationship:

The public dialogues generate demand for further philosophical understanding.

The held treatises supply a form of understanding unavailable through the dialogues alone.

Their difficulty and restricted circulation preserve the distinction between the prepared and unprepared reader.

The paper calls this the scarcity function of the dialogues: they increase the value of the held line by creating a desire for what it contains.

4. Theophrastus occupies the draft position
This is arguably the most original and conceptually important part of the paper.

Sharks rejects the idea that Theophrastus should simply be understood as the next historical author in a sequence:

Plato → Aristotle → Theophrastus.

Instead, Theophrastus occupies what Sharks calls the draft position.

The definition in §7.1 is precise. A draft is material that:

Exists in a reusable form.

Remains incompletely integrated into a finished work or inquiry.

Functions as an intermediate state that explains otherwise difficult-to-understand features of finished works.

Theophrastus is therefore not merely an intermediary in the sense of transmitting Aristotle's ideas.

He occupies a productive position between the public and held lines, working in both directions.

From doctrine to dramatic representation
Sharks's principal example is Theophrastus's Characters.

The paper examines the treatment of eirōneia (εἰρωνεία), conventionally translated as irony or dissembling.

It compares three representations:

Form

Treatment of irony

Aristotle's Nicomachean Ethics

Defines the disposition as understating or denying one's actual qualities

Theophrastus's Characters 1

Turns the disposition into a recognisable human type

Plato's dialogues

Dramatise the disposition through Socrates and his interlocutors

The point is not simply that all three texts discuss irony.

It is that the same disposition changes its function according to its position in the production process.

Aristotle supplies a definition. Theophrastus gives that definition a human embodiment. Plato's dialogues turn the disposition into a dramatic and argumentative event.

Why Thrasymachus matters
One of the most revealing examples is Republic 337a.

Thrasymachus attacks Socrates for his habitual irony:

αὕτη ʼκείνη ἡ εἰωθυῖα εἰρωνεία Σωκράτους

Sharks argues that the negative valuation associated with Theophrastus's sketch appears in the mouth of a defeated interlocutor in Plato.

This is significant because the dramatic dialogue does not simply reproduce a definition. It places the disposition in conflict, allowing its valuation to become part of the philosophical drama.

The same trait can therefore be:

A defined ethical disposition.

A recognisable character type.

A contested practice within philosophical conversation.

Theophrastus's Characters is particularly important because Sharks reads it as a draft moving from the treatise's abstract analysis towards the polished dramatic form of the dialogues.

Theophrastus also works in the other direction
The argument is explicitly bidirectional.

In De sensibus, Theophrastus extracts and evaluates doctrines from Plato's Timaeus. He identifies what Plato discusses and what he leaves untreated.

Sharks sees this as the reverse operation: dramatic or philosophical material is converted into doctrinal analysis.

Thus Theophrastus is the workshop between the two finished products.

5. Aristotle's critical signs become philosophical operations
Another important argument appears in §6.

Sharks examines the critical signs reported by Diogenes Laertius III.65–66.

These signs are not treated merely as editorial conventions. They are read as operations that distinguish, qualify, reject or otherwise mark textual material.

The most important example is the obelus.

In textual criticism, the obelus marks material subject to athetesis: material regarded as suspect or to be rejected.

Sharks argues that the logic of this editorial operation becomes philosophical when applied to argument itself.

The rejection of a textual proposition becomes something like the refutation of one's own position.

This is another instance of the paper's wider thesis: operations that appear in one register of the corpus can become meaningful in another.

The critical signs provide evidence of a relationship between textual production and philosophical procedure.

6. The separation must remain visible
In §8, Sharks brings the different lines together by identifying six mechanisms through which the division is produced and maintained:

Name and biography: The attribution of works and the construction of authorial identities.

Genre: The distinction between dialogue, treatise and working paper.

Audience: Different readerships and regimes of instruction.

Publication: Different modes of circulation and access.

Custody: Different arrangements for preserving and transmitting texts.

Later filing: The bibliographical and editorial classifications imposed by subsequent readers.

The final mechanism is especially important.

Sharks considers the roles of Andronicus and Thrasyllus in the subsequent organisation of the corpus.

The division is therefore not simply an original compositional arrangement. It is also sustained through the historical processes by which texts are classified and received.

There is a particularly interesting consequence in §8.3: announcing the unity of the corpus would damage its operation.

If readers are told that the dramatic playwright and the author of the technical lecture notes are one and the same maker, they may cease to encounter the dialogues as dialogues and the treatises as treatises.

The genres need to retain their distinct identities because those identities help produce their respective reading experiences.

7. The paper makes predictions, not just interpretive claims
Section 9 is important because Sharks attempts to distinguish his account from the conventional theory of intellectual succession.

The paper identifies predictions that can be tested against the textual and bibliographical record.

Two are particularly significant.

First: the catalogue prediction.

Sharks proposes classifying the titles in Diogenes Laertius V.42–50 according to whether they face the public line, the held line or a third category of physical-data collections.

He predicts that the first two categories together will account for most titles, with the physical-data collections reported separately.

Second: the disposition prediction.

Where the same disposition appears in all three forms, its valuation should change according to its position in the production apparatus.

The irony example is the demonstration: an ethical disposition, a character sketch and a dramatic accusation have different evaluative functions.

These predictions matter because Sharks wants to show that the proposed arrangement explains coordinated features of the corpus that a simple sequence of authorship would not predict.

8. What is the overall argumentative structure?
The paper can be reconstructed as the following sequence:

PUBLIC LINE — PLATO

The dialogues stage unresolved philosophical problems.

Produce desire, questions and interpretive capacity.

HELD LINE — ARISTOTLE

The treatises deliver compressed theoretical treatments.

Preserve knowledge for readers prepared by the public line.

DRAFT POSITION — THEOPHRASTUS

Working materials mediate between both forms.

Convert doctrine into character and dramatic encounter into doctrine.

Theophrastus is not simply a chronological successor: his position is functional and bidirectional.

The central proposition is that the corpus is not just a collection of philosophical works. It is an apparatus for producing different kinds of readers through different kinds of writing.

9. My assessment of the argument
I think there are three particularly strong aspects of the paper, and two important points where its argument needs to be distinguished from what it has conclusively established.

Its strongest contribution is the shift from intellectual influence to reader production.

Rather than asking simply whether Aristotle borrowed from Plato, Sharks asks what sort of reader the Platonic dialogues produce and what the Aristotelian treatises subsequently presuppose.

That is a productive interpretive question. It directs attention towards the relation between textual form, intellectual preparation and philosophical reception.

Second, the draft-position concept is genuinely useful.

It avoids treating Theophrastus as an inferior or derivative Aristotle. Instead, it gives his work a distinct function in the production of the corpus.

The comparison of ethical definition, character sketch and dramatic enactment is the clearest illustration of this idea.

Third, the paper's predictions give its hypothesis some methodological discipline.

The account is not supposed to explain everything retrospectively. It makes claims about catalogue distributions and the changing valuation of dispositions that could fail.

There are, however, two qualifications.

- Textual dependence is not identical to functional dependence. The comparisons between the Sophist and Prior Analytics, the Theaetetus and De anima, and the Republic and Nicomachean Ethics establish interesting philosophical correspondences. They do not, by themselves, establish that the Aristotelian texts were composed specifically to depend on the Platonic dialogues' reader-training function. That stronger conclusion requires additional evidence about composition and historical transmission.

- The historical production hypothesis needs to be distinguished from the explanatory model. The paper's interpretation of Gellius, Plutarch, Diogenes Laertius and the later cataloguing traditions is intended to support a coordinated production apparatus. But evidence of restricted circulation, different audiences and editorial classification does not automatically demonstrate a single organising authorial intention.

That distinction matters particularly because the paper's hypothesis is not merely that the corpus functions as if it were a coordinated system. It advances a stronger heteronymic account of its production.

The historical claim therefore deserves independent scrutiny.

Conclusion
The most important thing to understand about Sharks's paper is that it changes the object of interpretation.

The object is no longer simply Plato's philosophy, Aristotle's philosophy or Theophrastus's philosophy considered separately.

It is the relationship between three textual forms and the readers those forms produce.

Plato makes the reader want and learn to philosophise.

Aristotle gives that reader a more technically demanding philosophical form.

Theophrastus supplies the working materials through which the two forms are translated into one another.

The resulting corpus is a self-differentiating intellectual production system.

In that sense, the title is exact: the subject is not merely the production of philosophical texts, but the production of readers.
