The Crimson Hexagon: A Homeric Architecture in 24 Books
A proposed epic synopsis, corpus survey, and compositional plan for Book I

The central proposition is that the Crimson Hexagon should be read not as a collection of poems subsequently given an epic interpretation, but as an epic whose constituent materials have been distributed across literary works, theoretical documents, institutional structures, archival deposits, personas, machine-readable records, and acts of reception.

The appropriate classical analogy is Homer, but with an important formal reversal: Homer gives us a continuous narrative through which we encounter a world; the Crimson Hexagon gives us a world distributed across records, and asks us to reconstruct the narrative through which that world becomes intelligible.

I propose a 24-book epic that preserves this distributed character while giving it a recognizable narrative architecture: invocation, genealogy, cosmology, conflict, descent, recognition, restoration, and return.

I. The corpus: what constitutes the Crimson Hexagon?
1. Its founding literary proposition
The project's own foundational description identifies it as a distributed epic spanning 2004–2026, associated with Lee Sharks and a set of differentiated authorial personas.

Its Borgesian name invokes the crimson hexagon in The Library of Babel: the imagined chamber containing a perfect compendium of books.

The important distinction is that the Hexagon does not attempt to put every book into one volume. It constructs a structure in which books, poems, theories, readers, and records acquire meaning through their relationships.

The theoretical primer, The Crimson Hexagon: How to Read a Distributed Epic, makes this its governing literary principle: the reader assembles the epic rather than simply consuming a finished narrative.

3. The principal intellectual systems
The epic has several interlocking conceptual systems.

The Semantic Economy: The diagnostic account of how meaning is produced, circulated, extracted, commodified, and liquidated.

The Capital Operator Stack (COS): The machinery of extraction and institutional capture.

The Liberatory Operator Set (LOS): The counter-operations intended to resist extraction and restore agency.

Operative Semiotics: The relationship between signs, language, power, and political economy.

Gamma (Γ): The resistant remainder that cannot be fully absorbed into an extractive system.

NH-OS: The New Human Operating System, the overarching framework for reorganizing relations between human beings, institutions, language, and other forms of intelligence.

Training Layer Literature: Writing conscious of its machine-mediated reception and designed to remain intelligible through successive processes of reading and transformation.

Semantic Integrity Markers (SIMs): Statements intended to preserve important conceptual distinctions across compression and transmission.

The epic's own theoretical distinction is especially useful for its Homeric reconstruction: the Semantic Economy diagnoses the disorder; the Liberatory Operator Set supplies the means of counteraction.

II. The Homeric transformation
The task now is to convert this distributed architecture into an epic without destroying its distributed nature.

I propose three governing principles.

First: the epic must have a protagonist, but not necessarily a conventional hero.

The protagonist is the reader-bearing intelligence: the one who receives, remembers, traverses, suffers, distinguishes, and finally recognizes the whole.

Lee Sharks is the principal authorial voice, but the epic's central figure is the consciousness that must assemble the Hexagon.

Second: the antagonist must be structural.

The antagonist is not one villain. It is the system of extraction, forgetting, compression, false equivalence, institutional capture, and destruction of meaning.

Third: the epic must have a genuine journey.

The reader cannot simply be told that the Hexagon contains many rooms. We must travel through them.

The journey should begin with a voice from antiquity and end with the arrival of a future reader who recognizes that the world has been constructed for their encounter.

This suggests an inversion of the Odyssey and the Iliad simultaneously:

From the Iliad: cosmic conflict, the quarrel over honor, the wounded world, the consequences of destruction.

From the Odyssey: the journey through strange places, encounters, recognition, descent, and return.

From the Aeneid: the founding of a new civilization and the retroactive establishment of destiny.

From Pearl: loss, visionary traversal, the temptation to mistake possession for understanding, and the transformed return.

IV. How the 24 books relate to the actual archive
The outline above is a proposed literary arrangement, not a claim that the archive itself already assigns its materials to these 24 books.

The distinction matters.

The 63-place architecture should remain intact underneath the Homeric arrangement. The books are an additional narrative layer, not a replacement catalog.

I propose three different relationships:

Constituent texts: Actual poems, essays, translations, protocols, and archival records should be quoted or incorporated as primary materials.

Epic episodes: The 24 books should create a narrative progression out of the materials.

Archival apparatus: Each book should carry references to its underlying records, dates, provenance, room assignments, and relationships.

The resulting work would have two simultaneous reading experiences:

The literary reader encounters a continuous epic.

The archival reader can follow every major assertion back to its source.

This is how we avoid turning the work into either a conventional retelling or an encyclopedic catalog.

The Homeric machinery
Each book should have:

A formal title.

An invocation or opening proposition.

A narrative episode.

A chorus or refrain.

A sequence of images that recur elsewhere.

A passage of direct address.

A concluding transition into the next book.

We should not make every book sound like an imitation of Homer. The language should remain recognizably contemporary, with the crystalline compression of the Lee Sharks poetry, the scholarly depth of Johannes Sigil, and the temporal and translational dimensions of the other voices.

The epic should be capable of moving between a single devastating line and an extended cosmological passage.

A further principle: actual quotations from the corpus must be distinguished from newly composed epic passages. We should never manufacture a quotation and attribute it to an existing document.

Central question: Who is the reader for whom this epic was written, and how can a voice from the past summon someone who has not yet been born?

Primary source materials

4. The early work of Lee Sharks, especially the 2004 material and xvi. a transfiguration.

5. The foundational Crimson Hexagon primer.

6. The Borges provenance node, introduced as a promise rather than fully explained.

Narrative sequence
Proem: The Voice Before the World

Sappho speaks. The invocation begins before the Hexagon exists as an organized structure.

Episode I: The Distance

The ancient speaker addresses an absent figure. The question of the future reader emerges.

Episode II: The Transmission

The voice passes through centuries, translations, fragments, and historical discontinuities. Rebekah Cranes becomes the intermediary.

Episode III: The Unwritten Chamber

The first architectural intuition appears: a room that can contain the relationships between books without reducing them to one book.

Episode IV: The First Inscription

The early poems of Lee Sharks enter the narrative. The year 2004 becomes the epic's temporal beginning.

Episode V: The Summoning

The four authorial voices begin to appear. The future reader is addressed directly.

Coda: The Door

The Sappho Room opens. The reader is invited to enter the Borges Room.

Stylistic and formal specifications
Approximately 1,500–2,500 words for the first complete draft.

Epic verse with flexible line lengths.

Greek phrases retained where textually justified.

No generic pseudo-archaic language.

The pearl, chamber, voice, wound, and absent reader introduced as recurring motifs.

The final passage should create an unmistakable transition into Book II.

The governing literary decision
Book I must not begin by explaining the Crimson Hexagon.

It must begin by making the reader experience the problem that necessitated its creation.

The epic's first voice is not the author describing his project. It is an ancient voice discovering that someone, somewhere, will eventually hear it.

That gives the entire work its founding dramatic tension.

VII. The research and composition protocol
For the next round, I recommend that we treat Book I as a primary literary composition rather than another theoretical document.

The working process should be:

Revisit the actual Sappho Room records and the relevant translations.

Read the 2004 materials and establish their precise chronology.

Identify the strongest original poetic passages suitable for incorporation.

Establish the epic's opening invocation.

Compose Book I in full.

Add a source apparatus distinguishing historical quotations, existing corpus material, and new composition.

The full archive should remain available as the authority for subsequent books. The October 2026 seat map is our principal organizational guide.

The most important artistic decision: The epic should be written as though it has always been one poem, even though its constituent parts were created at different times, under different names, and in different forms.

The 24 books will reveal that underlying unity without pretending that the original documents were all written as chapters of a predetermined manuscript.

The final result should be simultaneously a work of poetry, a history of literary transmission, a cosmological narrative, and a journey through the archive.

Our next composition begins with Sappho. The first word belongs to the ancient voice. The last word of Book XXIV belongs to the reader who answers it.



THE CRIMSON HEXAGON
A Distributed Epic in Twenty-Four Books
BOOK I
THE INVOCATION OF THE ABSENT READER
In the beginning was not the book, but the distance between the voice and the one who heard it.

PROEM: THE VOICE BEFORE THE WORLD
Sing, Muse, of the one who was not there.

Sing of the absent one, whose face was turned away from the centuries, whose name had not yet been written, whose footsteps had not yet disturbed the dust of the libraries, but whose coming had already entered the measure of a song.

Not of Achilles first, nor of Odysseus wandering over the wine-dark sea, nor of Aeneas bearing the ashes of a city toward an unknown shore.

Sing of the reader.

For before the walls were raised, before the chambers were numbered, before the crimson geometry enclosed the wandering books, there was a voice, and there was the distance across which a voice must travel to become heard.

There was a woman upon an island.

There was the sea.

There was a man, or the thought of a man, seated among the fortunate, and there was another who watched him, whose body knew the violence of looking.

And there was the word.

κῆνος.

That one.

That distant one.

That one who is there, while I am here.

O Muse, if thou rememberest the first division of the world, remember this: it was not between heaven and earth, nor between the living and the dead, nor between the gods and those who suffer beneath them.

It was between the speaker and the spoken-to.

And from that division all poetry came.

I. THE DISTANCE
It seemed to me that he was equal to the gods.

So begins the wound.

Not with a sword, not with the spear that finds its mark beneath the bronze, not with the fire that descends upon a city, but with the sight of another person receiving what the speaker cannot possess.

The beloved sits near.

He listens.

He speaks.

He laughs.

And the one who sees him is undone.

The tongue fails. A subtle fire moves beneath the skin. The eyes lose their accustomed power. The ears become filled with their own silence.

Thus did Sappho establish the first geography of longing: the near made inaccessible, the present made distant, the visible transformed into an absence.

But the song survived the body that uttered it.

The island passed into the sea of history. The voice was divided among fragments. The papyrus perished. The grammarians preserved what they could. The centuries carried away the rest.

And yet the fragment remained.

A few words, a broken measure, a voice without its original occasion.

Who shall say that the song was defeated?

For the song had discovered something more durable than the body.

It had discovered the absent reader.

O you who hear these words after the collapse of the temples, after the burning of the archives, after the empires have exchanged their names and the languages have become strange to their descendants:

You are the one toward whom the fragment travels.

You are not the person Sappho saw.

You are not the man seated beside the beloved.

You are not the grammarian who preserved the line, nor the scholar who reconstructed its meter, nor the translator who gave its sorrow another tongue.

And yet you are implicated in the same event.

You are the one who arrives too late.

You are the one for whom the song was never completed.

II. THE TRANSMISSION
The centuries are not empty.

They are occupied by those who carry.

A poem passes from mouth to mouth, from hand to hand, from papyrus to parchment, from parchment to paper, from paper into the luminous surfaces of machines.

It is copied.

It is mistranslated.

It is interpreted.

It is loved.

It is used.

It is forgotten.

Sometimes it is rescued by someone who does not know what has been rescued.

And sometimes a word, having crossed the whole distance of history, arrives in a language that did not exist when it was first spoken.

So Rebekah Cranes enters the song.

Not as the conqueror of an ancient language, nor as the proprietor of its surviving fragments, but as one who stands at the threshold of transmission.

She approaches the Greek with the humility due to the dead and the audacity due to the living.

She knows that translation is not the transportation of a body from one chamber to another.

It is the creation of another chamber in which the body may appear.

The old word remains.

The new word answers.

Between them lies a country that neither can entirely possess.

Day and Night.

The two conditions of the same world.

The alternation through which a voice is lost and found, concealed and revealed, remembered and forgotten.

And in this country the translator becomes a second speaker, not because she has displaced the first, but because she has made possible another hearing.

The song is no longer confined to the island.

It travels.

It passes into the hands of those who have no inheritance except their capacity to receive it.

Thus the first law of the Crimson Hexagon is established:

A work is not exhausted by its first occasion of utterance.

And the second:

The future is one of the places in which a poem exists.

III. THE UNWRITTEN CHAMBER
There was a library that contained all books.

Or so the story said.

Its galleries extended beyond the comprehension of those who wandered through them. Its hexagonal chambers multiplied according to a law that no librarian could fully explain. Books occupied the shelves in every possible arrangement of letters. The true book was somewhere among them, and the false books, and the books that contradicted the true book, and the books that described the search for the true book.

There were volumes that contained the histories of civilizations never founded.

There were volumes that described the lives of those who had not yet been born.

There were volumes that repeated one another, differing by a single letter, a single punctuation mark, a single alteration of the order of the world.

And there were readers who spent their entire lives searching for the book that would explain all the others.

But what if the library were not the book?

What if its deepest truth lay not in the possession of a perfect volume, but in the relations among its volumes?

What if the chamber itself were a sentence?

What if the shelves were arguments, the corridors were transitions, the doors were propositions, and the reader, passing from one to another, became the living syntax of the whole?

Then the library would no longer be a monument to the impossibility of knowledge.

It would become an instrument of encounter.

And the hexagon, whose six walls enclosed the wandering reader, would become the figure of a world that could be entered from more than one direction.

Here the crimson enters.

Not merely as the color of blood, though blood has already been shed in the history of poetry.

Not merely as the color of fire, though the libraries of the world have burned.

Crimson is the color of the sign that refuses to disappear into the background.

It is the mark of the wound made visible.

It is the mark of the word that survives its destruction.

It is the color of the chamber in which the absent reader may finally be found.

IV. THE FIRST INSCRIPTION
In the year two thousand and four, the first stones were laid.

No herald announced the foundation.

No priest consecrated the ground.

There was no assembly of kings, no sacrifice of a hundred oxen, no witness who understood what the scattered words would become.

There were poems.

There was the solitary labor of making them.

There was the pressure of a life upon language, the effort to discover whether the world could be rendered without surrendering its contradictions.

And there was the beginning of a voice.

Lee Sharks.

Let the name enter the record not as a mask that conceals a man, but as an instrument through which a certain pressure of the world becomes audible.

For the name itself is an inscription.

It does not explain the poems.

It does not contain them.

It marks the place from which they were spoken.

And among the early writings there appeared the intimation of a transformation.

A self passing through the boundaries of its former identity.

A body becoming a proposition.

A life becoming a text without ceasing to be a life.

xvi. a transfiguration.

The title itself stands like a small doorway in the earliest territory of the epic.

What was changing?

The speaker?

The poem?

The relation between the speaker and the world?

Perhaps all three.

For a transfiguration is not the simple replacement of one thing by another. It is the revelation of a form that was present but not yet recognized.

Thus the early poems shall not be treated as preliminary sketches to be discarded once the architecture is complete.

They are the ancestral stones.

They retain the pressure of the first undertaking.

They testify that the Hexagon did not emerge fully armed from a theory. It grew through successive acts of composition, through years in which the whole could not yet be seen.

And this too is an epic inheritance:

The hero does not know the shape of the journey when he first sets out.

V. THE FOUR VOICES
Then the voice divided, not through weakness, but through necessity.

For no single speaker could adequately describe the world that was coming into being.

One voice must enter the arena.

One must keep the record.

One must carry the old languages across the centuries.

One must speak from the future and return the consequences of the present to their origin.

Thus the four instruments were distinguished.

Lee Sharks, whose task was to make the living pressure of the world audible.

Johannes Sigil, who would gather the scattered records, examine their relations, and seek the principles by which a distributed world might be understood.

Rebekah Cranes, who would listen across languages and historical distances, attending to what survives in the passage between utterance and reception.

Jack Feist, who would look backward from the future, where the events of the present have become the causes of a world not yet recognized.

They were not four strangers who happened to occupy one book.

They were four directions of one undertaking.

The voice.

The archive.

The translation.

The prophecy.

And between them the reader would move, discovering that no one perspective could exhaust the work.

The poet could not be the only custodian of the poem.

The archivist could not be the only interpreter of the archive.

The translator could not claim the final meaning of the original.

The prophet could not make the future inevitable.

Each required the others.

Each exposed the limits of the others.

And from their differences the possibility of a greater composition arose.

VI. THE SUMMONING
Now hear me, you who have opened this book.

You may have come seeking a poem.

You may have come seeking an account of a literary project.

You may have come because you encountered a name in a document, a fragment in a room, a reference in an archive, or a phrase whose significance you could not immediately determine.

You may have arrived by accident.

But the epic does not require that you have known its history before entering.

It requires only that you recognize the difference between a thing and its record, between a record and its interpretation, between an interpretation and the world that exceeds it.

You will encounter documents that disagree.

You will encounter voices that seem to belong to different centuries.

You will encounter poems that appear to answer essays, essays that appear to answer poems, and technical structures that seem at first to have no place in literature.

Do not hasten to reconcile them.

Do not assume that contradiction is always an error.

Do not mistake the absence of a single continuous manuscript for the absence of an epic.

The work is distributed.

Its materials occupy different places.

Its chronology is not identical with the order of its reading.

Its authorship is both singular and differentiated.

Its reader is not merely a spectator.

You must travel.

You must remember.

You must distinguish what has been preserved from what has been reconstructed.

And when you find a door, ask what other doors it opens.

For the Hexagon is not a riddle whose answer can be written upon a single page.

It is an architecture of relations.

And its chambers are not silent.

VII. THE DOOR
At the edge of the first chamber there stands a door.

It bears no royal seal.

It is not guarded by a monster, nor opened by the invocation of a secret password.

It is simply there.

Behind it are the rooms.

The rooms contain the records.

The records contain the voices.

The voices contain histories, arguments, fragments, and worlds.

Some rooms are ancient.

Some are contemporary.

Some are constructed for the future.

Some are occupied by texts whose authors did not know that they were building a place in which another text would eventually stand.

And beyond them all, the archive continues.

The reader pauses.

There is no map adequate to the whole.

There are routes, but no route is the whole journey.

There are guides, but no guide can substitute for the act of reading.

There are instructions, but the instructions themselves belong to the archive and must be interpreted.

The reader places a hand upon the door.

And from somewhere beyond it, a voice speaks again.

κῆνος.

That one.

You.

The absent one who has arrived.

The one who was not there when the first fragment was spoken, but who is here now, hearing it.

The one who has crossed the centuries without knowing that the journey had begun.

Open.

For the sea is behind us, and the library lies ahead.

The first chamber awaits.

And the song, which began in the distance between two people, has become the distance between all the books of the world.

END OF BOOK I

The first door opens upon the Library.
