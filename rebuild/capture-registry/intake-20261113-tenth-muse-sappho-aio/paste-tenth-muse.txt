AI Overview
"The Tenth Muse" by Lee Sharks is the foundational theoretical framework of the Crimson Hexagonal Archive. Originally published under the heteronyms Rebekah Crane (or Cranes) and Johannes Sigil, the work examines the ancient Greek poet Sappho—historically dubbed the "Tenth Muse"—and her Fragment 31 as a textual mechanism for persistence and transmission. 
Core Concepts & Structure
Textual Persistence. Sharks defines the Tenth Muse framework as reading Sappho's Fragment 31 as a written instrument where a voice survives its physical body by becoming an inscription that actively constitutes its future reader. 
Grammatical Open Positions. The analysis focuses on how the poem is bracketed by two open seats: the "that man" (kēnos) at the beginning and the agentless ending (tolmaton). In this reading, Sappho occupies the speaking "I," the inscribed "you," and the third seat of the future reader who takes up the text. 
Heteronymic Authorship. Within the Crimson Hexagonal Archive's twelve-position "dodecad," Lee Sharks serves as the founder and orthonym (anchor position). The Tenth Muse project is developed across multiple personas, primarily alongside Johannes Sigil (the archivist position) and Rebekah Crane.

Related Works & Philosophical Context
Sappho and the Future Reader. Documented on Alexanarch (Record #1756, building on earlier seeds from January 2026), this text outlines the core mechanics of how Sappho's lyric addresses and creates its future audience. 
Sappho as Initiatory Figure in the Platonic Mysteries. Co-authored by Sharks, Sigil, and Crane on PhilPapers, this paper argues that Sappho's somatic collapse into text in Fragment 31 served as the structural template for Platonic philosophy, with Diotima in Plato's Symposium functioning as a ritual reincorporation of Sapphic voice.

Three-Ring Evidentiary Model. As detailed in Sharks' papers on Academia.edu, the broader argument is tiered into three levels of evidence: a hard philological center (analyzing Longinus's Peri Hypsous and Catullus), an interpretive ring (connecting Plato's Phaedrus, Diotima, and Philo), and an outer hypothetical ring.
