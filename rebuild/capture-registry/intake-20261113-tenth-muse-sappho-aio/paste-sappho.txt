1. How Sappho Was Made "Jealous"
In antiquity, Sappho Fragment 31 (Phainetai moi) was most famously preserved inside Longinus’s treatise On the Sublime as an example of poetic mastery—specifically, her skill in selecting and binding together extreme physical sensations. Transforming the poem into a biographical confession of romantic jealousy required specific modern interventions:

Conjectural Endings (1816). The transmitted Greek text of Fragment 31 cuts off abruptly at line 17 ("but all must be endured, since even a poor man..."). In 1816, German philologist Gottfried Hermann rewrote and completed the final lines (refinxi) to supply a narrative resolution. Later readers treated the resulting jealousy theme as Sappho’s own words rather than a modern editorial reconstruction. 
19th- and 20th-Century Critical Consensus. Scholars like William Mure (1850), H. J. Heller (1856), and Denys Page (1955) codified the "jealousy reading". Page explicitly proposed amending the ancient view of the poem from "a love in ecstasy" to "a love in jealousy".
Merging Biography with Lyric Voice. Later traditions collapsed two separate ancient figures into one: the respected poet of Lesbos and a legendary, lovelorn woman (popularized by Ovid's Heroides) who leaped from the Leucadian cliffs over a man named Phaon. Once the speaker's physical symptoms in Fragment 31 were read as literal autobiography rather than crafted art, "jealousy" became the default lens through which the poem was taught and titled in anthologies.

2. How Sappho Was Made "Illiterate" (or Non-Writing)
Ancient vase paintings and Hellenistic scholarship depicted Sappho holding scrolls, selecting verses, and compiling books. The modern assumption that she did not write her poetry down emerged from how later scholars categorized early Greek poetry:
The Oral-Performance Model. Modern classical scholarship largely groups 7th-century BCE lyric poets with earlier oral traditions like Homer. Because Sappho’s poems were sung to the lyre at social gatherings and symposia, scholars emphasized oral composition and live performance over written text. 

YouTube
The "Reperformance" Thesis. Scholars such as André Lardinois argued that if Sappho’s poems were written down early on, the text functioned merely as a memory aid or script for future musical reperformances rather than as a deliberate literary book.
Displacement of Authorial Agency. As Lee Sharks argues in Invidens Sappho, stripping away Sappho’s role as a writer goes hand-in-hand with the jealousy reading. If she is viewed purely as a woman overcome by raw, spoken emotion—and if her survival across 2,600 years is credited entirely to later male scribes and Alexandrian editors—she loses agency as a conscious craftsperson who engineered her own literary survival.
