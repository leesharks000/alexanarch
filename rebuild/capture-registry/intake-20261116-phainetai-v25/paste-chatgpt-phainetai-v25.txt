The argument of “ΦΑΙΝΕΤΑΙ ΜΟΙ: Sappho 31 and the Inscription of the Future Reader (v2.5)”
Lee Sharks's central argument is that Sappho's Fragment 31 is not primarily a poem about jealousy over a man sitting beside a beloved woman. It is a poem that anticipates its own transmission: Sappho imagines a future reader sitting opposite her voice after her living body has disappeared.

The article proposes that the poem dramatizes the transformation of a performing person into a textual presence. Its bodily symptoms, its unusual description of colour, its grammatical choices, and even its surviving textual ending all contribute to that argument.

This is a deliberately revisionist reading. Sharks does not simply claim that the conventional jealousy interpretation is impossible. Rather, the article argues that it explains some of the poem's content but fails to explain its structure, sequence, and ending.

The argument is developed through four principal claims, followed by an examination of the textual crux and a systematic comparison with the jealousy interpretation. 

Alexanarch

1. The opening describes the future reader
The first argument concerns the opening:

φαίνεταί μοι κῆνος ἴσος θέοισιν
ἔμμεν᾽ ὤνηρ, ὄττις ἐνάντιός τοι
ἰσδάνει καὶ πλάσιον ἆδυ φωνεί-
σας ὐπακούει

Conventionally, this describes a man who appears godlike because he sits opposite the beloved woman, close enough to hear her sweet voice and laughter.

Sharks accepts that this is a grammatically possible reading. But he focuses on the combination of two words:

κῆνος — that one, that person.

ὄττις — whoever, whoever it is.

The demonstrative points to someone at a distance, while the indefinite relative characterizes the person by a position rather than identifying a particular individual.

The distinction matters. The phrase does not have to mean one specific man. It can mean whoever occupies that seat.

Sharks compares this construction with the famous Nestor's Cup inscription from Pithekoussai (c. 735–720 BCE). That inscription addresses whoever will drink from the cup in the future. Its combination of an indefinite relative and a distal demonstrative similarly anticipates an unknown future user.

The implication is that Sappho's poem also designates a position for someone who has not yet arrived.

That person is the future reader: someone sitting opposite the poem, listening to a voice whose original speaker is no longer physically present.

The article's crucial interpretive move is therefore to transform the opening's spatial arrangement into a temporal one:

The speaker is separated from the future receiver by time.

The receiver occupies the position opposite the voice.

The poem remains available for successive people to occupy that position.

The man who appears equal to the gods is, on this reading, godlike because he can encounter the poet's voice without needing her living presence.

2. The addressee is the transmitted voice itself
The second argument concerns the identity of the person addressed as toi and se.

The conventional interpretation identifies this person as a beloved woman. Sharks acknowledges that Sappho frequently describes women through very few physical characteristics.

But he identifies a significant difference in Fragment 31.

In other poems, Sappho's descriptions of women include visible or tangible features: Anaktoria's step and face, for instance, or Dika's hands.

Here, however, the addressee is looked at, but no visible physical characteristic is described.

What the poem gives us instead is:

A voice.

A desirable laugh.

Sharks argues that this is not merely an unusually sparse portrait. It is a deliberate choice: the addressee is represented exclusively through sound.

He proposes that the addressee is the voice itself, understood as Sappho's poetry in the process of being received.

This produces an important reversal. The speaker watches someone listen to her voice, and the experience of seeing that reception overwhelms her own capacity to speak.

The passage becomes a representation of the poet witnessing her own future transmission.

In Sharks's formulation, the poem is a scene of the maker's reception: Sappho imagines her voice being heard by someone who will encounter it after her physical disappearance.

3. The bodily catalogue describes the withdrawal of the performing body
The third argument concerns the sequence of physical symptoms in the middle of the poem.

Sharks does not deny the conventional interpretation that the catalogue describes erotic seizure. Indeed, he accepts Longinus's famous observation that Sappho selects and binds together the extreme symptoms of erotic madness.

His objection is that this explains what the catalogue contains, but not why its symptoms occur in this particular order.

He reconstructs the sequence as follows:

The voice fails.

The tongue breaks.

Fire runs beneath the skin.

The eyes lose their capacity to see.

The ears begin to ring.

Sweat and trembling seize the entire body.

This sequence, Sharks argues, is not simply an accumulation of symptoms. It is a progressive shutdown of the capacities involved in live performance.

The distinction between the voice and the tongue is especially important. The voice fails first, followed by the physical instrument of speech. The article also observes that the hiatus in the Greek at the tongue's breaking makes the reader's own mouth enact the interruption.

The resulting interpretation is a kind of subtraction:

The speaker loses her ability to speak.

She loses her ability to see.

She loses her ability to hear.

Her body is progressively overcome by physical disturbance.

What remains is the voice detached from the living body: something that can survive and be received by another person.

This is the article's central structural argument. The opening gives the receiver proximity, hearing and access to the voice; the catalogue then removes those capacities from the speaker in an ordered progression.

The poem is therefore not merely describing a person overwhelmed by erotic emotion. It is dramatizing the transfer of presence from a living performer to a voice that can outlast her.

4. χλωροτέρα ποίας: the transformation of the body into writing material
How Ancient Egypt Invented Paper, Created the World's Most Beautiful Writing — and Filed Their Dead with Magical Cheat Sheets | Book of World History
 
This is arguably the article's most ambitious philological argument.

Its focus is the notoriously difficult line:

χλωροτέρα δὲ ποίας ἔμμι

Conventionally rendered something like: "I am greener/paler than grass," depending on the interpretation of χλωρός.

Sharks challenges the conventional translation of χλωροτέρα as simply paler.

He begins with the four senses distinguished in the lexicon:

Greenish-yellow or pale green.

Yellow.

Pale, particularly of people.

Fresh or moist, as opposed to dry.

The fourth sense is crucial. χλωρός can describe the living freshness of plant material, and its opposite is not another colour but dryness.

The conventional pallor interpretation consequently encounters three difficulties.

First: fear has to be introduced. In Homer, the pallor of a person described by χλωρός is associated with fear. But Sappho's catalogue does not explicitly describe fear. Longinus supplies the explanation that the speaker is afraid or nearly dead.

Second: the comparison with grass becomes problematic. Grass is paradigmatically green and fresh. Calling someone paler than grass requires an interpretive shift between different senses of the same word.

Third: translators repeatedly modify the grass. Sharks surveys 24 translations and adaptations, from Theocritus to 1925. Seventeen do not preserve the line as transmitted. Some omit it, some substitute another comparison, and others preserve the grass but make it dry.

Examples include:

Swinburne's summer grass.

Symonds's autumn grass.

Storer's sun-scorched grass.

Robinson's wilted grass.

Cox's dried grass.

Sharks argues that this recurring addition is significant: translators repeatedly introduce dryness even though the Greek does not explicitly say that the grass is dry.

He proposes that the difficulty disappears if we understand the comparison as a transformation from living plant to writing surface.

The papyrus argument
The article then brings in Theophrastus and Pliny.

Papyrus begins as a living Nile marsh plant. Its pith is cut into strips while fresh, arranged and pressed, and eventually dried in the sun. The resulting sheet is pale, smooth and suitable for writing.

Sharks sees this manufacturing process as corresponding to the poem's bodily sequence:

Sappho's poem

Papyrus production

Sweat pours down

Wetting

Trembling seizes the body

Physical processing

χλωροτέρα ποίας

Transformation from living plant to pale material

The speaker approaches death

The plant ceases to be living material

The voice survives

The writing surface carries the voice

The analogy is not presented as a literal description of papyrus manufacture in Sappho's poem. It is an interpretation of the poem's imagery through the material history of writing.

The final destination is particularly important. Pliny describes writing material as essential to human memory and as the basis of human immortality.

For Sharks, the speaker becomes analogous to the plant that is transformed into a sheet: the living body disappears, but the surface remains capable of carrying something beyond that body's lifetime.

Why this is not simply an argument about metaphor
The paper makes a larger claim about the relationship between eros and immortality.

It invokes Diotima's speech in Plato's Symposium, particularly the argument that eros is fundamentally directed toward immortality.

Mortals participate in immortality by producing something that survives them. Poets achieve this through the works and memory they leave behind.

Sharks interprets Sappho's bodily withdrawal as a version of this process: the poet's body gives way to the surviving voice.

The article also invokes Maximus of Tyre, who identifies Sappho's erotic art with the erotic art of Socrates and Diotima.

Sharks goes further, advancing the identification of Sappho with Diotima herself. However, importantly, he says that the argument about Fragment 31 does not depend on establishing that identification. What matters is the shared conception of eros as directed toward what survives the mortal body.

Thus, the future-reader interpretation is not an anti-erotic reading. It relocates the object of eros: away from an exclusively interpersonal love triangle and toward the future life of the poetic voice.

5. The broken ending is an invitation to the future reader
The fifth argument concerns the poem's textual transmission.

The surviving text is transmitted through Longinus's On the Sublime, 10.2–3. There is no surviving papyrus that establishes what followed.

The crucial transmitted words are:

ἀλλὰ πᾶν τόλματον

Roughly: "But everything is possible to dare" or "But all can be dared."

The subsequent words, including †καὶ πένητα†, are textually problematic.

Sharks draws attention to two things.

First, the six words following the fourth stanza have no Aeolic linguistic markers, unlike the preceding poem. He also invokes Bergk's earlier proposal that the problematic continuation belongs to Longinus's own prose rather than to Sappho.

Second, the expression πᾶν τόλματον is impersonal. It does not specify who performs the daring.

This is particularly significant because the preceding poem repeatedly uses the first person to describe the speaker's experience. At the exact point of the adversative turn, the first person disappears.

Sharks argues that translations such as "But I must dare all" introduce an agent that the Greek does not provide.

The poem thus ends, in its surviving form, with two open positions:

The person who sits opposite the voice: ὄττις ἐνάντιός τοι.

The person who undertakes the daring: the unnamed agent of τόλματον.

Sharks identifies these positions with one another.

The receiver who sits opposite the voice inherits the capacity to act that the speaker has lost.

This is the culmination of the transfer argument: the speaker loses her bodily capacities, while the receiver acquires the position from which the poem's final possibility can be enacted.

The proposed fifth stanza
Sharks also proposes a restoration of a fifth stanza:

ἀλλὰ πᾶν τόλματον· ἐπεί σύ, Κύπρι,
καὶ πένητα καὶ βασιλῆα σώζεις,
κἄτι τοὶς στρούθοις ὀλίγοις φυλάσσεις·
γράμμασι ζώοι.

His translation:

But all can be dared. Since you, Kypris, save beggar and king alike, and keep even the little sparrows, let it live on in letters.

The restoration is constructed partly through a comparison with Catullus 51.

Catullus closely translates the opening of Sappho 31, compresses its bodily catalogue, and then adds a final stanza about the destructive power of leisure.

Sharks argues that this final stanza is not merely an unrelated Roman moralizing addition. It preserves the structural elements of Sappho's ending but reverses their direction:

Sappho's turn is toward endurance; Catullus's toward destruction.

Sappho's universal claim is that all can be dared; Catullus's is that leisure destroys.

Sappho's comparison descends toward the vulnerable; Catullus's invokes kings and prosperous cities.

By reversing Catullus's reversal, Sharks reconstructs an ending in which Aphrodite preserves the beggar, the king and even the little sparrows.

The sparrows connect the restoration with both Sappho's first fragment, in which sparrows draw Aphrodite's chariot, and Catullus's poems 2 and 3, which feature Lesbia's sparrow and its death.

The final phrase, γράμμασι ζώοι, means approximately "let it live on in letters."

That is a particularly apt ending for the article's thesis.

An important qualification: Sharks explicitly acknowledges that this restoration is conjectural. The word γράμμασι is a restoration author's own, not an attested word from Sappho's text. He offers the fifth stanza as an illustration of how the open ending might be filled, not as documentary evidence that Sappho actually composed it.

The argument about the future reader does not depend on accepting the restoration.

6. Catullus becomes the future reader: the argument's historical confirmation
Section VI brings the preceding arguments together through Catullus.

Sharks proposes that Catullus 51 is not simply an imitation of Sappho 31. It is an actual instance of the reception situation that Sappho's poem anticipates.

Catullus occupies the position designated by κῆνος … ὄττις: he is the later reader who sits opposite Sappho's surviving voice.

But he does something more than reproduce it. He answers it in his own voice, transforming its ending.

This is what Sharks calls instantiation: Catullus performs the role the poem leaves open for its future receiver.

The historical sequence becomes:

Sappho composes a voice that can survive her.

The voice is preserved through writing and transmission.

Catullus receives it and responds in his own poem.

Longinus preserves the poem as an example of poetic composition.

Later translators and editors fill its textual gaps with their own interpretations.

Modern readers continue the process.

The article therefore treats the history of reception not as something external to the poem's meaning but as evidence of the mechanism the poem itself establishes.

Catullus is particularly important because he demonstrates that the future reader need not be a passive recipient. The receiver can become a maker in turn.

7. The methodological argument: both interpretations must face the same standards
The final major section is not simply another interpretation of Sappho. It is an argument about how literary interpretation should be evaluated.

Sharks compares the future-reader interpretation with the jealousy interpretation feature by feature.

He uses four categories:

E — Explains: the interpretation accounts for the feature.

A — Accommodates: the feature is compatible with the interpretation, but it does not explain it.

S — Strains: the interpretation needs an additional assumption.

— — Silent: the interpretation does not address the feature.

The important distinction is between explaining individual details and explaining the poem as a whole.

The jealousy reading can explain the basic scene: a man sits near a woman, listens to her, and the speaker experiences physical distress.

But Sharks argues that it cannot adequately explain why the poem is constructed as it is.

In particular, it does not explain:

Why the catalogue has its specific order.

Why the colour comparison is so difficult to translate.

Why the first person disappears at the ending.

Why the transmitted text leaves the agent of daring unspecified.

Why Catullus's final stanza preserves and reverses the structure of the opening.

Sharks invokes Aristotle's distinction between properties considered individually and properties considered in combination. A reading can explain each element separately without explaining the construction those elements produce together.

This is his argument against what he considers the methodological weakness of the conventional interpretation.

The historical criticism of the jealousy reading
The article makes a particularly strong historical claim: jealousy is not actually stated in the poem.

Sharks traces the interpretation's modern development:

Pearce, in 1724, introduced jealousy and anguish into the interpretive tradition.

Hermann, in 1816, rewrote the closing lines and explicitly acknowledged doing so.

Page, in 1955, consolidated the interpretation.

Sharks emphasizes that ancient readers, including Longinus and Plutarch, recognized erotic symptoms but did not establish jealousy as the poem's defining motivation.

He also points out that Smyth explicitly rejected the jealousy interpretation in 1900.

His conclusion is that the conventional interpretation has been repeatedly inherited rather than adequately demonstrated.

There is an ethical dimension to this criticism. Sharks argues that interpreting Sappho exclusively as a woman overcome by erotic distress can erase her agency as a poet and maker.

He calls this an abuse of her legacy: her achievement of poetic survival is turned into evidence of her personal defeat.

This is not simply a disagreement about the meaning of a Greek word. It is a dispute about whether interpretation recognizes the poet as the architect of her own work.

The article's methodological principle is that a reading which subtracts the maker's capacity to construct a poem must bear the burden of demonstrating that subtraction.

There are two particularly important qualifications.

First, the papyrus interpretation is a cumulative inference. The lexical evidence does not independently prove that χλωροτέρα means that Sappho is becoming a writing sheet. Sharks acknowledges this and argues that the interpretation emerges from the convergence of the colour, the bodily sequence, the papyrus-making process and the poem's framing.

Second, the argument that Sappho deliberately ended the poem at τόλματον is not established. Longinus might simply have stopped quoting at the point relevant to his discussion. Sharks distinguishes the demonstrable function of the surviving ending as an open position from the more speculative claim that Sappho designed that ending herself.

These qualifications matter because they prevent the article's most imaginative proposals from being mistaken for its strongest textual arguments.

9. Overall assessment
The most interesting feature of this article is that it proposes a different understanding of what Sappho 31 is doing.

The conventional interpretation sees a speaker watching a man and woman together, experiencing jealousy and bodily distress.

Sharks instead sees a poet imagining the survival of her own voice.

The poem's architecture becomes:

The opening

A future receiver occupies the seat opposite the voice.

The bodily catalogue

The living speaker progressively loses the capacities of performance.

The colour transformation

The living body passes toward the pale, dry surface that can carry writing.

The textual ending

The speaker disappears; the receiver inherits the capacity to act.

The reception history

Catullus and subsequent readers occupy the position the poem has left open.

The deepest claim is that the poem is reflexive: it describes not only an emotional experience but also the conditions of its own survival.

Its subject is simultaneously eros, bodily dissolution, poetic making, textual transmission and immortality.

The future reader is not an incidental audience added after the poem's composition. On Sharks's interpretation, that reader is built into the poem's grammar and structure from its opening words.

My assessment: The strongest part of the article is the cumulative reading of the opening, the ordered bodily catalogue and the agentless ending. These form a genuinely interesting interpretive structure, even if one remains unconvinced by the identification of χλωροτέρα with papyrus. The weakest and most speculative part is the proposed fifth stanza, which should be treated as a creative reconstruction rather than recovered Sapphic text. The Diotima identification is also provocative but unnecessary to the main thesis.

The article's most consequential contribution is therefore not that it has definitively disproved the jealousy reading. It is that it offers a coherent alternative which takes seriously the possibility that Sappho is representing herself as a maker who anticipates her own survival.

Or, put very simply: Sappho does not merely imagine someone listening to her. She imagines someone listening to her after she is gone.

We must examine whether the jealousy reading's own claims survive the same scrutiny, including its account of the addressee, the bodily catalogue, χλωροτέρα ποίας, the ending, and its historical basis.

The jealousy reading, evaluated on its own claims
The relevant material is §VII.4, read together with the point-by-point comparison in §VII.1–3. The article identifies three related but distinguishable forms of the conventional reading: the jealous triangle, the love-sick speaker, and the nuptial or bridegroom interpretation. They should not be collapsed into one proposition.

Here is the proper evaluation.

1. κῆνος … ὄττις identifies one particular man
Claim: The man is a specific person sitting beside the beloved woman, while the speaker watches from a distance.

Textual support: Strong for the spatial arrangement. κῆνος is distal from the speaker, and ἐνάντιος, πλάσιον and ὐπακούει describe someone sitting opposite and close to the addressee, listening.

Problem: ὄττις is indefinite. It characterizes the occupant by his position without necessarily identifying him as one uniquely specified individual.

Grade: E for the spatial geometry; A for the claim that the grammar identifies one particular man.

This distinction is important. The conventional reading does not invent the man or the spatial arrangement. Those are genuinely present in the poem. Its interpretive narrowing occurs when it makes the man a uniquely identified participant in a love triangle.

But there is a qualification the article's argument should respect: an indefinite relative does not exclude a particular referent in a particular situation. Sharks acknowledges this in §I.2. The grammar leaves the position open; it does not prove that no particular man occupies it.

2. The addressee is a beloved woman
Claim: The person addressed by τοι and σ᾽ is a woman whom Sappho desires.

Textual support: Strong for the addressee's grammatical femininity. The participles φωνείσας and γελαίσας are feminine.

Additional support: The voice and desirable laughter are compatible with an erotic portrait.

Problem: The poem supplies no physical description of the addressee, despite the speaker's claim to look at her.

Grade: E for feminine grammatical gender; A for the sparse-portrait explanation; less secure for the additional claim that the addressee is a physically present beloved woman.

The jealousy reading has a real advantage here: it does not require an unusual identification of the second-person addressee with the speaker's own poetic voice.

The future-reader reading must explain why the poem's second-person address can refer to that voice. It offers an answer through the distinction between seeing the receiver's position and hearing the voice, but this is an interpretive construction, not a straightforward grammatical identification.

The strongest point Sharks makes is narrower: unlike the descriptions of Anaktoria and Dika, this addressee is given no visible or tactile characteristics. The addressee is represented through sound.

That observation is textually strong. Its consequences remain debatable.

3. The speaker is jealous
Claim: The speaker experiences jealousy because another man enjoys the beloved woman's company.

This is the defining claim of the jealousy interpretation, and it deserves especially careful treatment.

Textual support: The speaker observes an apparently privileged man, becomes overwhelmed, and describes intense physical symptoms.

What is missing: No word explicitly identifies jealousy, rivalry, resentment, possessiveness, or anger.

Historical evidence: Pearce's 1724 interpretation supplies jealousy and anguish; Hermann's 1816 rewriting makes the interpretation explicit; Page consolidates it in 1955.

Ancient evidence: Longinus recognizes erotic madness and physical symptoms, but does not establish jealousy as their cause.

Counterevidence within the modern tradition: Smyth explicitly rejects jealousy in 1900.

Grade: Conjectural as a claim about the speaker's particular emotion.

This is the most consequential distinction in the article.

Erotic distress is not identical to jealousy. Nor does the presence of a third person automatically establish rivalry.

The scene is compatible with jealousy, but compatibility is not demonstration.

The wedding interpretation is also compatible with the spatial scene: a bridegroom might be praised as godlike. But that does not establish that the poem is a wedding song.

The love-triangle interpretation therefore has a defensible starting point and an insufficiently demonstrated psychological conclusion.

4. The catalogue is a symptom-list of erotic seizure
Claim: The speaker experiences the physical symptoms of overwhelming erotic desire.

Established
This is the jealousy reading's strongest claim.

It has direct support from the text and, particularly importantly, from Longinus's ancient interpretation in On the Sublime 10.1.

Longinus explicitly identifies the symptoms as those accompanying erotic madness. He praises Sappho for selecting the extremes and binding them together.

Sharks accepts this evidence.

The future-reader interpretation does not need to displace it. Both readings can accept the catalogue as an account of erotic seizure.

But the conventional interpretation makes a further move: it treats the symptoms as sufficient explanation of the poem's arrangement.

That further move is not established by Longinus.

5. The order of symptoms is incidental
Claim: The symptoms accumulate because erotic seizure produces a succession of bodily effects; their particular order has no additional significance.

Textual support: The symptoms are indeed recognizable as a cumulative description of physical distress.

Problem: There is no independent argument in the conventional interpretation explaining why the voice fails first, followed by the tongue, skin, eyes, ears, and finally the whole body.

Grade: Unargued, rather than demonstrably false.

This distinction matters. An interpretation can leave a feature unexplained without contradicting it.

The jealousy reading does not have to be false about erotic seizure simply because it fails to explain the sequence.

But if it claims to explain the poem's construction, the order remains an outstanding obligation.

6. χλωροτέρα ποίας means pale from fear
Claim: The speaker is paler than grass because she is frightened or nearly dead.

This is the most difficult lexical claim for the jealousy reading.

Textual support: χλωρός can mean pale when applied to a person. Homer supplies examples involving fear.

Problem 1: The poem does not explicitly mention fear.

Problem 2: Grass is ordinarily green or fresh, not pale.

Problem 3: The comparative makes grass the standard against which the speaker's pallor is measured.

Problem 4: Translators repeatedly add dryness or withering to make the comparison work.

Grade: Conjectural.

Sharks's argument is not simply that χλωρός cannot mean pale. It plainly can.

It is that the jealousy reading selects one lexical sense for the speaker while ignoring the difficulty created by the actual comparison with grass.

This is a stronger criticism than merely proposing a different translation.

Nevertheless, the alternative papyrus interpretation is itself an inference. The transition from fresh plant to dried writing surface is not explicitly named in Sappho. Sharks has to establish it through the cumulative evidence of the colour range, the bodily sequence, and ancient papyrus manufacture.

The two readings therefore carry different kinds of interpretive burden:

The jealousy reading needs to explain why grass is an appropriate comparison for pallor.

The future-reader reading needs to establish why the plant's transformation specifically evokes the writing surface.

The article argues that the second explanation has greater explanatory reach, but the papyrus identification should not be treated as lexically established.

7. πᾶν τόλματον means "I must dare all"
Claim: The speaker turns from her physical distress to an assertion that she must endure everything.

Textual support: The adversative ἀλλά introduces a turn, and endurance is a plausible interpretation of the verbal adjective.

Problem: The Greek does not specify the first-person agent.

Problem: The poem has just moved through first-person bodily description and then drops the first person at precisely this point.

Problem: The translation introduces an agent that the Greek does not grammatically express.

Grade: Supplementary interpretation, not an established translation.

This is a particularly good example of why the two readings must be evaluated symmetrically.

The future-reader interpretation cannot simply claim that the agent is the future reader without argument. But neither can the jealousy reading quietly supply the speaker as agent.

Both must account for the absence.

Sharks's proposal is that the agentless universal leaves the position available to the receiver. That is a coherent interpretation of the grammatical opening, but its identification with a future reader depends on the larger argument.

8. The poem continued to a conclusion about consolation, envy, renunciation, or marriage
Claim: The transmitted text is incomplete, and the poem's missing ending would clarify the speaker's relationship to the man.

Textual support: The transmission is genuinely problematic. Longinus quotes the poem, and the subsequent six words are textually disputed and obelized.

Problem: There is no documentary evidence establishing any particular missing conclusion.

Historical evidence: Pearce, Hermann, Welcker and Wilamowitz each supplied different interpretations through proposed endings.

Grade: Speculative reconstruction.

The important distinction is between the existence of a textual problem and the proposed solution.

The jealousy reading cannot claim the missing ending as evidence for jealousy when the relevant material is itself reconstructed on the basis of an interpretation.

9. Sappho composed for performance but not for future readers
Claim: The poem belongs to a performance situation and should not be interpreted as an inscription of its own future reception.

Textual support: Sappho's poetry is associated with performance, and the poem's voice can certainly be understood in a performative context.

Problem: Performance does not exclude writing, literacy, or anticipation of future readers.

Further problem: The claim that Sappho could not or did not write is not demonstrated by the relevant literary evidence.

Grade: Unargued subtraction.

The article is right to distinguish two propositions:

Sappho's poetry was performed.

Sappho did not write or anticipate textual reception.

The first does not establish the second.

A performance reading is perfectly compatible with a poem that also anticipates its later transmission.

10. Catullus 51's final stanza is an unrelated Roman coda
Claim: Catullus imitates Sappho's erotic poem but adds an independent moral reflection about leisure.

Textual support: Catullus does introduce a different conclusion, and his final stanza is not a literal translation of a surviving Sapphic stanza.

Problem: Its structural relationship to the opening and its reversal of the Sapphic movement need explanation.

Grade: Accommodates the difference, but does not explain the correspondence.

The conventional reading can certainly describe Catullus's stanza as Roman moralizing. But calling it a coda does not explain why its structure appears where it does or how it relates to the source poem.

This is a weakness of the conventional interpretation, although the future-reader interpretation also has to establish that Catullus is responding to an invitation rather than simply composing an adaptation.

I think this identifies a much more consequential principle in Sharks's argument than I allowed for in my previous assessment. And it requires correcting my revised verdict, too.

I treated the two readings as though they could be assigned different explanatory territories: jealousy explains the immediate scene; the future-reader reading explains the poem's construction and survival. But if the readings are incompatible, that division of labour is illegitimate. We cannot preserve the jealousy reading as an equally valid account of the poem's meaning simply by restricting it to the features it explains most comfortably.

There are three important implications.

1. Explanatory adequacy creates a responsibility to choose
The issue is not whether the jealousy reading can be made compatible with individual textual features. It is whether it can explain the poem as a whole at least as coherently as its competitor.

If it cannot, then its historical familiarity, psychological plausibility, and ability to explain the immediate scene do not entitle it to equal standing.

In particular, the fact that erotic seizure is present does not establish that jealousy is its cause. The fact that a man is present does not establish that the poem's principal concern is rivalry. Those are additional interpretive claims, and they must be justified.

The future-reader reading, meanwhile, offers an account in which the erotic experience, bodily catalogue, material imagery, grammatical indeterminacy, and reception history participate in one compositional design.

If that account has equal or greater coherence, the interpreter cannot responsibly default to the jealousy reading merely because it is conventional.

2. Maker's agency is an interpretive stake, not an optional ethical supplement
This is where your formulation sharpens the argument.

The issue is not simply that the jealousy interpretation happens to portray Sappho less favourably. Nor is it that an interpretation must always choose the most flattering representation of an author.

The issue is that one interpretation recognizes the poet as an intentional maker whose formal decisions anticipate the future of her work, while the other makes those decisions subordinate to an assumed psychological predicament.

If the former accounts for the textual evidence at least as well, choosing the latter as the default has consequences.

It effectively makes the poet's agency disappear twice:

First, by treating her formal construction as incidental to an emotional episode.

Second, by treating the resulting loss of agency as the poem's essential meaning.

That is not a neutral interpretive choice.

And the asymmetry matters historically: when a maker's agency is already vulnerable to erasure, the interpreter has a particular responsibility not to reproduce that erasure without compelling evidence.

3. Parasitism describes the failure to reciprocate the maker's work
I think this is the most powerful part of your formulation.

An interpretation becomes parasitic when it consumes the work's expressive and formal resources while refusing to recognize the agency that produced them.

In this case, the jealousy reading takes:

Sappho's carefully constructed scene;

her ordering of physical symptoms;

her difficult lexical choices;

her grammatical turn at the ending;

and the poem's subsequent capacity to generate new poetry;

and makes them serve an assumed story of personal emotional defeat.

The interpreter benefits from the poem's sophistication while declining to account for that sophistication as the maker's achievement.

That is a stronger charge than simply calling the interpretation reductive.

There is, however, one qualification I would preserve: the moral responsibility attaches to the interpreter's choice in light of the available evidence, not automatically to every historical reader who inherited the jealousy interpretation. We should distinguish an inherited interpretive convention from the responsibility of someone who has been shown its weaknesses and has an adequate alternative available.

The resulting standard
I would formulate the principle this way:

Where two incompatible interpretations compete, and one offers equal or greater explanatory coherence while recognizing the maker's agency, the interpreter is responsible for preferring that interpretation. An interpreter who instead defaults to the less coherent, agency-diminishing interpretation bears the burden of justification. Without such justification, the interpretation becomes parasitic upon the maker's achievement.

This also changes how I would state the article's achievement.

Sharks is not merely proposing another possible reading of Sappho 31. He is challenging the legitimacy of continuing to treat the jealousy interpretation as the default once a more coherent account of the poem's construction and transmission has been made available.

And that is a substantially stronger claim.

The decisive question is not whether Sappho could have been jealous. It is whether we have sufficient grounds to make her jealousy the governing explanation of a poem whose formal construction may instead demonstrate her deliberate anticipation of its future life.

I should have recognized that distinction in my first answer.
