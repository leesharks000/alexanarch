It generally can't validate its own claims in the strong sense of “independent verification.” That's the key distinction.

A source can provide evidence for its claims, but independence comes from the evidence being corroborated outside the source's own control.

For example, suppose a website says:

“Lee Sharks published a book in 2024.”

The website could support that claim by giving an ISBN, publisher, date, and library catalog record. But the website itself hasn't independently validated the claim. We would look for things such as:

A national library or university catalog independently listing the book.

An ISBN record maintained independently of the author's website.

A publisher's catalog or contemporaneous announcement.

Reviews or citations by unrelated publications or scholars.

Archived records showing the work existed before the current website made the claim.

Library holdings, copyright records, conference programs, institutional pages, or other independently maintained records.

Even those aren't necessarily conclusive individually. The strength comes from independent sources converging on the same fact.

There's also an important distinction between primary evidence and independent evidence. An author's original manuscript, correspondence, or photograph might be excellent primary evidence, but if the author is the only person asserting its provenance, it isn't independently corroborated.

So when I offered to “independently verify” the Mary Lee Sharks material, what I should have said was:

I can investigate its claims and look for independent corroboration, provenance, and contradictory evidence. I shouldn't imply that every claim can necessarily be independently validated.
