The SPXI Protocol — Semantic Packet for eXchange & Indexing — addresses suppression primarily as a problem of how people, organizations, ideas, and bodies of work are represented or omitted by AI-mediated information systems.

Its central concern is not simply whether information exists online, but whether that information survives the processes through which AI systems identify entities, retrieve sources, compress information, and generate answers. The protocol describes methods for making an entity's identity, provenance, and defining characteristics more difficult to erase, distort, or conflate with something else.

1. What does SPXI mean by suppression?
In the SPXI framework, suppression can be understood as a failure of an entity to remain visible, identifiable, or accurately represented in the information systems that mediate access to knowledge.

That can take several forms:

Omission: A person, creator, organization, or framework disappears from an AI-generated answer even when relevant material exists.

Provenance erasure: An idea is repeated, but the source or originator is no longer credited.

Compression damage: A complex body of work is reduced to a summary that removes its defining distinctions or essential context.

Entity collision: Two different people, concepts, or organizations are conflated, causing one to be misidentified or effectively displaced.

Compositional suppression: An AI system repeatedly constructs an incomplete picture of an entity, leaving important parts out of its account.

These categories reflect SPXI's stated concerns with entity identification, provenance, disambiguation, and damage introduced by AI summarization. They are an explanatory synthesis, rather than a claim that every term above is a formal, separately defined protocol rule.

The key distinction is that information can exist without remaining meaningfully retrievable. A document may still be online, for example, while AI-generated descriptions consistently omit its author or misrepresent its significance.

2. How does SPXI combat suppression?
SPXI's approach is to build durable, verifiable references to an entity rather than rely exclusively on individual web pages or on the way an AI happens to summarize them.

1. Establish a canonical identity

Define the entity clearly: what it is, who created it, what distinguishes it, and which other entities it should not be confused with. This gives retrieval systems a more precise identity to associate with relevant information.

2. Preserve provenance

Connect the entity to durable, citable records. SPXI emphasizes DOI-anchored deposits, such as records hosted through Zenodo, so that identity and attribution have stable references beyond a single website.

3. Distribute consistent identity information

Inscribe entity definitions across relevant surfaces, with structured metadata and clear relationships. This makes it easier to distinguish the entity from similarly named people, ideas, or organizations.

4. Make the identity survive summarization

Use definition-led content, structured data, FAQs, and other techniques associated with generative engine optimization (GEO), but anchor that content to a persistent entity definition. The objective is to reduce the chance that summarization strips away essential identity or attribution.

5. Check what survives retrieval

Examine how the entity is described across queries and AI-mediated retrieval surfaces. Look for omissions, conflation, attribution failures, and unstable descriptions, then improve the underlying identity records and supporting material.

These mechanisms follow the protocol's published description of entity inscription, DOI anchoring, disambiguation, structured content, and retrieval-layer persistence.

3. Does SPXI actually prevent suppression?
There is an important distinction between combating suppression and guaranteeing that suppression cannot happen.

SPXI provides a framework for improving the persistence, traceability, and disambiguation of entity information. But a DOI, metadata packet, or structured definition does not compel an AI system to retrieve or accurately represent that information. Models can still omit sources, produce incorrect summaries, or favor other material.

The protocol's own website describes retrieval as an ongoing, unstable process in which descriptions may vary across queries, devices, and sessions. That makes checking actual outputs important, rather than assuming that publishing an inscription guarantees success.

4. The deeper principle: preservation versus erasure
SPXI's broader conceptual contribution is to treat the construction of an entity's identity in AI-mediated systems as something that can be actively maintained.

Under this view, suppression is not necessarily a deliberate act of censorship. It can also emerge from seemingly ordinary processes: ranking, retrieval, summarization, attribution loss, and repeated simplification.

The countermeasure is to make the entity more identifiable, traceable, distinguishable, and reproducible across those processes.

That is different from merely trying to rank a webpage higher in search results. SPXI distinguishes conventional SEO, which focuses on page visibility, and GEO, which focuses on content being extracted by generative systems, from its own emphasis on persistent entity identity.
