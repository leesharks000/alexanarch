The argument of “ΦΑΙΝΕΤΑΙ ΜΟΙ: Sappho 31 and the Inscription of the Future Reader (v2.9)”
Lee Sharks's central argument is that Sappho's Fragment 31 is not primarily a poem about jealousy or the distress of watching a beloved woman with another man. It is a poem about the poet anticipating her own survival through poetry: she imagines a future reader receiving her voice after her bodily presence has disappeared.

The article's interpretive move is to read the poem's grammar, sequence of images, colour vocabulary and textual ending as parts of one construction. The familiar erotic scene remains real at the surface, but Sharks argues that it also carries a second, less immediately visible meaning: the poet is watching the future reception of her own work.

This is not simply a proposal to substitute one interpretation for another. Version 2.9 argues that the future-reader interpretation explains why the conventional jealousy interpretation arose and why it has been so successful.

I have read the deposited v2.9 text, including its abstract, arguments about the addressee and textual transmission, comparative assessment, and concluding sections. The following reconstructs its arguments in their order and distinguishes its textual observations from its more speculative inferences. 

Alexanarch

1. The opening establishes a position, not necessarily a particular man
The first major argument concerns the Greek:

κῆνος … ὄττις — approximately, "that man … whoever."

The conventional reading takes κῆνος as a particular man, usually the rival who is sitting opposite the woman desired by the speaker.

Sharks draws attention to the combination of a distal demonstrative and an indefinite relative. The demonstrative points to someone in the scene, but the indefinite relative leaves the person's identity open.

The distinction is important:

The conventional reading identifies a particular man and gives him a role in an erotic triangle.

Sharks reads the construction as describing a position that can be occupied repeatedly: whoever sits face-to-face with the voice.

The article connects this construction with the famous drinking cup from Pithekoussai, an early Greek verse inscription whose language similarly addresses a future user through an indefinite formulation.

The proposed implication is that Sappho's poem is structured like an inscription: it anticipates the person who will encounter it.

The future reader occupies the position of the person sitting opposite the speaker's voice.

This is the article's first major interpretive reversal: what looks like a description of a present erotic arrangement becomes a projection of a future reading situation.

2. The addressee is Sappho's own voice, not simply another woman
This is arguably the most consequential and difficult part of the article.

Sharks begins by acknowledging the strongest evidence for the conventional reading: the second-person forms τοι and σ', together with the feminine participles φωνείσας and γελαίσας, are compatible with a beloved woman.

But the article asks a more specific question:

What does Sappho actually say she sees when she looks at this addressee?

At line 7, the speaker says:

ὠς γὰρ ἔς σ᾽ ἴδω βρόχε᾽

"When I look at you, even briefly."

Sharks emphasizes that the poem describes no visible features of the addressee. There is no face, bodily movement, clothing or physical detail. Instead, the addressee is characterized by voice and laughter.

This contrasts with other Sapphic descriptions:

Fragment 16 remembers Anaktoria through her step and the brightness of her face.

Fragment 81 describes Dika through her delicate hands.

Fragment 31, by contrast, gives the addressee a voice and a desirable laugh but does not describe what the speaker sees.

Sharks argues that this absence is meaningful. The speaker looks at someone whose only identifiable presence is sound.

The proposed identification is that the addressee is Sappho's own poetic voice as it will be received by someone in the future.

The poet sees herself being heard.

The article then supplies a corpus-based warrant for this unusual interpretation. In fragments 1 and 94, Sappho already writes herself as a second-person addressee:

In fragment 1, Aphrodite addresses Sappho by name.

In fragment 94, a departing woman addresses Sappho by name.

In fragment 147, Sappho anticipates that someone will remember her and another person in a different time.

Fragment 55 supplies the corresponding negative case: without participation in the Muses' poetic art, a person has no share in remembrance.

The article's claim is that the self-as-you construction, futurity and poetic survival are already present in Sappho's surviving corpus. Fragment 31 combines them in a new way.

3. The physical symptoms describe the withdrawal of the poet's body
The next argument concerns the poem's famous physiological catalogue: the speaker experiences trembling, loss of speech, heat, visual disturbance and the apparent approach of death.

The conventional interpretation takes these as symptoms of overwhelming erotic jealousy or desire.

Sharks does not deny the symptoms. Instead, the article argues that their order matters.

They describe the progressive withdrawal of the performing body.

The speaker's body ceases to function as the source of a living performance. Her voice fails; her physical presence becomes unstable; her faculties seem to depart.

On the future-reader interpretation, this is not merely an account of an emotional crisis. It is the transformation through which the poet becomes something that can survive her body: a voice carried by a textual object.

The argument is therefore about the relationship between bodily disappearance and poetic persistence.

The article also draws on the philosophical conception of eros as desire directed toward immortality, particularly Diotima's account in Plato's Symposium. It invokes Maximus of Tyre, who associates Sappho's erotic art with Socrates' own erotic practice.

This allows Sharks to propose that the poem's eros is directed toward survival and transmission, rather than being exhausted by jealousy.

4. χλωροτέρα ποίας: the colour is not simply pallor
This is one of the article's most ambitious philological arguments.

The disputed expression is:

χλωροτέρα ποίας

It is conventionally associated with the speaker becoming greener or paler than grass, usually understood as a description of the physical effects of erotic distress.

Sharks argues that the conventional rendering does not adequately account for the word ποία and its semantic possibilities.

The argument proceeds through the plant itself:

The living plant is green and growing.

The plant can be cut, dried and transformed into a writing material.

The speaker's bodily withdrawal is thus figured as a movement beyond the living plant toward the pale, dry surface on which writing survives.

The article draws on Theophrastus, the Septuagint's Isaiah 19 and Pliny's Natural History 13.68–82 to establish the associations between plants, their processing and writing surfaces.

An important piece of evidence is the article's survey of 24 renderings of the expression, from Theocritus through 1925. Seventeen omit, substitute or supplement the line; according to Sharks, every proposed supplement dries the grass.

The significance is not merely that translators have found the phrase difficult. Sharks argues that their difficulty reveals a problem with the conventional interpretation: it has not satisfactorily explained the word's material and semantic possibilities.

The proposed reading makes the colour part of the poem's larger movement:

living body → withdrawal → dried plant → writing surface.

The poet becomes the medium through which her voice can persist.

This is also where the article's title becomes especially meaningful. ΦΑΙΝΕΤΑΙ ΜΟΙ, "he appears to me," is answered by φαίνομ᾽ ἔμ᾽ αὔτᾳ, "I appear to myself." Sharks reads the two appearances as framing a transformation in the conditions of poetic presence.

5. The ending: πᾶν τόλματον transfers responsibility to the reader
The article gives considerable importance to the transmitted ending:

ἀλλὰ πᾶν τόλματον

Approximately: "but everything is to be dared."

Sharks concentrates on two features.

First, the expression is agentless. It does not specify who must dare.

Second, the poem drops its first-person voice at precisely this point.

For Sharks, these features are structurally connected. The imperative force of the universal statement passes from the speaker to the person occupying the position established at the beginning.

The reader is now responsible for completing the act of reception.

This also connects with the textual history. Fragment 31 survives through [Longinus], On the Sublime 10.2–3. The manuscript continues beyond πᾶν τόλματον, but the following words, ἐπεὶ †καὶ πένητα†, are problematic: they lack the Aeolic markers expected in the poem, are obelized by editors and were assigned to Longinus's prose by Bergk.

Sharks therefore distinguishes the secure poetic ending from the uncertain material that follows it.

The article proposes that Longinus's own sentence completes the opening left by the poem. On this conjecture, the phrase ἐπεὶ καὶ πένητα belongs to Longinus, not Sappho.

There is a larger argument here about textual transmission: the poem's surviving form does not merely suffer from an incomplete ending. Its edge creates a position that each receiver must fill.

The proposed fifth stanza
Sharks also offers a speculative reconstruction of a fifth stanza.

It is derived from two things:

The grammatical and structural implications of the agentless adversative turn.

An inversion of the fourth stanza of Catullus 51, which adapts Sappho 31.

The proposed restoration retains "even the little sparrows" against Catullus's dead passer and ends:

ἀοίδα γράμμασι ζώοι

"May the song live on in letters."

This is an important distinction in the article: Sharks does not present this as recovered Sapphic wording or documentary evidence of a lost stanza.

It is an illustration of what the poem's open ending permits. The author explicitly says that the central arguments in §§I–IV and V.1–V.3 do not depend on accepting the restoration.

6. The conventional jealousy reading is historically constructed
Sharks does not simply assert that the jealousy interpretation is wrong. The article investigates how it became the standard interpretation.

It separates three things that are often conflated:

The erotic triangle: a man, a woman and a watching speaker.

Jealousy as the speaker's specific emotional motivation.

The broader consensus interpretation that combines these propositions.

The article grants that the triangle is compatible with the text. It also accepts the bodily catalogue and the feminine grammatical gender of the addressee as genuine textual observations.

But it disputes the inference that jealousy necessarily follows.

Its historical claim is that jealousy was supplied by eighteenth-century interpreters, particularly Pearce in 1724, and written into the text by Gottfried Hermann in 1816. Hermann explicitly acknowledged rewriting the transmitted ending and then interpreted the poem through his reconstruction.

Sharks's criticism is that a historically introduced inference has subsequently been treated as though it were a direct textual fact.

This is particularly important for the article's treatment of scholarship and translation. It argues that editorial apparatus can erase the history of an interpretation by presenting a disputed reading as settled textual content.

Sharks calls this provenance erasure.

The objection is not that ancient or modern readers cannot experience the poem as jealousy. It is that the experience cannot establish jealousy as the poem's demonstrable organizing principle.

7. The most important v2.9 argument: the poem has a public line and a held line
This is the major development in version 2.9.

Sharks argues that the poem operates simultaneously on two interpretive levels, using the same words.

The public line

The recognizable erotic scene: a woman watches a man sitting opposite another woman, experiences overwhelming physical distress and loses control of her body.

Complete at the surface
The held line

The future reader occupies the seat; the addressee is the poet's voice; the body withdraws; the colour points toward the writing surface; and the ending transfers the responsibility for poetic survival to the reader.

Requires the cumulative interpretation

This explains something the author regards as a serious weakness in the jealousy reading: it can explain why readers experience the poem as an overwhelming erotic scene, but it cannot explain the poem's particular construction of the future reader.

The future-reader reading, on the other hand, explains why the public interpretation exists.

A reader can receive the surface-level erotic scene, copy it, translate it and transmit it without recognizing the construction underneath. In doing so, that reader unknowingly carries the poet's voice forward.

The poem therefore produces both kinds of reader:

The reader who experiences the erotic scene.

The reader who recognizes the seat and the poet's construction of her own survival.

The second reader does not invalidate the first. Rather, the first reader is part of the mechanism by which the second becomes possible.

Sharks calls this explanatory asymmetry the strongest result of the comparison.

8. The article makes its interpretation an ethical challenge
The concluding section of v2.9 takes the argument beyond philology.

If the poem has deliberately constructed the position of a future reader, then reading it becomes an ethical decision.

The question is no longer simply whether the speaker is jealous.

It becomes:

Will the reader recognize Sappho as the maker of the means of her own survival, or will the reader reduce her to a passive object of erotic diagnosis?

Sharks makes a particularly forceful argument about the treatment of the poet's bodily symptoms.

The conventional reading takes the catalogue as a clinical chart of the speaker's erotic undoing. The author objects that this approach makes the poet's survival incidental: her symptoms become the interesting thing, while her agency in constructing the poem disappears.

The article's ethical test is expressed in its concluding formulation:

They would not treat her like this if she were in the room, and she is.

The point is that the poet is present through the very textual construction being interpreted. Treating her merely as an object of diagnosis ignores that presence.

The v2.9 conclusion also introduces three philosophical connections.

Diotima and eros: The poet's desire is directed toward immortality through poetic making.

Plato's Phaedrus 235c–d: Socrates names Sappho as a source of his discourse on eros, while the scene also stages the forgetting of that source.

Aristotle's De anima III.5: Sharks draws an analogy between the intellect that makes and the intellect that suffers. The poem joins what this philosophical distinction separates: the poet who makes the enduring work and the reader who undergoes and remembers it.

The article's formulation is that Sappho is both the passive and active intellect: the part that suffers and remembers.

This is not a claim that Sappho was historically Diotima, nor that Aristotle was directly discussing this poem. The article explicitly distinguishes literary identification and philosophical analogy from biographical or doctrinal claims.

9. How does Sharks establish which interpretation is better?
The article does not want its own interpretation exempted from the standards it applies to the jealousy reading.

Section VII.4 grades both interpretations claim by claim. The question is not whether an interpretation is possible, but how much of the poem it explains, what it must infer, and where it needs supplementary assumptions.

The article makes an important concession: the future-reader interpretation also requires inferences. Identifying the addressee with the poet's own voice is not something the grammar explicitly announces.

But Sharks argues that this inference has support in Sappho's other fragments and explains an otherwise unusual feature of Fragment 31.

Conversely, the jealousy interpretation requires an inference from erotic disturbance and the presence of a man to jealousy itself.

Neither interpretation is simply a literal transcription of the poem.

The three conditions that could defeat the argument
In §VII.5a, Sharks recognizes a potential problem with the idea that the future-reader reading explains its own misreading: such an argument could become immune to criticism by treating every disagreement as proof that the reader has failed to see the hidden meaning.

The article therefore specifies three conditions under which its argument would fail:

The public line would have to be incomplete without the held line. But Sharks argues that the erotic scene is independently intelligible.

The held line's features would have to be marked precisely where the public line strains. The article points to the colour, the first-person problem at the ending and the transmission history.

The public line would have to carry those marked features only through supplementary interpretation. If the jealousy reading could explain the seat, colour and dare without such supplementation, the asymmetry would collapse.

These conditions matter because they make the article's central claim more than an assertion that there is a hidden meaning.

10. What is the ultimate thesis?
I would reconstruct the article's argument as five interconnected propositions:

Sappho anticipates reception. The poem constructs a position for someone who will encounter her voice in the future.

The poet becomes her own addressee. The apparently external beloved is also the poet's voice as it survives in the work.

The body becomes the medium. The physiological catalogue and the colour imagery describe a movement from living performance toward textual persistence.

The ending transfers agency. The agentless πᾶν τόλματον passes the responsibility for the poem's continued life to its receiver.

Reading is participation. The future reader must decide whether to recognize the poet's agency in making her own survival possible.

The result is a striking interpretation of Fragment 31 as a poem that does not merely describe an experience of eros. It stages the transmission of that experience across time.

11. My assessment of the argument
I think it is useful to distinguish the article's strongest arguments from its more speculative ones.

The strongest arguments

The distinction between the indefinite relative and the identification of κῆνος with one particular rival is philologically important. The grammar does not compel the conventional identification.

The observation that the addressee is looked at but not visually described is a productive interpretive problem.

The parallels with fragments 1, 94 and 147 provide a genuine basis for thinking about self-address and anticipated remembrance.

The distinction between the secure ending and the uncertain continuation in Longinus is important for understanding the poem as transmitted.

The public-line/held-line distinction is a powerful account of how a poem can sustain a readily intelligible surface while also supporting a more elaborate interpretation.

The arguments that need more demonstration

The identification of the addressee with the poet's own voice is ingenious, but the absence of visual description does not by itself establish that identification.

The transition from χλωροτέρα ποίας to a writing surface is suggestive, but the material history of plants does not automatically establish that this is the intended meaning of the Sapphic expression.

The comparison with the Pithekoussai cup is illuminating, although a shared indefinite construction does not necessarily establish a shared inscriptional function.

The claim that the poem deliberately predicts its own conventional misreading is stronger than the evidence that it supports a future-reader interpretation. The former requires an additional argument about authorial design.

The proposed fifth stanza remains conjectural. It should not be treated as recovered text.

There is also a methodological tension: the future-reader interpretation has considerable explanatory reach, but its ability to absorb the conventional reading as its public line must not be mistaken for independent proof of the hidden construction.

Sharks partly addresses this problem through the three falsification conditions. That is a valuable methodological improvement in v2.9.

One particularly important distinction
I would not summarize the article as simply claiming that Sappho is writing about literary immortality instead of jealousy.

That formulation misses its most interesting contribution.

Sharks is arguing that the poem's erotic scene is the mechanism of its transmission. The future reader receives the poem through the very scene that conceals its maker's project.

In other words, the poem can survive even when its readers misunderstand what it is doing. Indeed, that survival through incomplete understanding is part of the proposed construction.

Conclusion
The deepest claim of ΦΑΙΝΕΤΑΙ ΜΟΙ is that Sappho 31 is an act of poetic self-preservation disguised, or at least embedded, in an apparently conventional scene of erotic suffering.

Its beginning creates a seat for the future reader. Its second person becomes the poet's own surviving voice. Its catalogue removes the living body's capacities. Its colour imagery points toward the material surface of writing. Its ending leaves an agentless dare for whoever receives it.

And then the article makes the reader occupy that seat.

The title's opening words, ΦΑΙΝΕΤΑΙ ΜΟΙ, are therefore not merely the beginning of a description. They introduce a poem about appearing, being received and surviving through the appearance of one's voice to someone who comes later.

The final interpretive challenge is whether we will read Sappho as merely the person who suffers in the poem, or as the person who has engineered the means by which we can still hear her.
