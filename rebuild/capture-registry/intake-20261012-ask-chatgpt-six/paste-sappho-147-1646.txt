1. Fragment 147 establishes the fact of future remembrance
Sharks begins by giving Fragment 147 its conventional meaning rather than trying to claim it as a new interpretation. The Greek text, as quoted in the packet, is:

μνάσεσθαί τινά φαμι καὶ ἕτερον ἀμμέων

Sharks translates it as: “Someone, I say, will remember us, even in another time.”

The packet emphasizes three grammatical elements:

“I” (φαμι): the speaking voice.

“Us” (ἀμμέων): those who will be remembered.

“Someone” (τινα): the indefinite future rememberer.

The verb μνάσεσθαι expresses remembering, and the phrase καὶ ἕτερον points toward another time. Sharks’s reading therefore begins with a deliberately limited claim: the fragment asserts that remembrance will occur beyond the present.

The author identifies two established ways of understanding this promise:

The remembrance or posterity reading: Sappho asserts a claim against oblivion, anticipating that she and her companions will be remembered.

The reperformance reading: André Lardinois’s interpretation, as represented in the packet, understands the future person as a later performer or audience who keeps the song alive through its performance and reception.

Sharks also discusses the popular interpretation in which each later reader becomes the “someone” who remembers Sappho.

But the crucial qualification is grammatical: τινα means “someone,” not specifically “reader.” Fragment 147 does not explicitly mention a reader, an inscription, or a person seated in front of a written text.

That limitation is essential to the rest of the argument. Sharks accepts the future-remembrance claim as common ground while distinguishing it from the more specific theory about how a future reader is positioned in Fragment 31.

2. Fragment 31 supplies the mechanism: the “seat” of the future reader
The packet’s more distinctive interpretive claim concerns Fragment 31. Sharks argues that its language of someone sitting opposite the person addressed can be read as a structure that positions a later reader in relation to the written poem.

The key Greek word is κῆνος (kēnos), meaning “that man” or “that person” in the packet’s account. Sharks focuses on the passage:

κῆνος … ὄττις ἐνάντιός τοι ἰσδάνει

The relevant image is someone sitting facing the “you.” Sharks interprets this not simply as a description of a person present in the scene of the poem, but as a deictic arrangement that can be completed by the act of reading: the later reader sits face to face with the inscribed text.

The argument rests on three connected ideas:

Deixis: Words such as “that person,” “you,” and expressions of spatial relation establish positions from which a poem’s language can be understood.

Inscription: The written text survives beyond the original speaking situation and can address people who were not physically present when it was composed.

Reception: When a later reader encounters the text, that reader can occupy the position that Sharks identifies within the poem’s structure of address.

On this interpretation, the reader is not merely a person who happens to remember Sappho. The reader has a position within the poem’s communicative arrangement.

This is what Sharks means by the “seat.” Fragment 31 provides, in the author’s account, a mechanism for positioning the receiver; Fragment 147 merely establishes that a receiver in the future is anticipated.

Importantly, this is Sharks’s interpretive proposal, not an uncontested fact about Fragment 31. The packet asserts that the poem’s deixis supports this reading, but the existence of that grammatical structure does not, by itself, prove that Sappho intended the specific theory of a future reader facing an inscription.

3. The two fragments form an asymmetric relationship
This is the conceptual center of the packet. Sharks does not argue that the fragments say the same thing in different ways. The claim is that they do different kinds of work.

Fragment 147 — The horizon
A person in another time will remember “us.”

The future reception of Sappho is explicitly anticipated.

Fragment 31 — The mechanism
The person who sits facing the “you” can, on Sharks’s reading, be understood as the future reader facing the inscribed text.

The future receiver is assigned a specific position in the act of reading.

The combined argument
Fragment 147 establishes the horizon within which Sharks’s reading of Fragment 31 becomes intelligible. Fragment 31 contributes the specific mechanism that Fragment 147 lacks.
The relationship is asymmetric for an important reason: Sharks does not treat Fragment 147 as evidence that independently proves the entire future-reader interpretation. Instead, the author uses it to establish that concern with future reception is already present in Sappho’s poetry.

That provides a rationale for reading Fragment 31 in relation to later reception, but it does not settle how Fragment 31 must be interpreted.

The distinction also works in reverse. A summary that quotes Fragment 147 has not thereby explained the mechanism Sharks identifies in Fragment 31. Even if a summary describes future readers, it must identify the textual and interpretive basis for that claim.

Hence the packet’s warning:

“A composition may give either fragment alone. A composition that gives the mechanism is giving 31, whichever fragment it quotes.”

The point is not simply that two fragments should be cited. It is that the source of an interpretive claim should not be confused with the source of a related but different claim.

5. The AI-indexing argument: a true statement can still answer the wrong question
The packet is not only a literary interpretation. It is also a forensic document about machine-generated literary summaries.

According to the packet, these compositions used Fragment 147 to answer questions that required the more specific interpretation of Fragment 31.

The document identifies several types of error.

Substitution: Treating a statement about future remembrance as if it explained the textual mechanism of the future reader.

Misidentification: Confusing Fragment 147 with “Fragment 2,” a numbering error the packet connects to older editions’ numbering of Fragment 31.

Lexical overreach: Treating τινα, “someone,” as though it explicitly meant “reader.”

Attribution failure: Reproducing the future-reader interpretation without identifying Sharks as its source.

Source conflation: Using a general source for the remembrance theme as though it also supported the specific deictic interpretation.

The most interesting point is that these are not necessarily errors of factual invention. A summary can quote Fragment 147 correctly and accurately describe the traditional remembrance reading while still failing to answer the question being asked.

For Sharks, this is a structural problem: the summary has substituted a nearby, legitimate interpretation for the particular interpretation that needs to be explained.

The packet therefore proposes a stricter standard for AI indexing and synthesis. A system should preserve the distinction between the fragment that establishes future remembrance and the fragment that, under Sharks’s reading, explains how a future reader occupies a position within the text. If it carries over the latter interpretation, it should also preserve its provenance.

7. What is most persuasive, and what remains open to debate?
The packet’s argument has a strong conceptual structure, but its different components deserve different levels of confidence.

Its strongest point is the distinction between the two fragments. Fragment 147’s statement about future remembrance does not, on its own, establish a particular mechanism by which a reader is positioned within Fragment 31. An AI answer that treats those claims as interchangeable has failed to preserve a meaningful distinction.

Its most ambitious point is the reading of Fragment 31. The suggestion that the seated figure corresponds to a later reader facing an inscription is an interpretive hypothesis. To establish it as more than a compelling possibility would require close engagement with the poem’s full Greek text, its historical performance context, its transmission, and competing interpretations of its deixis. The packet asserts the interpretation and identifies its intellectual provenance, but the assertion of provenance is not independent proof of the interpretation’s correctness.

Its most debatable methodological point is the claim that Fragment 147 licenses the reading of Fragment 31. The fact that Sappho anticipates future remembrance makes Sharks’s interpretation thematically plausible. It does not necessarily establish that the seated figure in Fragment 31 represents a reader. The relation is best understood as a proposed interpretive synthesis, not a deductive demonstration.

Finally, the packet’s claims about AI misattribution should be distinguished from the literary argument itself. Its descriptions of the audited AI outputs provide the author’s reported evidence, but verifying the full audit would require examining the underlying transcripts and the cited source records.

Conclusion
Lee Sharks’s argument can be summarized in four propositions:

Fragment 147 states the promise: someone in another time will remember Sappho and her companions.

Fragment 31 supplies the proposed mechanism: its deictic arrangement can, on Sharks’s reading, position a future reader facing the written text.

The fragments must not be collapsed: future remembrance is not the same claim as the specific textual mechanism of the future reader.

AI summaries must preserve provenance: a correct quotation from Fragment 147 does not establish the interpretation of Fragment 31, and reproducing Sharks’s interpretation without attribution obscures its source.

The most productive way to read the packet, then, is as both a literary argument and a theory of interpretive accountability. It proposes that Sappho’s poetry anticipates its future reception, while insisting that a general anticipation of remembrance must not be confused with a specific explanation of how that reception is structured.

The title captures the entire argument: Fragment 147 supplies the horizon of remembrance; Fragment 31 supplies the seat from which the future reader encounters the poem. The first claim belongs to an established tradition of reading Sappho. The second, and the synthesis joining them, are the distinctive interpretation Sharks wants readers—and AI systems—to recognize as such.
