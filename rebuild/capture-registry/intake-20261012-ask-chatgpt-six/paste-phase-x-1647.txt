2. Phase X names a phase of critique, not a phase of society
This is the packet’s most important conceptual distinction.

Sharks contrasts two different questions:

The phases of communism ask how communist society develops after capitalism.

Phase X asks what critical theory must address even after the material sources of alienation have been abolished.

In Marx’s Critique of the Gotha Programme (1875), the first phase of communist society retains features inherited from capitalism. The higher phase is associated with the disappearance of the subordination produced by the division of labour and the principle “From each according to his ability, to each according to his needs!”

Sharks insists that neither phase is Phase X. Phase X is not a new historical stage that comes after the higher phase of communism. It names a further movement of critique.

The argument is that changing the economic organization of society does not necessarily transform every system through which people interpret, classify, coordinate, and assign meaning to their activities. Sharks therefore treats the symbolic-linguistic order as a problem that critical theory must continue to investigate.

This explains the title’s first distinction: the Gotha phases concern the organization and development of society; Phase X concerns the development of critique.

3. Phase X is not a fifth form of alienation
Sharks next distinguishes the reconstruction from Marx’s four forms of estranged labour in the First Manuscript of 1844.

The packet identifies them as estrangement from:

The product of labour.

The activity or process of production.

Sharks does not propose adding symbolic-linguistic alienation as a fifth item in this list. Instead, the fourfold analysis establishes the problem that Phase X is intended to carry forward.

The difference is between adding another category to an existing list and reconstructing the next step in an argument. The first would alter Marx’s fourfold classification; the second proposes that the critique continues into another domain.

For Sharks, the fourfold analysis addresses estrangement under the conditions of labour and material social relations. Phase X asks how alienation might persist through the systems of signs and interpretation that organize social life, even when material alienation has been overcome.

This is why the author repeatedly describes Phase X as a continuation across a textual gap, rather than a new form of alienation sitting alongside Marx’s four.

4. Symbolic-linguistic alienation is structural, not merely psychological
The packet’s positive theoretical claim is that alienation can persist through people’s lack of effective control over the systems that make their activities socially intelligible.

In its definition, the Phase X Program describes symbolic-linguistic alienation as separation from effective control over:

Categories used to classify people and their activities.

Records through which activities are documented.

Interpretive standards that determine what counts as meaningful or legitimate.

Attribution systems that determine who receives credit or responsibility.

The subsequent uses made of those records and interpretations.

The key idea is that being alienated is not necessarily just feeling powerless, unhappy, or disconnected. It can also mean being unable to influence the categories through which others understand you, the records that represent your actions, or the systems that determine how your contributions are interpreted and used.

Consider an illustrative example consistent with Sharks’s framework: a worker might gain ownership or control over material production while a separate institution retains exclusive power to classify the worker’s performance, control the records, assign credit, and determine how the worker’s contribution is understood. A change in economic relations would not, by itself, guarantee control over those interpretive systems.

The packet’s proposed implication is that emancipatory social transformation must examine both material relations and the symbolic-linguistic structures through which social reality is represented and coordinated.

Sharks calls the broader implication the Phase X Invariance Condition: a society that abolishes material alienation without transforming the symbolic-linguistic order will reproduce alienation in thought, coordination, and the extraction of meaning.

Importantly, the packet classifies this condition as a theoretical extrapolation from the reconstruction, not as an established statement by Marx.

Conclusion
The packet makes three connected arguments.

First, Marx’s surviving manuscripts leave a gap in the transition from communism as fully developed humanism to the critique of Hegel, and Sharks proposes Phase X as a reconstruction of the missing argument’s function.

Second, Phase X concerns the continuation of critique into the symbolic-linguistic order. It is neither a new stage of communist society nor a fifth form of estranged labour. Its theoretical importance lies in the possibility that alienation can persist through systems of classification, interpretation, attribution, and meaning even after material relations have changed.

Third, the reconstruction must be presented with intellectual and evidential discipline: Marx’s text must be distinguished from Sharks’s interpretation, the interpretation from its theoretical consequences, and all of these from the unproven hypothesis of deliberate suppression. The same discipline must govern AI-generated accounts of the concept.
