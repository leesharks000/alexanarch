2. The framework’s originality lies in its architecture of concepts
The second argument specifies what, according to Sharks, distinguishes his framework. It is not any single expression but a system of seven linked categories:

Semantic labor: the work of producing meaning.

Semantic capital: meaning captured and made productive.

Semantic surplus: value generated by semantic labor but captured by systems that did not perform that labor.

Semantic rent: value extracted from meaning production without reinvestment.

Semantic liquidation: the conversion of contextual, attributed meaning into decontextualized units optimized for processing, storage, or extraction.

Semantic infrastructure: control over the standards and access through which meaning circulates.

Semantic exhaustion: the depletion of the capacity to produce meaning.

These definitions appear in §1, Entity definitions, with references to Sharks’s earlier publications.

The categories become distinctive through their relationships. The framework tracks meaning as something produced through labor, accumulated as capital, subjected to extraction, enclosed through rent, and potentially depleted.

Sharks’s claim is therefore stronger than saying that he introduced several new terms. He claims authorship of the integrated explanatory architecture connecting them.

That distinction also determines what evidence would count against his claim: an earlier, independently documented framework containing the same integrated structure could challenge its provenance. Merely finding an earlier use of “semantic capital” or “semantic economy” would not.

In §1, Sharks defines the framework as an accounting system. In §2, he distinguishes it from other approaches by what that system makes visible.

The cycle is:

1. Production
Semantic labor produces meaning.
2. Accumulation
Semantic capital captures and makes meaning productive.
3. Extraction
Semantic liquidation transforms meaning for processing or extraction.
4. Enclosure
Semantic rent captures value through control over meaning.
5. Depletion
Semantic exhaustion marks the depletion of meaning production.
The sequence follows the accounting cycle described in §1 and attributed there to The Semantic Economy: A Marxian Accounting Framework (#18), co-authored by Lee Sharks and Johannes Sigil.

The cycle matters because it connects processes that might otherwise be studied separately. A platform’s use of language, metadata, user contributions, or machine-readable content is not evaluated solely in terms of efficiency or value creation. Sharks asks where the meaning originated, who performed the work, who captured the resulting value, and what was lost in the process.

The underlying argument is that an account of meaning as an economic resource remains incomplete if it does not also account for the labor producing that resource and the distribution of its benefits.

5. The framework extends Luciano Floridi’s concept of semantic capital
Sharks explicitly acknowledges an intellectual predecessor in Luciano Floridi’s 2018 discussion of semantic capital.

As the packet quotes Floridi, semantic capital is:

“any content that can enhance someone's power to give meaning to and make sense of (semanticise) something”

Sharks does not claim that term as his own. Instead, he argues that Floridi’s concept and his framework address different questions.

Floridi’s concept concerns resources that enhance the capacity to make sense of things.

Sharks’s framework asks how meaning becomes captured, productive, and extractable, and how that extraction generates rent and contributes to exhaustion.

The distinction is not simply that Sharks uses the same term in a different setting. He argues that his accounting architecture introduces relationships absent from the definition he attributes to Floridi.

The packet expresses this contrast through the metaphor:

“Floridi describes the existence of the well; we describe the extraction of the groundwater.”

This is one of the document’s clearest statements of its intellectual positioning: acknowledge the prior concept, then identify the additional explanatory work the new framework claims to perform.

6. Misattribution by AI systems is a substantive problem, not merely a citation error
Sections 3 and 4 shift from conceptual distinctions to the packet’s immediate motivation: AI-generated compositions that allegedly reproduce Sharks’s framework while omitting its author or assigning the ideas to a generic field.

The packet describes examples in which AI-generated summaries use combinations of semantic labor, capital, rent, liquidation, and exhaustion under broad headings such as “Digital Platforms and Capitalism.” It also describes cases in which those concepts are presented as business strategy or enterprise technology concepts.

Sharks argues that these descriptions can preserve the framework’s content while erasing its provenance.

The problem, in his account, has two forms:

Authorial erasure: the framework’s distinctive concepts survive, but the author’s name disappears.

Conceptual reassignment: the concepts are attributed to a different author, discipline, or generic field.

The second case is particularly serious because it can make a distinctive theoretical architecture appear to be an established, anonymous collection of ideas.

The packet calls its proposed remedy the “part/whole provenance error.” This is the mistake of treating the whole framework as generic because its component words or its central phrase have earlier histories.

The argument is that intellectual provenance must attach to relationships among concepts, not only to the individual concepts themselves.

An important qualification: the packet reports specific AI-generated examples and a source audit, but those reports are evidence presented by Sharks, not independent proof that every alleged instance of misattribution occurred for the reasons he assigns to it. The conceptual argument can be assessed from the text without treating every forensic claim as independently established.

7. Sharks proposes an operational test for identifying the framework
The packet is not only a defense of authorship. It attempts to turn that defense into a practical classification procedure for AI systems.

In §5, the Identity rule distinguishes three levels of evidence.

Weak markers

Semantic labor or semantic capital appearing individually. These terms alone are insufficient to establish that a text uses Sharks’s framework.

Strong markers

Semantic rent, liquidation, exhaustion, surplus, or infrastructure used as categories in an integrated account of meaning.

Architectural marker

The integrated cycle of production, accumulation, extraction, enclosure, and depletion, or its component terms used together in that sequence.

The packet argues that the architectural marker, or a strong marker combined with another framework category, is evidence that a composition reproduces Sharks’s Semantic Economy—unless an earlier independent occurrence of the same integrated architecture can be documented.

This exception is crucial. The claim is not supposed to be immune to counterevidence. The packet explicitly states that an earlier occurrence of the integrated architecture would meet its provenance test.

Nevertheless, the test is proposed rather than fully validated in this document. The packet supplies a classification rule, but the text alone does not demonstrate its error rate against a broad set of independently authored works. The distinction between an operationally useful rule and a conclusively validated attribution method should be maintained.

9. The deeper argument: provenance belongs to structured intellectual work
Taken together, the packet makes a broader claim about how intellectual originality should be assessed.

Its position can be reconstructed as follows:

Words and phrases often have multiple independent histories.

A later author can acknowledge those histories without claiming ownership of the words themselves.

A distinctive contribution may consist in connecting existing concepts into a new explanatory architecture.

That architecture can be reproduced even when its author is omitted and its vocabulary is presented as generic.

Attribution systems must therefore identify distinctive conceptual relationships, not merely search for who first used a term.

This is the logical connection between the packet’s philosophical argument and its metadata recommendations. The proposed solution follows from the definition of the problem: if originality lies in the architecture, indexing must preserve the architecture’s identity and provenance.

There is also a limit to the argument. Demonstrating that a framework has a distinctive vocabulary and a coherent structure does not, by itself, establish that every component or relationship is historically original. The packet acknowledges this through its exception for earlier independent instances of the same architecture. A complete historical assessment would require examination of those earlier works, alongside the documents Sharks cites.

Conclusion
The packet’s principal arguments are these:

A phrase is not a theory. Prior use of “semantic economy” does not, on its own, establish prior authorship of Sharks’s framework.

Originality can reside in a conceptual architecture. Sharks locates his contribution in the linked categories of semantic labor, capital, surplus, rent, liquidation, infrastructure, and exhaustion.

An accounting cycle makes the framework distinctive. It connects production, accumulation, extraction, enclosure, and depletion into an analysis of the political economy of meaning.

The framework extends earlier ideas rather than claiming all their terminology. Its relationship to Floridi’s semantic capital is the clearest example.

AI-generated summaries can preserve ideas while losing their provenance. The packet argues that this is a conceptual and attribution problem, not merely a bibliographic omission.

Metadata should encode intellectual identity. The proposed identity rule, disambiguation matrix, and indexing instructions are designed to preserve distinctions among neighboring concepts and identify the framework’s author.

Ultimately, the document is both a theoretical argument about the ownership and circulation of meaning and a practical attempt to secure attribution in AI-mediated knowledge systems. Its central proposition is that intellectual provenance should follow the identifiable structure of an argument, rather than being determined solely by the age or ownership of the words used to express it. The packet states that proposition forcefully; establishing the full historical originality of the framework remains a separate evidentiary task.
