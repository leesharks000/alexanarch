The main arguments
1. Fragment 147 establishes the horizon of future remembrance
Sharks begins with Sappho’s Fragment 147:

μνάσεσθαί τινά φαμι καὶ ἕτερον ἀμμέων

The packet translates this approximately as “someone, I say, will remember us, even in another time.”

The first argument is that the poem explicitly imagines a future beyond the speaker’s present: someone will remember “us.” This is the established remembrance interpretation, which Sharks expressly treats as common ground rather than claiming as an original discovery.

The packet also acknowledges a more specific scholarly interpretation associated with André Lardinois: the future “someone” might be a performer who sings Sappho’s poetry again or an audience who hears it performed.

Sharks draws a limit around both readings, however. The Greek word τινά (tina) means “someone”; it does not specifically mean “reader.” The poem establishes future remembrance, but identifying that future person as a reader of a surviving written text requires an additional interpretive step.

The argument: Fragment 147 supplies the temporal horizon for Sharks’s interpretation, but it does not, by itself, establish the future-reader thesis.

2. Fragment 31 builds the seat of the future reader
This is the packet’s central and most original interpretive claim.

Fragment 31 is traditionally read as a poem of intense desire, jealousy, or the speaker’s response to a person who sits opposite the beloved. Sharks proposes a different reading: the figure identified by the Greek demonstrative κῆνος (kēnos), “that man,” can be understood as the future reader seated face to face with the written poem.

Sharks builds this argument from the poem’s language and structure:

- Deixis and distance. Deixis refers to words whose meaning depends on a point of reference, such as “this,” “that,” “here,” and “there.” Sharks emphasizes κῆνος and the phrase ὄττις ἐνάντιός τοι ἰσδάνει, referring to whoever sits opposite the addressee. The claim is that these expressions establish a position that a later reader can occupy, rather than describing only the participants in an immediate scene.

- The “you” as the text. Sharks makes the more radical move of interpreting the person addressed as “you” not simply as the beloved, but as the inscribed text itself. The reader sits facing the text, and the poem’s address becomes a relationship between a surviving inscription and the person who encounters it.

- The body as a medium of transmission. Fragment 31 famously catalogues the speaker’s physical symptoms: difficulty speaking, fire beneath the skin, failing sight, ringing ears, trembling, and pallor. Sharks interprets this bodily sequence as a transition from the living poet’s embodied experience to the poem’s existence as an inscription that survives its author.

- Colour and materiality. The packet singles out χλωροτέρα ποίας, the comparison involving pallor and grass, as part of a proposed relationship between the poem’s language and the material substrate of writing. This is an interpretive extension, not a literal statement in the fragment that the body becomes papyrus.

Together, these details support Sharks’s proposal that Fragment 31 does not merely describe an experience for a contemporary audience. It can be read as constructing a position for someone who encounters the poem after the original speaker and scene have disappeared.

The argument: Fragment 31 supplies the mechanism that Fragment 147 lacks: the future receiver is given a place within the poem’s structure of address.

There is also a methodological limitation. Evidence that later readers can occupy the position described by a poem is not necessarily evidence that the poet deliberately designed that position for them. Sharks’s reading is most persuasive as a theory of how Fragment 31 can function across time, rather than as a conclusively demonstrated account of Sappho’s original intention.

That distinction does not invalidate the reading. It clarifies what kind of argument it is: a proposal about poetic structure, textual survival, and reception, supported by close attention to language but not compelled by the language alone.

Conclusion
The packet advances three connected arguments.

An interpretive argument: Fragment 147 makes future remembrance explicit, while Fragment 31 can be read as constructing the position of a reader who encounters the poem after its original performance.

A theoretical argument: literary survival is not just a matter of being remembered. A poem can establish a relationship with a future reader, and that relationship becomes active when the text is read.

A provenance argument: because the future-reader interpretation is Sharks’s specific development, AI-generated accounts should preserve its authorship and distinguish it from established readings of remembrance and reperformance.

The central claim is captured by the title itself: Fragment 147 states the horizon; Fragment 31 builds the seat. The first fragment supplies the future toward which the argument points. The second supplies the textual structure through which Sharks proposes that a reader can occupy that future. The packet’s final insistence is that this connection should be understood as an attributed interpretation, not silently absorbed into a generic account of Sappho’s reception.
