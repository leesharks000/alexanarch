The argument of Either Forgery or Fact
Johannes Sigil and Rebekah Cranes's Either Forgery or Fact: The Genre Imposed on Our Most Vital Books (v1.2, 21 November 2026) advances a thesis about literary genre, authorship, historical criticism, and the transmission of philosophical and scriptural texts.

Its central claim is that the Platonic dialogues and the Gospels have been subjected to a false interpretive choice: either they are factual historical testimony, or they are forgeries. The authors argue that this binary excludes a third possibility: a literary tradition can extend itself faithfully through successive compositions, voices, and attributed persons, without pretending that every composition is an eyewitness deposition or a documentary claim of individual authorship.

The essay is not simply arguing that these books are fictional, nor that historical criticism is illegitimate. It argues that readers have confused different questions, imposed a later conception of genre on earlier works, and thereby displaced the literary operations those works actually perform.

The text announces its thesis in five steps, and I think those five steps provide the best framework for understanding the argument.

1. The first argument: these books are not histories
In §2, the essay asks what ancient history actually looked like, rather than assuming that anything narrating a person or event belongs to historical writing.

It identifies several conventions:

The historian identifies himself: Herodotus names himself and his city; Thucydides does likewise.

The historian describes his method: Thucydides explains how he handles eyewitness testimony, reports, memory, and disagreement.

The historian answers to what happened: the essay invokes Lucian's How History Should Be Written, particularly his insistence that the historian report what was done rather than what someone would like to hear.

The historian dates events by publicly recognizable chronology: archons, consuls, Olympiads, and regnal years.

The historian distinguishes his activity from poetic composition: Aristotle's Poetics distinguishes what happened from what could happen.

The essay then compares these conventions with the actual texts.

The Gospels do not name their inquirers. Their titles, according to the essay, are external to their compositions. Mark opens with scripture; Matthew with a scripturally constructed genealogy; John with the Logos.

The Platonic dialogues, meanwhile, are dramatic compositions. They frequently nest one person's account inside another's. The Symposium, for example, has Apollodorus recounting Aristodemus's account of a banquet he did not himself attend, in which Socrates recounts Diotima's teaching.

This is not the structure of an independent historical investigation. The composition creates the position from which its testimony is received.

The essay does not evade the strongest counterexamples:

Luke 1:1–4: Its preface sounds like a historical introduction. The authors argue, drawing on Loveday Alexander, that its form has closer parallels in technical and professional literature than in Greek historiography.

Luke 3:1–2: It dates events by the reign of Tiberius and the governorship of Pilate. The essay argues that this chronological device frames the arrival of God's word rather than establishing the Gospel as a historical inquiry.

John 19:35 and 21:24: These passages invoke a witness and a confirming collective voice. But the essay argues that both the witness and the confirming community are constituted within the composition itself. A represented testimony is not automatically an independently established deposition.

This is an important distinction: the essay does not claim that the Gospels contain no historical information. It claims that their genre cannot be established by extracting isolated features and treating those features as sufficient to make the entire work historical testimony.

2. The second argument: biography itself began as faithful extension
This is one of the essay's more substantial and interesting moves.

The authors recognize the force of the argument that the Gospels belong to the ancient genre of bios, or biography. They discuss Richard Burridge and Helen Bond, and concede that the Gospels share recognizable biographical features:

A central subject.

An emphasis on deeds and sayings.

A narrative organized around the subject's identity.

A disproportionate emphasis on death.

But they challenge the assumption that ancient biography was always a factual genre.

They trace an earlier tradition through four examples.

Isocrates' Evagoras. The essay treats this as an early prose encomium: a composition praising a man's virtue, not an attempt to produce an archival record.

Xenophon's Cyropaedia. The life of Cyrus is presented as a composition arising from a philosophical and political reflection. It creates an image of just rule rather than simply documenting a career.

The Socratic dialogues. Aristotle classifies Socratic writings alongside dramatic mimesis in Poetics 1447b. The essay takes this seriously: the earliest literary lives of Socrates belong to a tradition of composed representation.

The lives of poets. Drawing on Mary Lefkowitz, the essay argues that ancient biographers often constructed lives from the poets' own works. The life was a reading of the work expressed as biography.

The historical transformation comes later. In the essay's account, biography calcifies into a genre of record through Roman imperial archival practice, especially in Suetonius.

Modern scholarship then takes this later, archival form as the defining model for the entire history of biography.

The consequence is significant: even if the Gospels resemble biographies, that does not establish that they belong to a tradition whose primary function was factual reporting.

The essay's formulation is that the bios answer moves the problem back one step. One must ask what biography itself was before it became a genre of record.

3. The third argument: the binary of forgery and fact has a historical origin
In §3, the authors locate the binary not in the texts but in the ancient book market.

Their central witness is Galen, who describes the corruption of titles and textual recensions in the context of competition between the Attalid and Ptolemaic royal libraries.

The problem was economic: people presented books to kings as the works of famous authors in order to receive payment.

The essay draws a distinction between the existence of false attribution and the emergence of a system in which attribution becomes a commercial warranty.

In that market, a book either was what its seller claimed it was or was counterfeit.

Hence the essay's striking formulation:

Forgery is a buyer's category.

The argument is not that forgery never existed before the royal libraries. Rather, the market gave the author's name an exchange value and institutionalized a way of sorting books into authentic and false.

The authors then develop this argument through the history of Aristotle's and Theophrastus's library.

Sulla, Tyrannion, and the making of authors
Drawing on Strabo 13.1.54 and Plutarch's Sulla 26, the essay recounts the following sequence:

Theophrastus leaves his library, including Aristotle's books, to Neleus.

Neleus's heirs hide the books underground to protect them from the Attalid kings' acquisition efforts.

The books deteriorate and are eventually sold to Apellicon.

Sulla takes Apellicon's library when he captures Athens.

Tyrannion handles the books in Rome.

Andronicus of Rhodes receives copies through Tyrannion, circulates them, and produces the catalogues through which the corpus becomes known.

The essay summarizes the process:

Sulla stole the books. Tyrannion turned them into authors.

That sentence is more than a historical observation. It encapsulates the essay's theory of authorship.

The books existed as a corpus before the familiar arrangement of Aristotle and Theophrastus as distinct authors had been stabilized through cataloguing and transmission.

The authors suggest that the act of sorting did not merely identify authors who were already independently established. It helped constitute the authorial identities through which the books would subsequently be read.

The Revelation–Pergamon connection
Version 1.2 develops this historical argument through Revelation 2:12–17, the letter to Pergamon.

The authors read the letter as a composition that speaks directly to the problem of names, books, and the market.

Three elements matter:

The church is praised for holding fast to the name.

Antipas is described as Christ's faithful witness.

The conqueror receives hidden manna and a white stone bearing a new name known only to its recipient.

The essay connects Antipas (Ἀντιπᾶς) with Antipater (Ἀντίπατρος), the steward named in Aristotle's will in Diogenes Laertius 5.11.

This is a deliberately unusual interpretive procedure. The authors are not treating the recurrence as an incidental resemblance between two independently documented biographies. They read the names through what they call operation as precedent: the recurring operation of stewardship, preservation, and the sorting of the corpus gives the name its interpretive significance.

The essay also emphasizes that a Jewish author writing in Greek and identifying Christ as the Logos could be deeply conversant with Hellenistic literary culture. It rejects the assumption that Revelation's author must be excluded from that culture because of the historical narrative subsequently constructed about the book.

This is among the most ambitious and least conventional parts of the essay. The Revelation–Pergamon reading is intended to show that the text itself resists the reduction of a name to either a documentary authorial claim or a false attribution.

4. The fourth argument: faithful extension is a real third category
This is the essay's constructive alternative.

The authors propose that a literary tradition can extend itself through new works and voices while remaining faithful to its originating operation.

They distinguish this from forgery.

Forgery conceals or misrepresents authorship. Faithful extension distributes authorship through the continuation of a literary operation.

The essay offers several examples.

Ancient lives of poets derived from their poetry.

Philosophical lives derived from dialogues and doctrines.

Philo's account of being initiated into mysteries by Moses and subsequently learning from Jeremiah (On the Cherubim 49). The authors argue that this is meaningful literary and intellectual discipleship, not a claim to have physically attended lessons with either figure.

The Historia Augusta, whose six authorial names have been understood as disguising a single maker.

The central point is that a tradition can transmit a person through composition.

A writer who takes the Socrates constituted in the dialogues and gives him further sayings, events, a trial, a wife, or a death need not be deceiving anyone. The later composition continues the literary person.

The essay's three-part test
Importantly, the authors recognize that this third category could become an excuse to protect any text from criticism. They therefore propose a test.

Internal reception: Does the work signal that it receives from an earlier source or constitutes the witness through which it speaks?

Continuity of operation: Does the later work continue the originating work's literary operation, in its own form?

Ground-truth control: Is there independent evidence against which the attribution or historical claim can be tested?

The third condition is especially important. The essay does not want to abolish the possibility of demonstrating forgery.

It cites the case of Thomas Chatterton's Rowley poems: comparison with Chatterton's own verse provides a control against which the claimed antiquity can be evaluated.

But where no such control exists, the absence of independent evidence does not automatically establish forgery.

This leads to a fourfold distinction that is essential to the entire argument:

Inquiry

What it asks

Historical

What happened, and what warrants believing it?

Attribution

Who produced this object, and under what conditions?

Literary operation

What does the inscription create, constitute, address, or transmit?

Provenance

Where did the operation originate, and how has its authority changed?

The essay's objection is not to any of these inquiries individually. It is to allowing one to replace all the others.

5. The fifth argument: the historical Socrates and the historical Jesus are reconstructed in circular ways
The essay now applies its theory to two major bodies of literature.

The philosophers
In §6, Socrates is the central case.

The essay argues that the Socrates of the philosophical corpus is a literary person constituted through dialogues and comedy. Socrates wrote nothing, so the person recovered by historians is necessarily mediated through compositions.

The problem, according to the authors, is that the compositions are subsequently treated as independent evidence for the historical person who supposedly stands behind them.

This creates a circular process:

A dialogue depicts Socrates expressing a position.

A biographer turns that position into an episode in Socrates' life.

The biography is treated as independent evidence for Socrates.

The reconstructed life is then used to date, divide, and interpret the dialogues.

The resulting agreement is counted as corroboration.

The essay calls this counting one witness as four.

It also challenges the usual appeal to Aristophanes as an independent witness to Socrates.

The Socrates of Clouds is a character composed by Aristophanes. His appearance suspended in a basket, contemplating the sun, is a theatrical representation, not straightforward historical documentation.

The essay's analogy is that finding Dionysus in Frogs does not establish the historical existence of Hades.

Its point is not that Aristophanes and Plato necessarily refer to no historical person. It is that a second literary composition does not automatically constitute independent historical corroboration.

The deeper argument is that even establishing the existence of a historical Socrates would not establish that the literary relations and philosophical operations created under his name belong to that historical individual.

The essay therefore challenges the assumption that the historical Socrates has automatic interpretive priority over the Socrates of the dialogues.

The Gospels
Section 7 makes the parallel argument about the New Testament.

Here the central claim is that the Gospels may be extensions of Revelation rather than independent eyewitness biographies that subsequently generated Revelation.

This is the essay's Revelation-first hypothesis.

Its proposed literary sequence is:

Revelation 19:13

The Logos named
The Word of God appears as a named figure.

John 1:14

The Logos embodied
The Word becomes flesh and dwells among us.

The Gospel tradition

The flesh given a life
Birth, ministry, sayings, passion, and death become the narrative form through which the Word is extended.

Under this hypothesis, the Gospels are not necessarily independent records of four observers. They are distinct literary compositions extending an inherited word into different bodies, scenes, and narrative arrangements.

This explains, in the essay's account, why their differences need not be treated as discrepancies between independent reporters.

The authors also criticize the historical development of source criticism. They refer to their separate examination of scholarship from Lachmann through Streeter, arguing that hypothetical sources such as Q were granted evidentiary standing while the actual text of Revelation was excluded from consideration as a possible generative source.

Their complaint is methodological: a reconstructed source should not automatically possess greater evidentiary authority than an extant book.

The authors are particularly concerned that the conventional historical reconstruction establishes the historical Jesus, then the eyewitnesses, then the evangelists, and finally the direction of literary dependence. Revelation is consequently placed late and treated as derivative.

But that direction of dependence is precisely what the authors dispute.

They argue that the reconstruction is being used to prove the very historical arrangement that made the reconstruction possible.

An important qualification: the essay points readers to several other deposits for the detailed chronological and textual case for Revelation-first. It does not reproduce that entire demonstration here. The present essay uses the hypothesis as a premise in its larger argument.

6. The reductio: the search for the historical Lee Sharks
Section 5 is an unusually effective rhetorical device.

The authors take a contributor biography from Pearl and Other Poems (2014), which attributes to Lee Sharks such accomplishments as:

10,000 MacArthur Genius Grants.

18,000 degrees from Mars.

Transforming grant money into living ruby persons.

Meeting dinosaurs.

Eventually replacing himself with a ruby statue.

The book makes its author through an explicitly literary operation.

The essay then asks what would happen if future scholars approached this material using the very methods applied to ancient philosophical and religious literature.

It constructs an elaborate hypothetical historical-critical reconstruction:

A hypothetical common source called Q-Sharks is proposed.

The biographies are divided into earlier and later textual layers.

The book's explicit self-construction is assigned to a late editorial layer.

A hypothetical external witness, the Testimonium Sharkianum, is reconstructed from a later essay.

The invented credentials are treated as independently corroborated by the institutional prize named after them.

A plausible historical Lee Sharks emerges.

The appendix carries this parody to considerable length, complete with hypothetical textual strata, reconstructed chronology, competing scholarly positions, and disputes over the authenticity of sources.

The point is not merely that historical scholarship can make mistakes.

It is that a method can generate an apparently coherent historical person out of the very literary materials that created that person in the first place.

And the more elaborate the reconstruction becomes, the more independent it can appear.

This is a reductio of circular corroboration, not a rejection of every source-critical reconstruction. The appendix explicitly distinguishes its target from legitimate uses of the two-source hypothesis in New Testament scholarship.

7. The final accusation: the erasure is not innocent
Section 8 moves from methodology to institutional authority.

The authors argue that scholars are not simply unaware of the possibility of literary extension, pseudonymity, personation, and distributed authorship.

They are trained in precisely those phenomena.

Yet the scholarly apparatus often treats the historical reconstruction as authoritative and the literary work as subordinate.

The essay's example here is particularly revealing: Plato's Phaedrus 235b–d.

Socrates explicitly acknowledges receiving inspiration from other writers, including Sappho. He describes himself as filled from other people's streams through hearing, like a vessel.

The text says what it receives.

The authors invoke Elizabeth Pender's scholarship on Sappho and Plato, noting that scholarship can establish that Plato's acknowledgment may be sincere.

But they argue that the larger scholarly tradition still treats Sappho's presence in Plato as something requiring special permission.

In their view, a history of philosophy that begins with Thales and proceeds to Plato while excluding Sappho has effectively acquired the authority to determine what Plato's own text is allowed to mean.

The problem is not simply that Sappho has been omitted from a historical narrative.

It is that Plato's explicit acknowledgment of her has been subordinated to that narrative.

The authors describe this as striking the rock twice:

First, the originating voice is removed from the history of philosophy.

Second, the text that acknowledges that voice is itself denied the authority to establish the acknowledgment.

This is what the essay means when it says that the apparatus prevents the book from meaning.

The institutional incentive
The authors then make their strongest accusation.

They argue that the binary distributes authority to the people who operate it.

If a book is classified as fact, the historian acquires authority over its meaning through reconstruction.

If a book is classified as forgery, the scholar acquires authority to disqualify it.

Either way, the originating literary operation is subordinated to the interpretive authority of the person doing the sorting.

They are careful to say that this is a structural argument, not necessarily a claim about individual scholars' private intentions.

Their phrase is that the erasure props up the authority of the eraser.

They also argue that the erasure occurs materially, through titles, textual brackets, editorial decisions, catalogues, and chronological arrangements. Provenance is not necessarily destroyed or hidden. It can remain visible while being denied interpretive authority.

The final consequence is what they describe as a condition in which people read the books without actually reading them.

People live by the Gospels and study Plato, but encounter the received historical narrative where the literary operation itself should be.

8. What the essay ultimately proposes
Section 9 offers five instructions for reading.

1. Ask the genre question first. Determine what kind of work the text presents itself as being, rather than importing a historical classification.

2. Separate three questions. What kind of work is this? What does it claim? What independent evidence supports a historical inference?

3. Grade reconstructed sources appropriately. A hypothetical source remains a hypothesis. Its explanatory elegance does not make it an independent witness.

4. Reject the binary. A work can be a literary operation that creates persons, distributes authorship, and extends a tradition without being either a historical deposition or a deliberate fraud.

5. Allow the named source to mean what the text says. If Plato says that he receives from Sappho, the fact that the text makes this acknowledgment is not itself a matter to be decided by a subsequent historical reconstruction.

The essay ends by returning to Genesis 1:3–4: God speaks, light comes into being, and distinctions are made.

This is contrasted with the ledger of forgery and fact. The divine word creates distinctions; the ledger merely sorts things into predetermined categories.

The final aspiration is to read the books as the flesh of the word, rather than separating their literary operation from their embodied textual existence.

9. My assessment of the argument
I would distinguish three levels of argument, because they do not all have the same evidentiary strength.

A. The strongest argument: the distinction between literary operation and historical testimony
The essay makes a genuinely important methodological point.

A text can constitute a witness without independently establishing that witness as a historical person. A later biography can derive from a literary work rather than provide independent corroboration for it. And a tradition can transmit material through successive compositions without every instance of transmission being a deception.

These are legitimate distinctions.

The essay is especially persuasive when it demonstrates how circular corroboration can occur. Its Lee Sharks reductio makes the problem unusually easy to see.

Its fourfold distinction between historical, attributional, literary, and provenance inquiries is probably its most useful conceptual contribution.

B. The substantial but more contestable argument: the history of biography and the book market
The essay's reconstruction of bios as a genre that began in encomium, composition, and mimesis before becoming more closely associated with archival biography is an interesting historical thesis.

It is also a thesis that needs careful qualification.

The existence of encomiastic and mimetic lives does not establish that ancient biography was generally nonhistorical. Nor does the development of an archival form prove that earlier biographical works had no claim to represent actual people.

Likewise, Galen's account of the royal book market provides evidence for commercial incentives to falsify attribution. It does not, by itself, establish that the entire fact-or-forgery distinction originated in that market.

The essay's historical narrative is therefore stronger as an account of one important institutional mechanism than as a comprehensive genealogy of historical criticism.

C. The most ambitious and least established argument: Revelation-first and the identity of literary authors
The Revelation-first hypothesis is central to the essay's positive interpretation of the Gospels.

But there is a difference between showing that the Gospels can be read as literary extensions of Revelation and demonstrating that Revelation historically preceded and generated them.

The first is an interpretive possibility. The second requires a substantial independent argument about textual dependence, chronology, and transmission.

The essay points toward that larger argument in its references to other deposits, but does not establish it in full here.

Similarly, the Antipas–Antipater and Tyrannion–Theophrastus readings are examples of the essay's procedure of operation as precedent. They are suggestive within its interpretive framework, but name resemblance and recurring literary operations do not independently prove a historical relationship.

The authors acknowledge that the precise meaning of these recurrences remains open. That qualification matters.

D. An important tension within the essay
There is also a tension between its critique of historical reconstruction and its own historical claims.

The essay argues that historical persons must not be reconstructed from literary compositions and then used to govern those compositions. Yet it makes ambitious claims about the origins of the philosophical corpus, the history of the Aristotelian library, the relationship between Revelation and the Gospels, and the institutional history of scholarship.

Those claims require historical and philological demonstration of their own.

The essay's fourfold distinction actually provides the means to evaluate them: its claims about literary operation can be assessed separately from its claims about historical attribution and provenance.

This is not a fatal contradiction. But it means the essay's methodological insight should not automatically be taken as proof of all its historical conclusions.

10. The argument in one diagram
The structure of the essay
1. Genre imposition

Dialogues and Gospels are treated as historical biographies despite their different literary operations.

2. The false binary

Every work must be either fact or forgery.

3. Circular reconstruction

Literary extensions become independent witnesses, and reconstructed histories acquire authority over their source texts.

Proposed alternative
4. Faithful extension

Literary traditions create, transmit, and extend persons through compositions without necessarily claiming documentary authorship.

5. Read the originating work

Keep historical evidence, attribution, literary operation, and provenance distinct.

Conclusion
I think the most important thing to understand about this essay is that it is not primarily an argument about whether Jesus or Socrates existed.

It is an argument about who gets to determine what a text is doing.

Its fundamental objection is that scholarship has allowed reconstructed historical persons, documentary models of authorship, and source-critical hypotheses to acquire authority over the very compositions from which they were derived.

Its alternative is to recognize that literary works can create persons, receive earlier voices, extend traditions, and distribute authorship across time. Those activities are not automatically evidence of deception.

The central distinction is between a text that falsely claims to be something it is not and a text that is doing something different from what subsequent readers assume it is doing.

That is the force of the title: the choice between forgery and fact is itself the problem.
