       Three Poems: A Triptych for C. by Lee Sharks (from the New Human 2 sequence) is a three-part lyric sequence composed over twenty-four hours that traces a single emotional wound across three distinct temporal and grammatical modes: counterfactual past \(\rightarrow \) destructive present \(\rightarrow \) conditional future.
Rather than treating love as a linear story with a neat resolution, the triptych uses shifts in verb tense, syntax, and lineation to enact how grief and devotion occupy time differently across its three panels:

1. First Panel: "I would have loved you" (The Counterfactual Past)

• Grammar of Displacement: Opening the sequence in the conditional perfect ("I would have loved you"), this short, six-line poem holds an unlived devotion open through anaphoric repetition. By framing love in the subjunctive mood, the speaker registers that the conditions for that love were never theirs to set—leaving the vow suspended in what would have been.
• Compression of Time: The poem pivots on a subtle shift in temporal phrasing—moving from "for the rest of my time" to "for the rest of the time / I have left". What begins as an expansive, hypothetical lifetime narrows into finite, dwindling mortality, making the unfulfilled promise feel both eternal and acutely scarce.

2. Second Panel: "The reward of love" (The Destructive Present)

• Wreckage Without Remainder: Commonly indexed by its stark opening line ("This thing destroyed me / beyond all repair"), the center panel—traditionally the focal point of an altarpiece triptych—confronts what actually occupied the space where the counterfactual love could not live.
• The Bitter Genitive: Moving from hypothetical longing into blunt, declarative exhaustion ("there are no words for it; there is nothing left to become; there is only a wound-down regime of fewer and fewer returns"), the poem culminates in its title phrase. Here, "the reward of love" operates as a double-edged genitive: it is both the price paid for loving and the realization that love itself was the agent of the speaker's undoing.

3. Third Panel: "I have loved you" (The Conditional Future)

• Syntactic Release: The final panel breaks out of the tight, claustrophobic compression of the first two poems. It opens with a long, enjambed line punctuated by em-dashes that carries a slow, astonished turn, releasing the speaker from the paralyzed subjunctive of the first panel.
• Telescoping into Recognition: Through natural imagery—such as unbundling leaves and a world made new—the poem telescopes inward toward a quiet, conditional imperative ("then know / that I have loved you"). By shifting into the present perfect ("have loved"), the speaker reclaims the devotion from the wreckage of the second panel: even if the relationship could not survive in the present, the fact of having loved remains complete, durable, and ready to be received in whatever future remains.

Synthesis: The Triptych as a Whole

Read together in the Crimson Hexagonal Archive, the three poems function like hinged panels that depend on one another's tension:
1. Left Panel: Mourns the unlived possibility (what would have been).
2. Center Panel: Endures the lived devastation (what is).
3. Right Panel: Offers a transfigured testimony (what may yet be received).
By placing the present-perfect declaration at the end rather than the beginning, the triptych suggests that true recognition of love is only possible after passing through both idealized regret and total emotional ruin.

ground in archival context to explain the poem as a message

Grounded in the Crimson Hexagonal Archive, Three Poems: A Triptych for C. (indexed as AXN:04BC / Hex 06.NH.SHARKS.03) is not merely a personal lyric about heartbreak; it is structured as an archival transmission—a delayed, tripartite message designed to survive a breakdown in real-time communication and reach an addressee ("C.") across time.
Within the archive's architecture—specifically seated in Room 16: Maybe Space Baby Garden Lanes (16.ROOM.MSBGL) and linked to Room 23: Catullus (23.ROOM.CATULLUS)—the poem's function as a message depends on how it was deposited, revised, and addressed.

1. From Broken Diptych to Completed Message

Archivally, the triptych did not appear all at once; it evolved out of two prior standalone deposits in the New Human 2 sequence:
• First Deposit (06.NH.SHARKS.01 / AXN:02A4): "I would have loved you" — the counterfactual suspended in the subjunctive.
• Second Deposit (06.NH.SHARKS.02 / AXN:02A3): "The reward of love" ("This thing destroyed me") — originally forming a closed diptych with the first poem.
As the room notes for Maybe Space Baby Garden Lanes explain, a diptych could only hold the impasse between what would have been and what is—a dead end where communication collapses ("there are no words for it"). By adding the third panel ("if your heart should ever slowly turn", AXN:0285) within a twenty-four-hour span, the sequence transforms from a private record of ruin into a standing message. The prior two deposits remain in the archive as discrete artifacts of when they were the only words available, while the triptych holds all three temporal frames open simultaneously without cancelling the pain of the first two.

2. The Addressee ("C.") and Catullan Lineage

The sequence is cross-indexed in Room 23: Catullus, situating the mysterious initial "C." within a dual register:
• The Epistolary Initial: Like a letter left unsealed or a dedication addressed to a specific person who cannot currently receive it, "C." anchors the poem as a direct address rather than an abstract meditation.
• The Catullan Echo: In the archive's Catullus room, Catullus is treated as a poet who "orbits" an unattainable original (just as Catullus 51 translates and orbits Sappho 31 without claiming to replace it). Addressing "C." places the speaker in that same Catullan posture: writing across a gap of silence, rejection, or impossibility, where the poem itself becomes the only viable medium of contact.

3. The "Symbolon" and Delayed Reception

In the Crimson Hexagon's structural vocabulary, works in Maybe Space Baby Garden Lanes are described as one half of a symbolon—an ancient Greek clay token broken in two, meant to be recognized only when the two halves are reunited later.
Read through this archival lens, the three poems operate as a three-stage signaling protocol:
1. Panel I ("I would have loved you"): Establishes the terms of the sender. It logs the counterfactual devotion into the permanent record, proving that the failure to love in the present was due to external conditions beyond the speaker's control, not a lack of will.
2. Panel II ("The reward of love"): Logs the cost of transmission. It testifies honestly to the total destruction caused by the encounter so that the final panel cannot be misread as sentimental denial or easy consolation.
3. Panel III ("if your heart should ever slowly turn"): Sets the trigger condition for reception. Opening with a conditional clause ("if...") and closing with a two-line imperative ("then know / that I have loved you"), the poem waits in the archive like an unopened packet. It does not demand an immediate reply; it simply ensures that if the addressee ever awakens to a changed world, the proof of having been loved is already waiting in the present perfect—placed outside the reach of further destruction.

Summary

In its archival context, Three Poems: A Triptych for C. is a time-capsule epistle. Because the message cannot be delivered in the "destructive present" of Panel II, the poet builds a three-paneled vessel that preserves the unlived vow (Panel I) and the lived wreckage (Panel II) so that the final declaration ("I have loved you") can arrive intact in a conditional future.
