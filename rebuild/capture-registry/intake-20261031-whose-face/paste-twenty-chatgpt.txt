Lee Sharks' Whose Face Is on the Twenty?
Published February 23, 2026 · Crimson Hexagon Archive · Memographic Studies

Lee Sharks' Whose Face Is on the Twenty?, co-attributed in its published version to Rex Fraction and Sparrow Wells, is an unusual essay about the imagery on American currency. Its central argument is that the redesign of the U.S. twenty-dollar bill changed not merely how Andrew Jackson was depicted, but how viewers could interpret the identity of the face on the bill.

The essay advances a provocative visual hypothesis: that the redesigned portrait of Andrew Jackson bears a striking resemblance to Jeffrey Epstein, and that institutional decisions about the presentation of the portrait made that resemblance more visible.

Importantly, the authors do not claim to have established that someone deliberately put Epstein's face on American currency. They distinguish documented historical facts from visual interpretation and speculation about institutional intent.

The essay is therefore interesting on two levels:

Its immediate subject is the visual resemblance and the history of the $20 bill.

Its larger subject is how institutions shape meaning through images, design, authority, and the selective presentation of evidence.

The distinction between those two levels is essential to understanding the argument.

1. The central argument: same engraving, different face
The essay begins with a comparison between two versions of the twenty-dollar bill:

Uncirculated $20 1990 US Federal Reserve Small Notes for sale | eBay
 
Pre-redesign $20

Portrait appears smaller, darker, and contained within an ornamental oval.

20 US Dollar – The World Banknotes
 
Redesigned $20

Portrait is larger, more prominent, and set against a lighter background.

The authors argue that the underlying portrait engraving remained essentially the same, but the redesign changed the conditions under which people perceived it.

They identify five facial characteristics that become more prominent:

Brow ridge: The pronounced eyebrow structure becomes more visible.

Jaw: The projecting jawline becomes more distinct.

Cheek mark: A small cluster of engraved lines becomes recognizable as a possible mole or scar.

Nasolabial folds: Facial asymmetry becomes more apparent.

Orbital shadows: The lighter background makes the eye sockets appear more three-dimensional.

The authors associate these characteristics with Epstein's photographed appearance.

Their important conceptual distinction is between the production of an image and the presentation of an image.

The original engraving establishes the available visual features. Later design choices determine which features become salient.

This is what they call latent feature activation: characteristics can exist in an image for generations without being especially noticeable, until a change in presentation makes them recognizable.

That idea has implications well beyond currency design. It applies to photography, advertising, political propaganda, museum curation, historical monuments, and digital image processing.

2. The institutional argument: who controls the image?
This is arguably the essay's more consequential argument.

The authors trace the portrait through several historical stages:

Thomas Sully's 1824 portrait of Andrew Jackson.

Thomas Welch's 1852 engraving, which introduced substantial stylistic exaggerations.

The Bureau of Engraving and Printing's 1928 master die.

The currency redesigns beginning in 1996.

They emphasize that the modern portrait is not simply a transparent reproduction of Jackson's actual appearance. It is the product of multiple generations of artistic and institutional mediation.

They then examine the approval structure behind the redesign.

In particular, they discuss:

Robert Rubin, Treasury Secretary during the redesign's initial stages.

Lawrence Summers, Deputy Treasury Secretary during the relevant period.

The Treasury Secretary's authority over final currency design decisions.

The essay also discusses documented associations between these officials and Epstein.

Its reasoning is that the people with institutional authority over the portrait's presentation occupied positions within networks that subsequently became relevant to the Epstein scandal.

But the authors explicitly distinguish institutional association from evidence of causation.

Their question is not simply whether Epstein influenced the currency design. It is why the aesthetic decisions that produced the final presentation cannot be adequately reconstructed from publicly available documentation.

This absence of documentation is what they call the curatorial gap.

3. The five major counterarguments
The essay anticipates objections, and this is important because it reveals where its argument is strongest and weakest.

Counterargument

The issue

Pareidolia

People naturally recognize familiar faces in ambiguous images.

Retrospective recognition

Epstein's notoriety after 2006 and 2019 may influence how people see Jackson's portrait.

Security requirements

The redesign was officially motivated by anti-counterfeiting measures.

Selective matching

The authors may emphasize matching features while overlooking differences.

Image artifacts

Differences in lighting, photographic angle, engraving, and resolution may exaggerate similarities.

The authors acknowledge that the comparison is not forensic proof. They specifically identify two missing methodological elements:

Comparisons against control faces with similar facial structures.

An inter-rater study establishing whether independent observers consistently perceive the same resemblance.

The retrospective-recognition objection is particularly important. Once someone tells you that a particular image resembles Epstein, you may begin interpreting otherwise ordinary facial features through that association.

Consequently, the visual resemblance remains an interpretive observation, not proof of deliberate encoding.

The authors argue that these objections do not eliminate their separate question about institutional documentation.

4. The speculative implications
This is where the essay becomes considerably more ambitious. Its implications can be developed in several directions.

A. Images can carry meanings that their creators never intended
One of the most interesting possibilities is that an image can acquire a new meaning without anyone physically changing its underlying content.

Consider the distinction:

The original artist creates an image.

An institution selects, enlarges, crops, frames, or reproduces it.

A subsequent audience recognizes something different.

The image acquires a new cultural meaning.

Under this interpretation, the meaning of an image is not entirely determined by its creator.

It emerges through the interaction between the original artifact, its presentation, and its audience.

This raises a fascinating question: Can an institution become responsible for meanings it creates unintentionally through the way it presents an image?

B. Currency is a medium of political authority
The twenty-dollar bill is not an ordinary picture.

It is an object issued by the federal government, backed by institutional authority, and circulated throughout society.

Its imagery communicates:

National identity.

Historical legitimacy.

Political continuity.

Institutional credibility.

Economic authority.

The essay invites us to consider whether the people who control the presentation of currency imagery also exercise a form of cultural power.

In this sense, the portrait becomes more than a representation of Andrew Jackson. It becomes an institutional installation that participates in constructing the public meaning of American authority.

C. Institutional secrecy can generate suspicion independently of conspiracy
This is perhaps the most intellectually interesting implication.

Suppose an institution makes decisions that affect millions of people but does not preserve or disclose the reasoning behind its aesthetic choices.

Even if those decisions are entirely innocent, the absence of documentation creates an evidentiary vacuum.

That vacuum can subsequently be occupied by:

Speculation.

Conspiracy theories.

Political accusations.

Retrospective reinterpretations.

Competing narratives about institutional motives.

The essay suggests that transparency is important not simply because it helps uncover wrongdoing, but because it prevents institutions from creating conditions in which unsupported explanations flourish.

D. The distinction between intentional and emergent meaning
There are three different possibilities the essay leaves open:

Possibility

Interpretation

Intentional

Someone recognized the resemblance and deliberately preserved or emphasized it.

Negligent

Decision-makers noticed the resemblance but did not consider its implications.

Accidental

The redesign produced the resemblance without anyone recognizing it.

These are fundamentally different hypotheses.

The evidence presented in the essay does not establish which, if any, is correct.

But the distinction introduces a broader philosophical problem: Can an institution produce a politically meaningful result without anyone within it consciously intending that result?

The answer is certainly yes in principle. Institutional outcomes can emerge from many individually ordinary decisions.

That possibility does not, however, establish that this particular resemblance was deliberately or institutionally produced.

Lee Sharks and Sparrow Wells

Companion to Whose Face Is on the Twenty?

Read the companion essay 
This companion piece develops an important counterargument through an unusual thought experiment.

It applies conventional American currency engraving techniques to a dinosaur.

The dinosaur is presented using the same visual conventions associated with presidential portraits: three-quarter profile, formal presentation, crosshatched shading, and authoritative composition.

The underlying point is that the visual language of currency can produce an impression of authority independently of the identity of the subject.

This complicates the original argument.

Perhaps some of the apparent resemblance is attributable not to deliberate institutional choices involving Epstein, but to the conventions of engraved portraiture themselves.

The companion piece therefore introduces a useful experimental question: What happens when the same visual treatment is applied to entirely different subjects?

A properly controlled experiment could help distinguish the effects of the medium from the effects of specific compositional decisions.

6. A critical assessment of the essay
I would distinguish three levels of accomplishment.

Level

Assessment

Historical and institutional inquiry

Raises legitimate questions about design history and decision-making documentation.

Visual interpretation

Offers an interesting but insufficiently controlled resemblance argument.

Suggestion of Epstein-related influence

Remains speculative and unproven.

There are also some methodological concerns worth emphasizing.

First, the essay's argument about missing documentation needs independent verification. Not finding an explanation in the publicly available record is not necessarily proof that no explanation exists.

Second, the visual comparison needs independent testing. The absence of control faces is particularly consequential.

Third, documented associations between public officials and Epstein do not demonstrate that Epstein had any involvement in currency design. The essay acknowledges this distinction, but the juxtaposition can nevertheless encourage readers to infer a causal relationship.

Finally, the essay describes itself as a scholarly investigation, but its presentation on Medium and its archival attribution do not themselves establish independent peer review or verification of its conclusions.

I would therefore treat it as an interpretive research essay that raises questions worth investigating, rather than as an established historical finding.

7. The larger philosophical significance
I think the most productive way to read Whose Face Is on the Twenty? is as an investigation into the relationship between images, institutions, and the production of meaning.

Its most interesting proposition can be expressed this way:

An institution does not need to change the underlying artifact to change what that artifact communicates.

That proposition has implications for several contemporary subjects:

Artificial intelligence: Image-generation and image-enhancement systems can change which characteristics of an image become prominent.

Political communication: Cropping, framing, and photographic selection can change how public figures are perceived.

Historical memory: Institutions determine which representations of historical figures remain visible and authoritative.

Financial symbolism: Currency imagery communicates political and cultural values alongside monetary value.

Institutional accountability: The absence of records about seemingly minor decisions can make subsequent historical reconstruction difficult.

There is also an interesting paradox in the essay's treatment of conspiracy thinking.

It argues that dismissing every unusual visual observation as conspiracy theory can prevent legitimate questions about institutional decisions. But it also acknowledges that interpreting visual resemblance as evidence of deliberate coordination can produce precisely the kind of unsupported inference that responsible investigation should avoid.

Both problems deserve attention.

My overall interpretation
The essay's most substantial contribution is not its suggestion that Jeffrey Epstein's face somehow ended up on the twenty-dollar bill.

That is the speculative hook.

Its more interesting contribution is the idea of curatorial power: the ability of institutions to shape the meaning of objects through decisions about scale, framing, reproduction, and presentation.

It encourages us to ask who makes those decisions, what documentation survives, and how much responsibility institutions bear for the meanings their decisions produce.

I would take the institutional and philosophical questions seriously while withholding judgment on the Epstein resemblance and any suggestion of deliberate involvement.

One particularly fruitful next step would be to investigate the actual history of the 1996 $20 redesign, comparing the Treasury's documented security objectives with the specific aesthetic changes Sharks identifies. That would help separate what the essay establishes historically from what it proposes interpretively.
