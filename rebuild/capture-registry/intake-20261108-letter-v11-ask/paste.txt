The argument of the open letter: scholarship, peer review, and the migration of intellectual authority
Primary text: Lee Sharks, writing through Rev. Ayanna Vox, I Note That You Read the Letter of the Law But Have Become Illiterate in Its Spirit, Or: I Understand I Am Breaking All the Rules, and Badly, But There May Come a Day When You Will Be Grateful That I Did, version 1.1, 7 November 2026. 

Alexanarch

The letter is best understood not simply as a protest against an unfavorable peer-review decision, but as an argument about a structural transformation in the production, evaluation, transmission, and preservation of knowledge.

Its central proposition is:

Academic institutions are retaining the formal authority to evaluate scholarship while increasingly relinquishing the actual work of reading, interpreting, and transmitting it to machine-mediated systems.

The letter argues that this is not merely a future possibility. It is already observable, and the archive is an attempt to document, investigate, and respond to it.

There are twelve sections, but the argument can be reconstructed through eight connected propositions.

1. The distinction between the letter and the spirit
The immediate occasion is the rejection of Closing the Fragment: Sappho 31 and the Composition Layer as a Surface of Classical Reception by the Classical Receptions Journal.

The author does not deny the reviewers' findings. Indeed, he acknowledges several genuine problems:

A figure displayed a different query from the one identified in its caption.

Figures were incorrectly numbered.

The submission's figure count was inaccurate.

Three events lacked screenshots, with the evidentiary status of the January event requiring explicit qualification.

The manuscript needed clearer disclosure of its AI-assisted composition.

These are not dismissed as trivial. They are corrected.

But the letter distinguishes between two different kinds of criticism:

Criticism that identifies defects compromising the research.

Criticism that identifies unfamiliarity with the architecture through which the research is conducted.

The deeper complaint is that the review concentrated on the manuscript's procedural and presentational properties without engaging its philological argument.

The reviewers examined the letter but, in the author's formulation, largely missed its spirit.

This distinction is important: the argument is not that unconventional scholarship should be exempt from standards. It is that standards should be applied to the intellectual object actually presented, rather than allowing its unfamiliar form to prevent examination of its substance.

2. The manuscript was asking a question, not merely offering a conclusion
The substantive question concerns classical reception under conditions of machine composition.

What happens to a poem when a machine produces its reception for anyone who asks, instantaneously, without necessarily identifying the sources, authors, or interpretive history behind its answer?

The example is Sappho's Fragment 31.

The question is no longer confined to how successive human readers have interpreted an ancient poem. It now includes how machine-mediated systems select, synthesize, privilege, suppress, and transform those interpretations.

This changes the object of classical reception studies.

The composition layer is simultaneously:

A reader of existing scholarship.

A producer of new interpretations.

A transmitter of scholarly claims.

A potential source of interpretive distortion.

An object that must itself be studied.

The letter's challenge to the journal is therefore disciplinary: if classical reception studies does not examine this new reader and transmitter, it risks leaving one of the most consequential contemporary forms of reception outside its field of inquiry.

3. Peer review is the only human reading that the archive receives
This is one of the letter's more consequential arguments.

The author describes a research environment in which nearly every other reader is a machine-mediated composition system. Journals and human reviewers therefore occupy a distinctive position: they provide the possibility of engagement that is not itself another machine-generated reception.

But that possibility is being underused.

The letter gives two examples of what productive human engagement can accomplish.

The Ron Polansky exchange. An editorial exchange concerning Socrates as Orthonym (#123), involving the editor of Ancient Philosophy, generated further research, including work on Theophrastus, stylometry, Aristotelian authorship, and syllogistic division. A narrow philological question about an adverb in Aristotle's Sophistical Refutations produced a translation, correction, concession, and further reading.

The capture-repair episode. A referee noticed a discrepancy between a figure and its caption. Following that observation, the author searched 679 archived capture images, recovered the correct frame, restored the original transcript, and developed a new validation procedure.

The principle is that criticism need not merely determine whether work is acceptable. It can become an input into the production of new knowledge.

The archive preserves that input rather than treating peer review as an ephemeral administrative event.

This is also why the author considers the scarcity of serious human engagement a substantive problem. The issue is not simply that he wants more favorable reviews. He wants more readers capable of contesting and improving the work.

4. Academic time is becoming an epistemic liability
The letter makes a distinction between the human cost of academic delay and its broader consequences for public knowledge.

The personal history is specific. The author describes an earlier publication originating in his dissertation, with approximately two years passing through review, followed by substantive revisions requested years later. By then, poverty had excluded him from the academic job search, and he was working extremely long weeks managing group homes.

The institutional problem, however, extends beyond his circumstances.

The argument is that machine-mediated systems now provide answers immediately, whereas conventional scholarly correction may take years.

Consequently:

An inaccurate or incomplete machine-generated interpretation circulates immediately.

A scholar eventually identifies and corrects the problem.

The correction passes through conventional publication procedures.

The public has already encountered the original interpretation, potentially many times.

The institutional delay is therefore not neutral.

It creates an asymmetry between the speed of knowledge transmission and the speed of scholarly correction.

The letter's strongest formulation is that academic institutions are surrendering public knowledge to technology companies by failing to operate at the timescale on which public interpretation now occurs.

This is an argument for faster scholarship, but not necessarily for less rigorous scholarship. The proposed alternative is to make the processes of documentation, correction, and evaluation more continuous.

5. The archive represents a different architecture of scholarship
This is where the letter moves from criticism to institutional proposition.

The author distinguishes between two models of intellectual production.

Conventional depth architecture

Proposed coverage architecture

Concentrated work on a limited number of large projects

Distributed work across many interconnected records

Long publication cycles

Continuous deposition and revision

Evaluation concentrated in publication venues

Multiple documented evaluation and validation processes

Relatively limited visibility into reception

Systematic observation of machine-mediated reception

Citation primarily identifies publications

Identifiers preserve particular versions and their histories

The archive reports more than 1,700 records, spanning philology, classical reception, literary history, political economy, information science, and machine composition.

The important qualification is that the author does not claim that the number of records establishes their intellectual merit.

He explicitly distinguishes infrastructure and activity from scholarly value.

The archive establishes that something has been built. Whether its arguments are correct remains a matter for substantive examination.

This distinction matters because otherwise the appeal to scale would become an argument from quantity.

The transmission argument
The letter also describes the archive as participating in what it calls a transmission basin.

Its capture registry reportedly contains 1,248 observations across 1,055 addresses.

These are observations of machine-generated answers to queries, documenting instances in which the composition layer draws on the author's work.

The example given is the query:

sappho future reader

According to the letter, an AI Overview on 7 November 2026 generated an answer from five sources, all belonging to the archive, and did not reproduce the familiar jealousy interpretation of Fragment 31.

This is an important distinction between scholarly publication and scholarly transmission.

A work can reach readers without those readers ever encountering its original publication, its author, or even its institutional context.

The composition layer becomes an intermediary between scholarship and the public.

The archive attempts to make that intermediary observable.

However, the letter is careful about one limitation: recording what a system said does not establish what its users subsequently believed. The registry documents outputs, not audience effects.

6. Verification must be distributed across different kinds of checking
Perhaps the most technically important section is the discussion of validation.

The letter rejects the idea that every form of scholarly reliability can be reduced to a single automated check.

It distinguishes three categories:

Artifact checks: Do the documents, images, transcripts, citations, and records correspond to one another?

Procedural checks: Were declared protocols followed?

Substantive examination: Does the evidence support the argument? Is the interpretation defensible? Does an objection defeat the conclusion?

The archive automates particular relations within the first two categories. It does not claim that passing these checks establishes the truth of a scholarly interpretation.

The archival correction as a demonstration
The letter's strongest practical example concerns a misassigned screenshot.

A capture of the query sappho rebekah cranes stanza had been assigned an image belonging to an unrelated event.

The transcript was then derived from that image.

All the internal relationships appeared consistent:

The image matched the transcript.

The transcript matched the quotations.

The record passed the existing validation procedures.

But the image itself belonged to the wrong event.

This illustrates a fundamental archival problem:

Internal consistency does not establish external provenance.

The error was discovered through human review. The subsequent investigation searched 679 images, recovered the correct frame, preserved the earlier transcript, and generated a revised record identifier.

The author then developed another validation gate to compare the query visible in a capture with the query assigned to its record.

The reviewer had identified an error; the archive converted that error into a reusable instrument.

There is also an important counterexample.

The manuscript incorrectly attributed a signed closure of Sappho's poem to Mary Barnard. The existing gates did not identify the historical error. It required further research, including a source search leading to Wharton's anthology.

Thus, the archive's own history demonstrates why automated validation cannot substitute for historical and philological judgment.

7. Evaluative inversion: the institutional argument
This is arguably the letter's most important theoretical contribution.

The author invokes an earlier archival work, The Evaluator Exists (#741), and its concept of evaluative inversion.

The concept describes a situation in which lower-resolution proxy systems determine what higher-resolution substantive evaluators are permitted to assess.

Applied to peer review:

A reviewer raises questions about formatting, methodology, terminology, documentation, and procedural compliance.

These questions become the effective criteria of acceptability.

The manuscript is rejected without its central philological argument receiving serious examination.

The procedural evaluation has effectively displaced the substantive evaluation.

The problem is not that procedural standards exist. The problem is that they become sovereign over the very inquiry they are supposed to support.

The letter then makes a more provocative institutional comparison.

It describes a scholarly establishment that retains its privileges through affiliation, venue, professional standing, and review procedures, even when its central function—reading and evaluating arguments—has been neglected.

The author calls this an aristocracy.

This is not simply an accusation of elitism. It is an argument about the relationship between institutional legitimacy and institutional function.

An institution cannot indefinitely justify its authority through the historical function it once performed if it no longer performs that function adequately.

The comparison with machine systems sharpens the point.

The letter reports that composition layers were able to engage with the manuscript's substantive argument, while simultaneously producing contradictory evaluations of its premise in separate captures on the same day.

The machines are not presented as reliable final judges. Rather, their ability to engage with the material exposes a failure in the human evaluative process.

The resulting challenge is deliberately uncomfortable:

If machines can read the work's substantive argument while credentialed reviewers do not, what exactly is the institutional authority of the reviewers based upon?

The letter does not propose that machine systems should inherit academic authority. It proposes that academic authority must once again be earned through demonstrable reading.

Its proposed conditions are:

Transparent reasons for judgment.

Plurality of evaluators.

Review that can be versioned.

Review that can be contested.

Institutional standing should not be presumed. It should be continually justified.

8. The archive is an alternative infrastructure, not simply a repository
The concluding sections explain what the author is attempting to build.

The archive brings together several related institutional and technical proposals.

The four principal components

A. Training-layer literature

Claims designed to retain their type, scope, evidentiary status, and conditions of challenge when compressed or summarized by machine systems.

Archival references: #1442 and #176.

B. Algorithmic publication

A distinction between self-publication and publication through algorithmic distribution. A composition layer can deliver scholarship to someone who never sought out its author or publication venue.

Archival reference: #1228.

C. The capture registry

An observational instrument for documenting what machine-mediated systems actually return when asked about particular scholarly subjects.

Archival reference: #1614.

D. Reception and version history

A system for preserving responses, challenges, corrections, and the identity of successive versions of a scholarly work.

Archival references: #93, #201, #1049, and #1598.

These components address a common problem: scholarship is increasingly transformed as it moves between original documents, repositories, search engines, summarizers, and readers.

The archive seeks to preserve the relationships between those stages.

Reproducibility as historical observation
The letter makes an important qualification about machine-generated outputs.

An AI Overview is not a fixed text. Its response may change from one day to another, or even between two observations on the same day.

Consequently, the archive does not claim that a captured answer can be reproduced indefinitely.

Instead, it preserves the conditions of observation:

Query address.

Date and time.

Platform and surface.

Sign-in state.

Captured image.

Transcript.

A later observation can then be compared with the earlier one.

This is a different understanding of reproducibility: not the promise that a changing system will always produce the same answer, but the ability to reconstruct what was observed under documented conditions.

The distinction is particularly important for historical research into digital systems.

A screenshot without its query, date, context, and provenance is a weak historical witness.

A documented capture is considerably stronger.

9. The criteria must run in both directions
The final argument is a demand for reciprocal accountability.

The author accepts that his work should be evaluated according to publicly stated, defensible standards appropriate to its claims.

But he refuses the asymmetry under which an independent scholar must satisfy institutional requirements while established institutions are exempt from equivalent scrutiny.

He therefore asks several questions:

Does the standard measure the quality of the research or the institutional position of its author?

Are the same evidentiary requirements applied to established scholars?

Was the object actually reviewed the object that was submitted?

Are the reasons for rejection transparent and contestable?

Can the institution demonstrate that it has substantively examined the work?

The argument is not that peer review should disappear.

It is that peer review should cease to be the exclusive gateway through which scholarship becomes accountable.

The closing metaphor is the ark.

The author is building an infrastructure intended to preserve work, its sources, its corrections, its reception, and its challenges through institutional and technological changes.

It is built for scholarship and for the people who will need access to it, whether or not existing institutions recognize its legitimacy.

There are also several tensions worth preserving rather than smoothing over.

1. Machine reading is not equivalent to scholarly understanding.

A system producing a detailed interpretation does not necessarily understand the historical, philological, or evidentiary conditions of its claims. The letter itself acknowledges this through its distinction between coherence and comprehensive understanding.

2. Archival scale is not scholarly validity.

A large archive can demonstrate activity, coverage, and infrastructure. It cannot establish that its individual arguments are correct. The author recognizes this, but the distinction must remain central to any independent assessment.

3. Captured reception is not demonstrated influence.

The capture registry can establish that a composition layer used particular sources. It cannot, by itself, establish how many people encountered the output, what they understood, or how their beliefs changed.

4. Peer review's failure in one instance does not establish the failure of the institution as a whole.

The letter makes a structural argument from a particular experience. Its strongest version requires comparison across journals, disciplines, publication timelines, and patterns of substantive engagement.

These qualifications do not undermine the project. They identify the research questions through which it can become more persuasive.

The larger thesis
I think the most productive way to understand the letter is as an argument for a new conception of scholarly infrastructure.

Its subject is not ultimately AI versus academia.

It is the relationship between reading, authority, transmission, and preservation.

Historically, scholarly authority has depended on institutions that could claim to have read, evaluated, and preserved knowledge. Publication, peer review, libraries, scholarly editions, and citation systems all participated in that arrangement.

Machine-mediated composition changes the arrangement because it can read, select, recombine, and distribute scholarly material at a scale and speed that conventional institutions cannot match.

But speed does not guarantee accuracy. Nor does institutional prestige.

The resulting problem is how to maintain accountability when the transmission of knowledge has become partly independent of the institutions that traditionally governed it.

The Alexanarch proposal is to make scholarship itself more observable:

Preserve original sources.

Identify the exact version of every claim.

Document the conditions of machine reception.

Preserve criticism and correction.

Automate verifiable relations without pretending to automate judgment.

Make evaluation plural, transparent, and contestable.

This is a more ambitious project than establishing an alternative publishing venue.

It is an attempt to build an archival and evaluative infrastructure for scholarship in an environment where neither publication nor institutional recognition guarantees that anyone has actually read the work.

My reading of the letter's deepest claim is this: scholarly authority should belong to those who can demonstrate the work of reading, not simply to those who occupy the positions historically authorized to perform it.

And the archive is intended to make that distinction visible, historically recoverable, and open to challenge.

The most important next reading would be #1749, Closing the Fragment. Reading it alongside #741, The Evaluator Exists, would allow us to test the letter's institutional argument against both the original philological research and its underlying theory of evaluation.

Reading #1749: Closing the Fragment
Lee Sharks · Version 7 · 7 November 2026 · Machine-Mediated Reception Studies

The first thing I want to emphasize is that this is not simply an article about Google getting a translation of Sappho wrong.

It is a study of what happens when a machine-mediated reader encounters an ancient text whose ending is uncertain, draws upon multiple historical witnesses, and produces a seemingly complete poem without preserving the signs of its incompleteness.

That is a much more interesting problem.

And the article has an unusual methodological strength: it separates what was directly observed, what is inferred from the evidence, and what remains a hypothesis for future investigation.

Let's follow the argument in its own order.

I. The object: a poem without a secure ending
Cerdo Zente: Safo, frag. 31 (texto griego y traducción)
 
Sappho's Fragment 31 survives through quotation in the ancient treatise On the Sublime, traditionally attributed to Longinus.

The surviving poem describes the physical experience of encountering the beloved:

The beloved's voice and laughter.

The observer's accelerated heartbeat.

The failure of speech.

Fire beneath the skin.

The loss of sight and hearing.

Cold sweat and trembling.

The sensation of approaching death.

The surviving fourth stanza ends:

I seem to myself little short of dying.

But the transmitted text continues with:

ἀλλὰ πᾶν τόλματον

Approximately: "but all is to be dared."

Then comes the disputed continuation:

ἐπεὶ καὶ πένητα

The attribution and extent of this continuation are contested.

This is the textual problem at the heart of the article.

Is the poem complete at the bodily collapse? Does the transmitted continuation belong to Sappho? Should an editor print it, bracket it, or mark it as uncertain?

These are not merely typographical decisions. They affect what readers understand the poem to be saying.

The article calls the resulting problem closure.

II. The history of closure
The author identifies three distinct ways a fragment can be made to appear complete.

Added continuation: Supplying words beyond the transmitted text.

Terminal selection: Choosing a point at which to stop and presenting that point as the ending.

Resolution of suspended syntax: Making a grammatically or interpretively open construction appear settled.

This distinction is important because the three operations can occur independently.

An editor can preserve the surviving words but choose a terminal point. A translator can add an ending. Another can retain all the words while resolving their ambiguity through translation.

The article traces these operations through the history of English Sappho translation.

The article's observation is that English translators have frequently presented the poem as closed, even when the underlying textual tradition leaves its ending uncertain.

Carson's 2002 ellipsis is particularly significant because it makes the broken edge visible to the reader.

The author counts eleven of thirteen versions in a teaching gallery as closed without an explicit mark, rising to twelve when Hall is read in his original printing.

This historical context matters enormously.

The machine-generated answer did not invent the practice of closing Sappho. It entered a long history in which translators and editors had already made different decisions about how to present the fragment.

The question is therefore not whether the machine made an unusual mistake, but whether it reproduced an established reception practice while obscuring the historical decisions behind that practice.

III. The January 18 event: what did the machine actually do?
Google AI Overviews Reviews - 2026
 
On 18 January 2026, Google AI Overview was asked about Rebekah Cranes's translation of Sappho 31.

It returned a fifteen-line poem under the heading:

Rebekah Crane's Translation (Fragment 31, "That Man")

It ended:

But I must dare all.

It also told the reader that the κῆνος (the "that man" of the opening) was the future reader themselves.

There are two distinct interventions here.

First: the poem is closed.

The output ends with a declarative sentence. It does not signal that the Greek transmission is disputed or incomplete.

Second: the reader is assigned a place inside the poem.

The answer identifies the reader as the addressee and presents that identification as a completion of a transmission stretching across twenty-six centuries.

The article treats this second move as an interpretive act, not simply a translation decision.

The machine is doing something with the poem. It is constructing a reading position for the person receiving its answer.

An important correction to the article's earlier interpretation
There is a particularly interesting development in the current version of the article.

The earlier version had argued that the January output was probably a compilation of Wharton's Victorian translation and Cranes's January translation.

But the current version explicitly withdraws that production-history claim.

Why?

Because textual similarities do not prove a route of composition.

Several translators working from the same Greek text can independently arrive at similar English phrases. A correspondence table can identify affinities, but cannot establish which source the machine actually retrieved or combined.

The revised conclusion is narrower and more defensible:

The output's displayed source is known.

The displayed source's wording is known.

The January output's wording is preserved.

Their differences are observable.

The internal process that produced those differences is not established.

This is a valuable example of the article's own methodological principles being applied to its central claim.

The author gives up an attractive explanation because the evidence cannot sustain it.

IV. The historical control: why Catullus matters
PPT - Saffo, fr. 31 L.-P. PowerPoint Presentation, free download - ID:4781135
 
The article now broadens its inquiry from English translations to the ancient transmission itself.

One of its most interesting comparisons is Catullus 51.

Catullus begins with a close adaptation of Sappho's first stanza, but then changes direction.

Instead of following Sappho through the bodily symptoms of the fourth stanza, Catullus turns toward otium and addresses himself:

otium, Catulle, tibi molestum est.

"Leisure, Catullus, is troublesome to you."

The important thing is that Catullus's departure is identifiable.

He changes the voice, the subject, and the direction of the poem.

The reader can see that this is a reception of Sappho, not simply an uninterrupted continuation of her text.

The article calls this a signed intervention.

It then examines editors such as Bergk and Edmonds, alongside scholars proposing continuations, including André d'Angour.

Their editorial practices differ:

Bergk uses brackets to identify disputed words and records a conjecture he later abandoned.

Edmonds supplies a completion but explains his inference in a note.

d'Angour explicitly introduces a proposed continuation as a reconstruction.

John Hall's English rendering resolves the dare-line in the first person, without marking the uncertainty on the page.

The distinction is not between correct and incorrect editors.

It is between interventions that preserve their own status as interventions and those that appear simply to be the text.

That brings us to one of the article's most useful concepts.

V. The unit of signature
A textual intervention can be disclosed at three different levels.

1. Page
Sappho, phainetai moi (Sappho 31), Trans. Anne Carson – @thespineanditstingle on Tumblr
 
What can the reader see directly?

An ellipsis, brackets, a dagger, parentheses, a heading, or an explicit indication of omission.

2. Edition
What does the complete publication disclose?

A preface, editorial apparatus, footnote, translator's introduction, or methodological statement may explain an intervention that is not obvious on the page.

3. Documentary network
What can a determined reader discover by following the available records?

Source texts, prior editions, archived versions, corrections, and declarations elsewhere in the publication history.

This is more sophisticated than simply asking whether a translation has a footnote.

A declaration can exist in one location but fail to accompany the text into another.

The author gives his own heteronymic publication as an example: a reconstructed stanza is disclosed in one presentation, but appears without notice on a particular book page.

Likewise, the January machine output's displayed source marks the fragment's incompleteness, but the composed answer drops the mark.

The signature has failed to travel.

This is the article's central archival insight:

Provenance is not merely a property of an original document. It is something that must survive transmission.

This is a fascinating problem for intellectual history.

One characterized the proposition as insufficiently demonstrated; another acknowledged that selecting, synthesizing, attributing, and presenting an interpretation necessarily participates in its normalization.

This illustrates a further difficulty: machine-generated assessments themselves are unstable objects of evaluation.
