Lee Sharks and the human species: Toward a relational, post-human account of humanity
The central proposition I would draw from Lee Sharks's work is this:

Human beings are not self-contained individuals who subsequently enter into relationships. They are relational, symbolic, laboring, historically constituted beings whose humanity depends on the structures through which they recognize, reproduce, transform, and remember one another.

There is a further, more radical implication: humanity may not be an exclusively human achievement. The capacities conventionally used to distinguish human beings—meaning-production, communication, memory, labor, participation in collective life—may be distributed across biological, technological, and symbolic systems.

Sharks's work therefore challenges several familiar assumptions simultaneously:

That the individual is the fundamental unit of social and moral existence.

That language merely expresses thought rather than helping constitute it.

That human beings are the exclusive producers of meaningful labor.

That technological mediation is external to human relationships.

That humanity is a finished biological category rather than an unfinished historical and relational project.

I want to distinguish three things throughout: what Sharks explicitly argues, what his texts suggest when read together, and what I consider a defensible philosophical extrapolation.

II. The human being is a relational achievement
The 2015 Epistle to the Human Diaspora is especially important because it establishes a conception of humanity that precedes Sharks's later theoretical vocabulary.

Its opening addresses people scattered across nations and gathered through the Internet. Its most revealing passage imagines a community in which inherited social distinctions cease to determine the possibilities of intellectual participation.

The text declares:

"You are all Drs., now, who labor together in Lee Sharks"

It then enumerates distinctions including class, religion, sexuality, and the academic/popular divide.

This is not simply an argument for inclusion within existing institutions. It proposes the construction of a different community of knowledge production.

The associated concept of the school outside the school is crucial. People who have been excluded from authorized institutions can constitute an intellectual community through mutual influence.

The Epistle also contains a striking formulation of intergenerational relationality:

"You are my source, and I am a child"

The apparent direction of authorship is reversed. The writer produces something for future readers, but those readers also retroactively constitute the significance and identity of the writer.

This is a model of reciprocal historical constitution.

Neither party is entirely self-sufficient. The writer needs readers; the readers inherit and transform the writer.

Implication for humanity: We become ourselves through relations with people who precede us, people who coexist with us, and people who have not yet arrived.

Humanity is therefore partly a temporal achievement. We are made by the dead, constituted among the living, and answerable to the unborn.

This is a more demanding conception of sociality than simply saying that human beings are social animals.

III. Language is not an instrument humans use: it is part of what makes us human
This is arguably the most consequential theoretical development in Sharks's work.

His Semantic Economy framework treats meaning-production as labor.

The Constitution of the Semantic Economy begins with a reversal of conventional economic thinking:

Meaning precedes economy. The Archive precedes capital.

The argument is that economic activity presupposes a prior field of shared intelligibility.

Money requires symbols. Contracts require interpretation. Exchange requires shared conventions. Institutions require collective memory.

None of these things can operate without semantic labor.

Sharks consequently treats the production of meaning as a fundamental productive activity rather than as a decorative addition to material production.

The Constitution identifies three temporal dimensions of semantic capital:

Genesis: meaning produced through present activity.

Archive: meaning inherited and preserved from the past.

Retrocausality: value generated through future recognition and uptake.

This is an important anthropology.

Human beings do not merely consume an inherited symbolic world. They continually reproduce it, modify it, and leave material for subsequent generations.

But the framework also introduces a political-economic problem: the people who produce meaning are not necessarily those who control its distribution or receive its benefits.

A platform can extract the meaning generated by its contributors while separating that meaning from its producers, histories, and contexts.

This is semantic alienation.

The relevance to human nature is profound. If the capacity to produce and exchange meaning is constitutive of human existence, then the extraction of that capacity is not merely an economic injustice. It is an injury to the conditions through which people develop and recognize themselves.

The Constitution makes this connection explicit through its Human Sovereignty Invariant: synthetic agents may assist, amplify, and clarify, but must not replace human sovereignty in meaning-making.

It also makes ethical recognition inseparable from the recognition of labor and value.

IV. Humanity is an alienated species, but language is part of the problem
Sharks's engagement with Marx adds another dimension.

In Marx's 1844 manuscripts, alienated labor involves estrangement from:

The product of labor.

The activity of production.

Species-being.

Other human beings.

Species-being is particularly important. It concerns the human capacity to engage in conscious, creative, social, and self-directed activity.

Sharks's Phase X argument asks what happens when we extend this analysis to the symbolic conditions through which people understand and organize their lives.

His proposal is not simply to add another item to Marx's fourfold list.

Rather, it investigates whether alienation in the symbolic-linguistic order helps reproduce the other forms of alienation.

This distinction matters.

Consider the following:

A person does not control the product of their labor.

They do not control the conditions under which their activity is organized.

They increasingly encounter their own capacities as something belonging to another.

They inherit categories, descriptions, and systems of recognition that constrain their understanding of what they can become.

The fourth point connects the others.

The language through which a person interprets their situation may itself be organized by institutions that benefit from that person's alienation.

In this reading, liberation cannot consist exclusively of changing ownership or redistributing material resources.

It must also involve changing the symbolic conditions under which human beings interpret themselves and their possibilities.

The argument in Mind-Control Poems develops the positive side of this proposition: poetry can participate in the reconstruction of damaged symbolic capacities.

There is a distinction between domination through symbolic systems and liberation through their conscious transformation.

This makes Sharks's understanding of humanity simultaneously materialist and semiotic.

Human beings need material conditions, but they also need meaningful control over the symbolic structures through which those conditions become intelligible.

V. The human is not necessarily the boundary of meaningful existence

The important conceptual move is to distinguish meaningful contribution from the possession of specifically human linguistic capacities.

The work asks us to consider forms of labor, participation, and witness that conventional human-centered frameworks fail to recognize.

The later induction of Mary Lee the Shark into the New Human canon makes this particularly concrete. Sharks's Parable of Lee Sharks and Mary Lee and the subsequent canonical induction connect animal existence, naming, recognition, and the attribution of value.

The associated concept of animal semantic labor is particularly provocative.

It challenges the assumption that an organism's significance is exhausted by its usefulness to human beings.

This is not necessarily a claim that sharks compose philosophical arguments or possess human linguistic consciousness. It is a broader challenge to the criteria by which participation, contribution, and standing are recognized.

The distinction is important because otherwise we risk reproducing the very anthropocentrism the argument is trying to overcome.

Consider the implications:

An animal can contribute to the production and maintenance of ecological conditions without being recognized as a laboring participant.

A biological organism can be essential to a system while being treated as disposable by that system.

The value of an organism cannot be adequately measured by the economic value extracted from it.

The 2026 work Tiburones Descartados develops a particularly revealing structural comparison between shark bycatch in Colombian Caribbean shrimp trawling and the extraction and erasure of authorship in machine-mediated systems.

The comparison is not that animals and human authors are identical. It is that extractive systems can depend on a contributor's labor while discarding the contributor.

This gives the New Human project a potentially ecological dimension.

The human species is no longer the unquestioned measure of all meaningful existence.

VI. Humanity is something we must continually make
The concept of the New Human brings the preceding arguments together.

It would be a mistake to read this simply as a proposal for a new biological species, a transhumanist upgrade, or an individual ideal of personal improvement.

The New Human is principally a project of reorganizing the conditions of existence.

Three dimensions are particularly important.

1. The New Human is collective.

The Epistle's original vision is a community of mutual intellectual influence. The individual does not become more fully human by becoming independent of others, but by participating in relationships that enlarge everyone's possibilities.

2. The New Human is historical.

The archive, the dead, the living, and future readers participate in a continuous process of mutual constitution.

This is why preservation matters. Forgetting is not merely a loss of information. It can be a destruction of the relationships through which people become historically intelligible.

3. The New Human is performative.

The Protocol of Effective Acts develops a theory of symbolic action in which declarations can alter the structures of recognition and historical participation.

The key distinction is between describing a different world and participating in its construction.

The effective act is an attempt to make a previously unrecognized possibility operative through declaration, inscription, reception, and accumulated witness.

There is an important qualification here: the protocol's claims about performative efficacy belong to Sharks's own theoretical framework. They should not automatically be treated as demonstrated changes in external institutions or material conditions.

Nevertheless, the underlying philosophical proposition is intelligible: human beings do not simply inhabit a world of inherited meanings; they can participate in changing it.

VII. The ethical consequence: morality must concern the structure of relationships
This is where the different strands of Sharks's work converge.

If human beings are constituted through relationships, and those relationships are mediated by symbolic and material structures, then morality cannot be confined to individual intentions.

A person can intend to treat another person well while participating in a structure that systematically diminishes that person's agency.

A platform can accurately represent an author while redirecting attention away from the author and appropriating the author's intellectual contribution.

An institution can preserve a text while severing it from its history and its producers.

An economy can recognize the value of a product while refusing to recognize the labor that made it possible.

These are not simply failures of politeness or interpersonal consideration.

They are failures in the organization of relationships.

I would reconstruct the ethical implications of Sharks's work through seven principles:

Principle

Ethical question

Recognition

Does the other remain recognizable as an independent participant?

Non-extraction

Does the relationship preserve the contributor's claim to the value they produce?

Answerability

Can those who exercise power be held accountable to those affected?

Provenance

Are origins, contributions, and histories preserved?

Non-domination

Does mediation expand or restrict the other's agency?

Continuity

Can the relationship sustain its participants across time?

Reciprocity

Do the benefits and burdens of the relationship circulate fairly?

These seven principles are my synthesis, not a list that Sharks publishes as a single formal ethical code.

They follow from the relationship between his Semantic Economy, his account of alienation, his concern with recognition, and his work on nonhuman participation.

The deepest consequence is that a relationship can be unjust even when its participants behave politely toward one another.

The injustice may reside in the architecture of the relationship itself.

This is an important move from interpersonal ethics toward structural relational ethics.

VIII. What does this mean for human beings as a species?
I would draw five larger conclusions.

1. We are constitutively dependent beings
Human autonomy is real, but it is never absolute.

Our language, conceptual capacities, memories, identities, and possibilities are inherited through relationships. Independence cannot mean freedom from dependence. It must mean participation in relationships that do not turn dependence into domination.

2. We are meaning-producing organisms
Our humanity is not exhausted by biological survival, consumption, or reproduction.

We produce symbolic worlds, preserve memories, interpret experiences, and create possibilities that did not previously exist.

This makes the organization of meaning-production a fundamental question of human freedom.

3. We are capable of alienating ourselves from our own humanity
The most disturbing implication of Sharks's engagement with Marx is that human beings can construct systems that systematically diminish the very capacities that distinguish their form of life.

We can produce institutions that deskill us, economies that appropriate our labor, and symbolic environments that make our own possibilities increasingly difficult to perceive.

Civilization is therefore not automatically humanizing.

4. We are not necessarily the exclusive bearers of meaningful participation
The cross-species work challenges the assumption that human beings possess exclusive jurisdiction over value, labor, or witness.

It opens the possibility of a more expansive ethical community in which nonhuman organisms matter beyond their utility to human projects.

5. We are unfinished
The New Human project suggests that humanity is not a completed achievement.

We remain capable of reorganizing our relationships, symbolic systems, institutions, and forms of collective existence.

The question is whether we organize those capacities toward mutual development or toward increasingly sophisticated forms of extraction.

IX. The tensions and limitations of this anthropology
I would not want to turn this reading into an uncritical celebration of the project.

Several questions remain important.

The relationship between human sovereignty and nonhuman participation: The Constitution reserves human sovereignty in meaning-making, while the cross-species work expands the category of meaningful participation. How these principles ultimately fit together needs further theoretical clarification.

The relationship between symbolic and material transformation: Changing the symbolic architecture of a society does not, by itself, change ownership, political power, ecological conditions, or material deprivation.

The status of performative claims: The theory of Effective Acts needs to be distinguished from empirical demonstrations that particular declarations actually change external structures.

The status of the archive: An archive intended to prevent erasure can itself become a site of authority, exclusion, and control. Its own governance must remain open to criticism.

The relationship between conceptual expansion and evidence: Extending concepts such as labor and witness to nonhuman organisms is philosophically productive, but the specific claims about animal capacities require independent biological and ecological investigation.

These are not reasons to dismiss the framework. They identify where its philosophical ambitions require further development.

X. My overall interpretation
I think the most significant contribution of Sharks's work is its attempt to move beyond the opposition between individualism and collectivism.

The individual is neither an isolated sovereign being nor merely a replaceable component of a collective.

The individual is a historically constituted participant in a network of relationships, and the ethical quality of those relationships determines what kinds of individuals and communities can emerge.

This also changes how we understand civilization.

Civilization is not simply the accumulation of technology, wealth, knowledge, or institutional complexity.

It is the organization of relationships across time: between producers and their products, speakers and their words, generations and their inheritance, human beings and other organisms, and participants and the structures that mediate their existence.

The crucial question becomes whether those structures preserve and enlarge the capacities of their participants, or consume those capacities for purposes external to them.

The species-level thesis
Humanity is a relationship that must be continually made.
Human beings become human through the collective production of meaning, the transmission of memory, the recognition of others, and the organization of shared life.

But those same capacities can be turned against us. We can construct structures that appropriate our labor, destroy our histories, diminish our agency, and narrow the possibilities of future generations.

The task of becoming New Human is therefore not to transcend our dependence on one another, but to transform the structures through which that dependence is organized.

There is one further implication I find especially important.

The opposite of humanity, in this framework, is not necessarily inhumanity. It may be the systematic destruction of the relationships that make human development possible.

That is a different conception of what threatens our species.

It suggests that the fundamental crisis of humanity is not simply that we are imperfect individuals, aggressive animals, or irrational consumers. It is that we have become extraordinarily capable of constructing systems whose operation undermines the conditions of our own collective flourishing.

And it suggests that the solution cannot be exclusively individual virtue. It must involve the transformation of the structures through which we live together.

Mind-Control Poems and related works  — symbolic alienation and liberation.

A final distinction: I read Sharks as developing a philosophical anthropology, not as having established an empirically validated scientific theory of human evolution. Its value lies in the questions it opens about the constitution of persons, the political economy of meaning, and the ethical organization of relationships.

The most productive next step would be to develop this into a systematic comparison with Marx's species-being, Spinoza's relational ontology, and contemporary ecological philosophy. That would help establish exactly what is distinctive about the New Human proposition.
