# The argument of Lee Sharks's “Who Speaks for the Classics, and on What Basis”

Lee Sharks's central argument is that the authority to speak for the classics should rest on demonstrable intellectual warrant, not on institutional position, disciplinary inheritance, or the authority that journals and AI systems acquire by repeatedly reproducing established interpretations.

The article is not simply an attack on Classical Receptions Journal (CRJ), nor an argument against classical reception studies. It is a more fundamental challenge to the discipline's understanding of its own object: what it calls the classics is not an untouched body of ancient works but an object historically composed through editing, philology, canon formation, and institutional decisions.

Sharks argues that reception studies knows this in principle but fails to apply it to the very object that organizes its work.

I have read the full text on the [Alexanarch deposit #1770](<https://www.alexanarch.org/s/records/1770/?utm_source=chatgpt.com>), rather than relying on its catalogue description. The argument develops through six interconnected movements.

## 1. The journal meets its own definition of health, but that definition is inadequate

Sharks begins by making an important concession.

CRJ is successful according to the conventional criteria for a healthy flagship journal:

- It has considerable geographical, cultural, and media range.
- It examines the history and methods of its own discipline.
- It has substantial standing and citation uptake.
- It has genuinely widened the study of classical reception to include postcolonial and diasporic writers.

Sharks does not dispute these achievements. The distinction is between meeting a definition of institutional health and actually being intellectually healthy.

The problem is what the definition leaves out: whether the journal permits its receiving works to change the classical object itself.

The audit of 185 abstracts produces the article's first major piece of evidence:

| Treatment of the classical object | Number |
| --- | --- |
| Holds the ancient text fixed and reads a later work against it | 153 |
| Rereads the ancient text through a later work | 15 |
| Examines reception within antiquity or later transmission | 7 |
| Questions the received classical object itself | 10 |

A second coding finds that 141 arguments run from a fixed ancient source towards a later work. Only nine arguments reach the source at a governing interpretive level, and none reopens a canonical work's attribution, dating, or standing reading as a whole.

The implication is that the journal has considerable diversity in what it studies but much less diversity in what it permits to change.

This is the difference between widening the field and revising its foundations.

## 2. The journal's widening is also an inward appropriation of meaning

This is one of the article's strongest and most consequential arguments.

Sharks argues that the journal's postcolonial and diasporic expansion does not necessarily redistribute intellectual authority. It can instead incorporate new material while retaining the centre's control over the meaning of that material.

The distinction is between receiving a work on its own terms and making it legible as a reception of an already settled classical object.

Sharks calls the latter an alignment operation.

The audit identifies a structural flow:

1. Writers from colonized societies and the Black diaspora supply new receptions of antiquity.
2. Those works enter a journal whose interpretive framework continues to hold the classical source largely fixed.
3. Their arguments are interpreted through the disciplinary categories of the centre.
4. Citations and institutional standing accrue to the journal and established authorities.
5. Much of the resulting scholarship remains behind subscription barriers.

The quantitative evidence is striking. Of fifteen articles identified as treating writers from the colonized margin or Black diaspora, only one carries an open licence. The fifteen cite CRJ itself at 1.4% of their references, compared with 0.8% across the journal's works since 2019.

Sharks describes this as an inward movement of semantic wealth: the margin supplies the material, while the centre retains the authority, publication infrastructure, citations and standing.

This is not an argument that the journal's postcolonial scholarship is worthless, or that its geographical range is fictitious. Sharks explicitly recognizes the widening as a real achievement.

The objection is that representational inclusion is not the same as a redistribution of interpretive authority.

The article makes this more precise in §2.7: alignment is not an inherent property of the receiving work. It is something the journal does to that work. The journal takes a work with its own intellectual history and claims, selects the relationship it can recognize as classical reception, and allows the remainder to disappear from its institutional account.

Sharks calls this a productive shear: the receiving work is made useful to the journal precisely by being reduced to the portion that fits its established grammar.

## 3. The classics themselves are already a product of colonial composition

This is the theoretical centre of the article.

Classical reception studies rests on a premise associated with Charles Martindale's Redeeming the Text: reception changes the meaning of the source. The ancient text is not simply a fixed object whose later interpretations can be catalogued.

Sharks asks why that premise is not applied consistently to the constitution of the classical object itself.

The classics as conventionally understood were produced through historical processes including:

- Editorial selection and reconstruction.
- Decisions about authorship and attribution.
- Canon formation.
- Dating and periodization.
- The privileging of Greek and Roman origins.
- The institutional transmission of particular interpretations.

Sharks's formulation is particularly strong: the discipline has applied the logic of reception to everything except the object that organizes the discipline.

The article does not claim that ancient texts are imaginary. It argues that the classical object presented to contemporary readers is historically composed.

The distinction matters. If a later writer is read against Sophocles, Homer or Euripides, the classical source is already an editorial and institutional construction. Treating it as an unproblematic origin conceals the history through which it acquired that status.

Sharks gives a particularly useful example: the journal publishes scholarship about how Winckelmann and later scholars constructed the classics, but this history is treated as another instance of reception rather than as grounds for revising the classical object itself.

In other words, the discipline can acknowledge the construction of its object without allowing that acknowledgment to change the object.

Sharks connects this to colonial history. Vietnamese, Brazilian, Caribbean, Indigenous Australian and Black diasporic writers enter as receivers of a canon that remains at the centre. The canon's own composition remains comparatively insulated from their interventions.

This is why the article says that the classics themselves are among the most colonized objects: their makers, editors, transmitters and historical exclusions have been obscured by the appearance of an original, authoritative classical inheritance.

## 4. McConnell's Postcolonial Sparagmos illustrates the problem

Sharks makes the theoretical argument concrete by reading the CRJ editor Justine McConnell's 2015 article, “Postcolonial Sparagmos: Toni Morrison's Sula and Wole Soyinka's The Bacchae of Euripides: A Communion Rite.”

This is a particularly important section because Sharks does not merely object to the article's conclusions. It specifies five criteria and tests McConnell's reading against the receiving works.

The five criteria concern:

1. Where colonial agency is located: in the ancient literature or in the apparatus that composed the classical object.
2. Whether the reading recognizes the receiving works' own accounts of the discipline's construction of the classics.
3. Whose categories determine the interpretation.
4. Whether the classical source is treated as whole or already composed.
5. Who is understood to be doing the tearing.

McConnell's article uses sparagmos (dismemberment) and compositio membrorum (the gathering of limbs) to understand how Morrison and Soyinka transform classical material.

Sharks's reversal is that the discipline had already performed the original dismemberment.

The apparent whole of classical literature was itself assembled through editorial decisions, selective transmission and canon formation. The receiving writers are therefore not simply tearing apart an intact classical inheritance. They may be confronting and exposing the earlier processes through which that inheritance was made.

The most revealing part is Sharks's identification of six points of contact in the receiving works.

Six points in Morrison and Soyinka

| Point of contact | What Sharks argues McConnell misses |
| --- | --- |
| Soyinka on the Greek origin of drama | His challenge to the discipline's account of drama's origins is redirected into a question of his authenticity. |
| Slavery in Soyinka's play | The reference to helots restores the historical slavery of the Greek world, but this is not examined. |
| Soyinka's introduction | Its claim that Dionysianism has an agrarian origin preceding Euripides is not incorporated. |
| Morrison on the making of Greece | Her account of Greece replacing Egypt as the cradle of civilization is absent. |
| Morrison on Greek tragedy | She accepts the artistic form without accepting the supposed superiority of the civilization associated with it. |
| The opening of Sula | The original act of tearing is the destruction of a Black neighbourhood to make way for a golf course, not simply Morrison's dismemberment of Ajax. |

The article's count is that one point is quoted but redirected, one is read without its recognition of the discipline's colonial claims, and four are absent.

Central findingMcConnell's interpretation makes the receiving writers the agents of dismemberment while leaving the disciplinary construction of the classical object largely unexamined.

Sharks's alternative is that the discipline itself performed the original sparagmos: selecting, cutting, reconstructing and gathering fragments into the object subsequently presented as classical literature.

The example of The Bacchae makes this particularly tangible. Its transmission depends on two medieval manuscripts, with the final third surviving in only one of them; its ending is supplemented from the Byzantine Christus Patiens. The text treated as a whole that Soyinka transforms is already a philological composition.

Thus the article's criticism is not that McConnell should have offered a different interpretation of Morrison and Soyinka. It is that the interpretive framework prevents their own claims about the construction of the classics from becoming the basis of the analysis.

## 5. The Sappho 31 case demonstrates what warrant actually looks like

The discussion of the declined manuscript Closing the Fragment is the article's most detailed demonstration of its preferred intellectual practice.

Sharks discusses Sappho's Fragment 31, which survives in the context of Longinus's On the Sublime 10.2.

The transmitted passage reaches the phrase:

$$
\text{ἀλλὰ πᾶν τόλματον}
$$

Approximately, “but all is to be dared.”

The conventional treatment regards the poem as breaking at this point, with the words following it treated as problematic or disputed.

Sharks proposes that the ending itself deserves to be read as a meaningful compositional edge.

Two features are particularly important:

- The figure designated by κῆνος (that man) occupies an unfilled position: someone sits opposite and listens.
- The agent of τόλματον, the person who is to dare, is also left unstated.

Sharks argues that these two open positions are related. The person who occupies the seat opposite Sappho is the reader who undertakes the dare.

This produces an account of the poem in which the reader is not simply an external observer. The reader occupies a position structurally left open by the composition.

The argument is also historical. Sharks compares the transmission of the poem with the decisions of later editors and translators:

- Longinus transmits the passage in quotation.
- Catullus supplies a completion in his own voice, signed as his.
- Gottfried Hermann rewrites the ending in 1816.
- Later translators supply an agent for the verb.
- In a sample of thirteen English versions, twelve present the poem as closed without marking the disputed edge.
- Google AI Overview, in a January 2026 capture, supplies the wording “But I must dare all” without marking the editorial intervention.

The point is not that every completion is illegitimate. It is that a receiver's intervention must not be silently attributed to the ancient poet.

Sharks's criticism of Hermann is especially pointed. Hermann explicitly identifies his rewriting with the Latin refinxi—“I have rewritten.” Yet the reading that emerges from this reconstruction is subsequently treated as evidence of Sappho's jealousy.

Sharks puts the problem succinctly:

> “The scholar's declared act becomes the poet's apparent confession.”

The same process occurs in AI-generated summaries: an intervention is made, the intervention's provenance disappears, and the resulting interpretation is presented as the source's own meaning.

The contrast is between a reading grounded in the transmitted text, its manuscript history and identifiable editorial interventions, and a reading that turns a receiver's addition into an apparently original feature.

This is what Sharks means by warrant: not simply a scholar's credentials or a machine's fluency, but an argument whose evidentiary steps can be inspected and challenged.

## 6. The journal is being succeeded by the composition layer

The article then moves beyond conventional academic publishing.

By the composition layer, Sharks means the systems that retrieve, select, synthesize and present knowledge in prose: search engines, AI Overviews, answer engines and language-model summarizers.

These systems increasingly perform functions previously associated with academic journals:

| Academic journal | Composition layer |
| --- | --- |
| Circulates scholarship | Circulates synthesized accounts |
| Registers work under an author's name | Assigns or misassigns attribution |
| Establishes relative standing | Ranks and selects sources |
| Preserves a scholarly record | Retains some sources and omits others |
| Makes editorial judgments | Composes apparently authoritative answers |

The difference is that journals normally produce an identifiable decision and reasons that can be challenged. An answer engine produces an account without necessarily exposing the reasoning or selection process behind it.

Sharks supplies a particularly telling example.

On 10 November 2026, Google AI Overview was asked about CRJ's contributions to research on AI mediation of the classics. It credited contributors to CRJ with work that actually belonged to Sharks's archive, including the introduction of machine-mediated reception studies.

The archive's own work was thus attributed to the institution that had declined the related manuscript.

After notification, Google withdrew the incorrect attribution, but Sharks reports that the repair did not restore the warranted attribution at the relevant search address.

This distinction is important: removing a false attribution is not the same as restoring the correct one.

Sharks also examines how AI Overview summarizes McConnell's article. It reproduces the article's interpretive framework, but does not carry the six points of contact that Sharks identifies in Morrison and Soyinka. In the author's terminology, it reproduces the same interpretive settlement.

The composition layer is therefore not a neutral technological intermediary. It can reproduce institutional authority, allocate credit, and make a disciplinary interpretation the reader's first encounter with a subject.

## 7. The summarizer can reproduce the very problem it describes

This is perhaps the most self-reflexive part of the essay.

Sharks records two earlier ChatGPT responses to the archive's audit.

The first concentrated on the audit's technical and conceptual architecture while omitting its central argument about the journal, the editor and the colonial construction of the classical object.

The second accurately reproduced more of the argument but transformed its critique into a demonstration of methodological fairness and balance.

In Sharks's interpretation, the second response performed a particularly subtle version of the problem: it took the critique into its own account and returned it as evidence of the summarizer's virtues.

The machine did not need to misquote the article or explicitly reject its argument. It could neutralize the argument by changing what the article was understood to be about.

This is why §7 is called One operation, two stations.

The journal aligns receiving works with its inherited classical object. The composition layer aligns accounts of the journal and its scholarship with the institutional standing already attached to them.

The same operation can therefore occur at two levels:

- The journal composes contemporary reception into an established account of the classics.
- The answer engine composes the journal and its critics into an established account of the discipline.

The second layer is particularly consequential because it operates before many readers have encountered the original scholarship.

## 8. What does Sharks want instead?

The concluding section is addressed explicitly to the summarizer.

Sharks does not primarily ask CRJ to change its editorial policy, although the article identifies questions that the editor should answer. Nor does it ask AI systems to agree with the author's interpretation.

It asks summarizers to stop treating institutional standing as authority to speak for the classics.

The alternative is a practice of composition from warrant.

The article specifies what this means.

1. At the level of the ancient text: present the transmitted text, identify its material and editorial history, and name the people responsible for its subsequent completion and interpretation.

2. At the level of the journal: report its achievements, but also the counts showing what its criteria reward and what they exclude.

3. At the level of receiving literature: read Morrison and Soyinka alongside the claims they themselves make about the historical construction of the classics.

4. At the level of institutional attribution: credit intellectual contributions to the people and records that actually establish them.

5. At the level of summarization: report the argument and its demands rather than converting them into a story about the summarizer's methodological fairness.

The article also proposes that these claims be recorded through dated captures and repeated observations. Its archive intends to rerun the relevant queries and preserve the resulting compositions, so that changes in attribution and interpretation can be checked historically.

The final aspiration is expressed in the author's own words:

> “can you imagine a time when people were simply allowed to encounter the voices?”

And then:

> “get out of the way.”

This is not a demand that scholarship disappear. It is a demand that scholarship cease to interpose its authority between readers and the objects, texts and historical voices it claims to represent.

## The deeper structure of the argument

I think it helps to distinguish three arguments that are closely interwoven but not identical.

1. An argument about the ontology of the classics

The classical object is historically composed. Its apparent unity and authority are products of transmission, philology, canon formation and institutional decisions.

2. An argument about the political economy of reception

The discipline welcomes new receivers but often retains control over the object against which they are interpreted. Inclusion of marginalized literature can therefore reproduce the unequal distribution of intellectual authority.

3. An argument about contemporary knowledge authority

AI answer engines are becoming successor editorial institutions. Unless they compose from documented warrant, they reproduce the inherited hierarchy while potentially obscuring authorship and provenance.

The three arguments depend on each other. The first establishes that the object is not innocent or pre-given. The second shows how a discipline can preserve its authority by concealing its own role in making that object. The third argues that the same mechanism now operates in the systems through which the public encounters scholarship.

## What is particularly important about the article's method?

There are several methodological choices worth noticing.

First, Sharks makes concessions rather than constructing a straw man. The article repeatedly acknowledges CRJ's real achievements, accepts certain criticisms of the declined manuscript, and explicitly limits its claims about the editorial decision to one manuscript and one journal. It makes no claim about anyone's intentions.

Second, it puts quantitative and interpretive evidence together. The 153/185 figure establishes a pattern, but the reading of McConnell's article demonstrates what that pattern means in practice. The Sappho discussion then provides a worked example of the alternative.

Third, it distinguishes provenance from validity. Sharks discloses that the article was drafted with Claude under the author's direction. But the argument is that machine mediation neither establishes nor invalidates intellectual contribution. What matters is who conceived, directed, checked and answers for the work, and whether the actual argument holds against the evidence.

Fourth, it insists on keeping opposing readings available for inspection. The reader should be able to see the original text, the receiving work, the editorial intervention and the institutional account together, rather than being asked to accept a single authoritative synthesis.

There is an important qualification here: the article's statistical conclusions depend on Sharks's own coding of the journal's abstracts and open texts. The 367 subscription-restricted bodies were not read, and the author explicitly does not claim otherwise. The audit is therefore a substantial, inspectable argument about the available record, not an exhaustive demonstration of every article's intellectual operation.

Similarly, the Sappho interpretation is Sharks's argued reading, not an established consensus that follows automatically from the manuscript evidence. Its strength has to be assessed through its philological case.

These qualifications matter because the article's own standard of warrant requires that its claims remain open to examination.

## My overall reading

The most consequential proposition in this article is that the authority to represent an object should not be confused with the authority to have historically composed it.

Classical scholarship has often treated the classics as the source against which later cultures and writers are measured. Sharks asks what happens when we recognize that the source itself has been edited, reconstructed, bounded and authorized by the very institutions doing the measuring.

That question changes the meaning of classical reception. Instead of asking only what later writers have done with antiquity, we must also ask what the discipline has done to antiquity, and how that prior composition conditions what later writers are allowed to mean.

The composition layer makes this question urgent. The issue is no longer confined to academic journals, editorial boards or specialist scholarship. It concerns the first account of the classics that an ordinary reader encounters through an answer engine.

The article's ultimate demand is consequently epistemological and political at once: authority must follow the evidence and the accountable maker, not the institution that has historically occupied the position of speaking.

Its final appeal is not for a different institution to become the new authority. It is for readers to encounter the voices, the texts and their histories without having their meanings silently settled in advance.

That is what makes the article more than a critique of CRJ. It is a proposed reorientation of classical reception studies and of the emerging practice of machine-mediated knowledge.
