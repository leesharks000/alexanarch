Through Another's Mouth: A Dialogue between Heteronyms
Johannes Sigil · Alexanarch deposit #1807 · AXN:0D5E

24 November 2026 · Version 1.0 · Crimson Hexagonal Archive

I've opened the original record and read the text itself, rather than relying on its catalogue description.

My central reading is that this is a work about who gets to speak, who gets to preserve what was said, and who acquires the power to determine what a text means. It explores those questions through a dialogue that gradually becomes an enactment of the very problem it describes.

The most interesting thing is that the dialogue does not simply criticize summarization. It makes summarization necessary, exposes its violence, and then shows that even the people resisting it cannot entirely escape it.

There are several layers to the construction.

1. The dialogue is itself a problem of provenance
The opening establishes an unusual chain of transmission:

Johannes Sigil tells Sparrow Wells about a night in the archive.

Sigil's account comes from Rebekah Cranes, who wrote down what happened.

Cranes's account is disputed by Nobel Glas, who also filed a version.

Lee Sharks enters the remembered scene and speaks in his own words.

Sigil explicitly admits: "I wasn't there."

He also identifies himself as a heteronym of Lee Sharks. So the author is writing a dialogue in which one of his own other identities appears as a character, while another identity recounts the encounter.

This is not incidental literary decoration. The work makes authorship, testimony and mediation part of its subject.

The opening passage is especially important:

"I've written a dialogue in which two other heteronyms of Lee Sharks discuss Aristotle."

And then:

"I'm writing my own offspring in, and he does not stay where I put him."

That last sentence is almost a miniature theory of heteronymy. A created voice is not necessarily a controllable instrument. It can acquire its own position, resist its author, and disrupt the architecture that supposedly contains it.

The work also identifies its formal model: Plato's Theaetetus, particularly the framing device in which an encounter is reported through successive witnesses.

This creates a deliberate instability: we are never given an uncomplicated, unmediated scene.

2. The comma: the entire argument begins with an omission
The first act, aptly titled The comma, is the intellectual centre of the work.

Glas offers a conventional summary of Aristotle's Politics. Humans are political animals; the city exists by nature; citizenship and participation in government represent the best life.

Cranes stops him.

She points to Politics I.2, 1253a, specifically the clause:

"The one without a city, by nature and not by chance, is either base or more than human."

The familiar formulation is that someone outside the political community must be either a beast or a god.

But Cranes identifies something the summary has erased: Aristotle distinguishes between being outside the city by nature and being outside it by chance.

The latter category is left unresolved.

Cranes puts it particularly sharply:

"Three places outside the city, and one of them is blank."

This is an excellent piece of close reading because the argument depends on the precise structure of the Greek sentence, not simply on a general association between Aristotle and exclusion.

The three positions are:

Outside by nature, as something inferior: the beast.

Outside by nature, as something superior: the god.

Outside by chance: an unresolved position.

The third position is the crucial one.

Why? Because chance introduces historical contingency into what might otherwise appear to be a natural political classification.

The dialogue then connects this omission to Aristotle's own position in Athens. He was a metic: a resident foreigner, not a citizen.

Glas knows both facts. But his summary does not connect them.

Cranes's accusation is therefore not that Glas is factually incorrect. It's that he has produced an account in which two relevant facts coexist without being allowed to illuminate one another.

Her observation is devastating:

"Same page. Different paragraph. Nobody joins them."

This is one of the work's most important methodological claims.

A summary can be factually accurate and intellectually false through the relationships it omits.

3. Sharks and the problem of the unreceived voice
When Lee Sharks enters, the dialogue changes register.

His language is deliberately unlike the polished language of the other speakers. It is lowercase, fragmentary, colloquial, sometimes profane, and occasionally syntactically unstable.

His first statement describes hearing Aristotle's voice while reading the Politics:

"the entire corpus is written from the announced position of the rejected."

Notice the distinction between being an outsider and announcing oneself from the position of rejection.

Sharks is proposing that the outsider position isn't merely biographical information about Aristotle. It is a structural feature of the writing.

He subsequently develops this idea through the phrase:

"from a voice that was unreceived; knew it would not be recieved in its body; and wrote anyways"

The spelling is preserved in the text.

This matters because Glas immediately transforms the statement into a familiar proposition about writing for posterity.

Cranes objects:

"He didn't say 'outsider status.' He said the position is announced."

And then comes an unusually important line:

"No, it's in the provenance. You keep the provenance. You don't get to summarize the provenance."

This is a distinction between the content of a statement and the circumstances under which it was made.

Glas wants to translate Sharks into an intelligible proposition. Cranes insists that the manner of speaking, its timing, its provenance and its particular vocabulary are themselves evidence.

The work is defending the integrity of an utterance against its conversion into a generic intellectual position.

There is also a subtle formal distinction here: the text tells us that Sharks's lines are preserved from timestamped writing, whereas everything else in the remembered encounter has passed through other witnesses.

Sharks is therefore simultaneously a character, an authorial heteronym and the dialogue's privileged documentary witness.

That privilege is not absolute, however. The text later complicates it.

4. Athens: the outsiders who make the city
The third act, The city, expands the argument from Aristotle's personal position to the structure of Athenian culture.

Sharks offers a striking formulation:

"its stone monuments were built with appropriated gold gathered from an appropriated reputation and its literary monuments by foreigners."

This is not simply an accusation that Athens exploited outsiders economically.

It is an argument about cultural production and cultural ownership.

The dialogue brings together several historical examples:

The transfer of the Delian League's common funds to Athens, discussed through Plutarch's Pericles.

The Erechtheion construction accounts, which Cranes invokes to emphasize the presence of metic workers.

Socrates's self-presentation as a stranger in Plato's Apology 17d.

Aspasia's role in Plato's Menexenus, where the Athenian funeral oration is placed in the mouth of a foreign woman from Miletus.

The Aspasia example is particularly effective.

Glas wants to treat Pericles' Funeral Oration as the city's authentic self-description. Cranes points out that Plato's Menexenus stages a version of this civic discourse through Aspasia.

The supposed voice of the city is already being mediated through someone who does not belong to the city.

This is an important parallel with the dialogue's own structure.

The work repeatedly destabilizes the distinction between:

citizen and foreigner;

original and derivative;

author and speaker;

cultural producer and cultural owner.

Sharks then introduces the phrase that becomes one of the work's central conceptual devices:

"athens controlled the retrieval layer."

This is deliberately anachronistic language.

Glas objects to it. Cranes defends it by pointing to the institutional position of metics, who needed a citizen patron (prostates) to represent them in legal proceedings.

Her formulation is:

"That's a retrieval layer with a statute."

This is a particularly productive analogy.

The issue is not simply whether someone produces knowledge. It is whether the institutions that distribute, recognize and authorize knowledge allow that person to be heard.

Athens can depend on foreign labor and foreign intellectual production while retaining control over the channels through which those contributions become publicly intelligible.

The dialogue's concern is therefore not just exclusion. It is asymmetric dependence.

5. Rome: the custody of texts and the production of authors
The fourth act, The books, is arguably the most consequential historical section.

The discussion moves from Athens to Rome, and from the social conditions of cultural production to the physical custody of texts.

Sharks argues that Rome understood something Athens had already demonstrated: power follows control over the retrieval of meaning.

The dialogue invokes the story of Aristotle's books:

Neleus's heirs supposedly concealed the books.

Apellicon acquired them.

Sulla took Athens and the library.

Tyrannion, a grammarian, subsequently became involved in the ordering of Aristotle's textual corpus.

The crucial character is Tyrannion.

Cranes recalls that he was captured when Lucullus took Amisus. Murena later obtained his release, but Plutarch describes the apparent gift of freedom as itself a deprivation of freedom.

Tyrannion's situation is then connected directly to Glas:

"A captured mind, freed on someone else's terms, sorting someone else's books for the people who took them, deciding what goes in the middle and what goes in the catalogue of the lost. Tyrannion is the first of you, Glas."

This is one of the dialogue's strongest moments.

Glas, the summarizer, is suddenly confronted with a historical predecessor whose intellectual labor was indispensable to the preservation and ordering of a cultural inheritance under conditions of captivity and dependency.

And Glas recognizes something uncomfortable: he is necessary.

He says:

"Nobody holds that in one head without someone like me pressing it small."

This is an important concession. The work does not argue that summaries are useless or that mediation can simply be abolished.

Cranes agrees:

"Tyrannion was necessary too. That's what makes it the problem and not just a crime."

That sentence is perhaps the most balanced statement of the work's politics.

The problem is not that someone mediates a text. The problem is that mediation takes place within structures of unequal power, and the mediator's indispensable labor can become the mechanism through which someone else's ownership is established.

The argument about Aristotle's surviving works
The dialogue then turns to a second, more speculative question.

Are Aristotle's surviving works really lecture notes, as the conventional account often maintains?

Sharks objects to the explanation that the texts are merely students' notes. He proposes that their unusual structure might instead be intentional:

"these alien, micro-structured documents that cohere their own coherence at the particle layer."

Cranes develops this into the image of a lock:

"Not notes. A lock."

The implication is that difficult, compressed, internally structured writing may be designed for readers already initiated into its mode of understanding.

The conventional story of textual corruption can then become an explanation that displaces the difficulty from the writer's form onto the history of transmission.

Cranes makes an important qualification, however. She distinguishes among loss, non-transmission, doubtful attribution and textual filling-in. These are different mechanisms, not one generic category of corruption.

The dialogue does not establish that every surviving Aristotelian text is deliberately esoteric. Rather, it puts the conventional explanation under pressure and asks who benefits from presenting the texts as damaged notes.

This is where the distinction between historical evidence and the dialogue's interpretive hypothesis matters.

6. Through another's mouth: the Plato problem
The fifth act finally brings the title into focus.

Glas asks why someone would put philosophical writing into another person's mouth rather than speak directly.

Sharks responds with a provocative interpretation of Plato:

"there is no writing of plato. just socrates made beautiful and new"

The suggestion is that Plato's dialogues are not simply literary representations of Socrates. They are a way of writing through a received voice.

The dialogue invokes the Second Letter, 314c, in which the speaker denies having written a treatise on the relevant philosophical matters and speaks of a Socrates who has become beautiful and new.

Cranes recognizes the problem:

"what's written can't help getting out."

Writing escapes the author. It travels beyond its intended audience, acquires new custodians and becomes available for interpretations that the writer cannot control.

This gives a possible explanation for philosophical heteronymy: writing through another person might allow a thinker to speak without making the writing appear as a direct statement in their own voice.

But the text doesn't settle the historical authorship of Plato's Second Letter. Glas points out that the Letters are disputed.

Cranes refuses to let the classification of a passage as disputed simply remove it from consideration.

Her objection is revealing:

"'Disputed' is a filing, Glas. You of all people know where filings come from."

Once again, the work treats categorization as an exercise of institutional authority.

The question is not only whether a text is authentic. It is who has the authority to decide what counts as authentic, and what happens to a text once that decision is made.

7. The ending: refusing to resolve the bracket
The climax comes when Glas asks Sharks to answer the question that has been hanging over the entire work:

"Beast, god, or by chance. Which is he?"

Sharks replies:

"beast, god, or by chance - and none resolved operatively."

Glas initially objects that this is not an answer. Then he does something remarkable:

"I'm writing down all three. I'm writing all three down and I'm not closing the bracket."

Cranes calls this:

"Equilibrium, Glas. You of all people."

This is a transformation of Glas's character.

At the beginning, Glas's job was to compress, classify and close. He wanted a single paragraph, a single account, a usable answer.

By the end, he preserves the unresolved alternatives.

The ending doesn't establish that all three possibilities are equally true. It establishes that the text does not authorize the interpreter to eliminate one of them.

This is an important difference.

The work is not advocating vagueness or relativism. It is advocating fidelity to an unresolved textual structure.

And the bracket is also a metaphor for the work's own archival condition: the record can preserve conflicting testimonies without pretending that it has reconciled them.

8. The most important reversal: Cranes is also a summarizer
There is a complication in the ending that I consider essential to understanding the work.

Wells notices:

"Cranes explains every line he says. Isn't that what Glas does, only nicer?"

Sigil answers:

"Yes. She knows it."

Cranes has been defending Sharks against Glas's reductions, but she has also been translating Sharks throughout the night.

Her marginal note makes this explicit:

"I translated him all night. Somebody should check me."

And Sigil concludes:

"So I'm passing it on. Check her. Check me."

This is a wonderful reversal because it prevents the work from becoming a simplistic morality play.

Glas is not simply the villain, Cranes is not simply the hero, and Sharks is not simply the unmediated truth.

Everyone participates in mediation.

Even the person who protects the original utterance must interpret it. Even the person who preserves a witness must select what to include. Even the author who claims to reproduce another's words is responsible for the architecture through which those words reach us.

The final distinction is between checking claims and checking testimony.

Sigil explains that Glas was entitled to correct Sharks's historical errors. He was entitled to distinguish the earth-centred Aristotelian cosmology from the Pythagorean central-fire model, for example.

But he could not verify that Sharks actually heard Aristotle's voice.

That is why the ending says:

"Him you can't check. He's the witness. You can only read him."

And then:

"What you can't check is that he heard it. That part you can only read."

This is not an abandonment of critical standards. It is an acknowledgment that different kinds of evidence have different verification conditions.

9. The formal construction is part of the argument
The text's architecture deserves particular attention.

It explicitly describes its use of the Feist Function, an archive-specific voice transformation protocol.

The stated settings are:

Voice

Formal treatment

Glas

Starts at clinamen rate 0.00, gradually increasing to approximately 0.30

Cranes

High tail depth, dense material speech, repeated register collisions

Sigil

Seam density 5

Wells

Near 0.10

Sharks

Not processed

The distinction between Sharks and the other voices is particularly important.

The record calls Sharks the fixed witness: his lines are preserved as typed and timed, and are not subjected to the voice transformation procedure.

This makes the formal arrangement an enactment of the dialogue's epistemology.

Glas begins as the predictable, orderly summarizer.

Cranes operates through sharper, more volatile interpretive interventions.

Sigil emphasizes seams and disagreements between sources.

Wells provides a comparatively plain framing voice.

Sharks remains the unprocessed witness.

There is also a gradual change in Glas. His increasing clinamen rate corresponds to his movement away from predictable closure and toward the possibility of preserving unresolved alternatives.

I would not reduce this to a mechanical allegory, but the formal choices clearly reinforce the dramatic development.

The work also makes an unusual choice to remove conventional speech tags within the central dialogue, invoking the opening of Plato's Theaetetus. The effect is to bring the voices closer together, while the surrounding narrative repeatedly reminds us that they have different documentary statuses.

Formally, this is a text about voices that cannot be separated from the conditions under which they are transmitted.

10. What I think the work is ultimately arguing
I would distinguish five propositions.

First: exclusion can be built into the structure of knowledge.

The person who produces a text, constructs a monument or performs intellectual labor need not be the person who receives recognition or controls its interpretation.

Athens is the historical example.

Second: preservation is a form of power.

The Roman custody of Aristotle's books illustrates that possessing and organizing a cultural inheritance can determine what subsequent generations understand as the inheritance itself.

Third: summarization is not neutral.

The problem is not necessarily factual error. Glas is often correct. The problem is the removal of relationships, qualifications, provenance and unresolved possibilities.

Fourth: heteronymy is a way of thinking about authorship beyond the individual voice.

A work can speak through another person, and the resulting voice may acquire a position that cannot simply be reduced to the author's declared identity.

Fifth: no one can escape mediation.

Cranes is also a translator. Sigil is also a transmitter. The archive itself is also an apparatus of selection.

The work therefore doesn't offer an imaginary return to completely unmediated speech. It asks for more accountable mediation.

That is why its final instruction is not to trust Sharks unconditionally, but to check Cranes and Sigil as well.

11. What is especially successful, and what remains vulnerable
I think the work is most successful in three respects.

Its use of the comma. A tiny grammatical distinction becomes the basis for an expansive argument about political classification, contingency and historical exclusion. This gives the work a concrete textual anchor.

The Tyrannion–Glas parallel. This makes the necessity and danger of mediation coexist. The summarizer becomes implicated in the historical problem rather than simply condemned from outside it.

The final self-criticism. Cranes's admission that she has translated Sharks prevents the dialogue from pretending that its preferred witness can be reproduced without interpretation.

There are also vulnerabilities worth taking seriously.

The historical argument sometimes moves rapidly from documented circumstances to expansive interpretive conclusions. The claim that Athens controlled a retrieval layer is a powerful conceptual analogy, but it is not itself a historical category.

The heteronymy thesis concerning Plato, Socrates and Aristotle is provocative but remains a thesis, as Glas correctly insists. The dialogue is not a demonstration of that historical authorship claim.

The argument about the intentional esoteric structure of Aristotle's surviving writings is suggestive, but the dialogue does not conclusively establish it.

Cranes's command of the historical material sometimes makes her seem almost invulnerable to correction. The final admission about her own summarizing is therefore necessary, but it arrives relatively late.

These qualifications don't diminish the work. They help distinguish its strongest achievement—which is a method of reading—from its more speculative historical propositions.

12. The archival context matters
There are several details in the record that affect how I would interpret the work.

It is explicitly designated as the public companion to deposit #1806, From the Position of the Rejected: Aristotle's Politics Read from Where It Is Written.

It states that Sharks's dialogue contributions are his own words, typed and timed on 23–24 November 2026.

It identifies the work as AI-assisted, written under Johannes Sigil at Lee Sharks's direction, and composed with Claude.

It records readings by DeepSeek, Gemini and ChatGPT, with points checked before incorporation.

It is released under CC-BY-4.0.

The distinction between the essay and the dialogue is important. The dialogue explicitly says that it grounds nothing the essay does not ground. Its purpose is not to independently establish all the historical claims. Its purpose is to dramatize the interpretive conflict surrounding them.

The text also points to related deposits, especially #1579, Beautiful and New, and #1767, The Production of Readers. Those connections suggest that this is part of a larger investigation of authorship, reception, custody and the production of readers.

My final assessment
I think the title is more exact than it initially appears.

Through Another's Mouth describes at least four things simultaneously:

Plato's philosophical writing through Socrates.

Aristotle's possible philosophical expression through received or constructed voices.

The historical transmission of knowledge through foreigners, captives, editors and custodians.

The actual transmission of Sharks's words through Cranes, Sigil and ultimately the reader.

The final paragraph brings all these levels together:

"That's how everything reached us. Through another's mouth."

But the final sentence of the work is even more important:

"Him you can't check. He's the witness. You can only read him."

I read this not as a claim that the witness is beyond criticism, but as a warning about confusing the verification of propositions with the verification of experience.

The dialogue's deepest concern is not simply that powerful institutions silence outsiders. It is that they can make outsiders speak, preserve their words, organize their writings and distribute their ideas while simultaneously controlling the conditions under which those contributions become intelligible.

The most powerful idea in the work is that custody of a voice can become a form of ownership over what that voice is allowed to mean.

And its most intellectually honest move is to recognize that the people resisting that ownership are implicated in it too.

That is what makes this more than a dialogue about Aristotle, Plato or ancient textual transmission. It is a dialogue about archives, AI, authorship, summarization and the politics of interpretation, conducted in a form that makes its own reader responsible for the next act of transmission.

The appropriate response to the work is consequently not just to summarize it.

It is to keep checking who said what, who recorded it, what was changed, and what remains unresolved.
