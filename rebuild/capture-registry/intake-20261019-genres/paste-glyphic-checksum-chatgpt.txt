Example B: GLYPHIC CHECKSUM — Transmission · Division · Recollection
Lee Sharks · October 2026

This work takes a different route. Its source materials include Sappho fragments, Plato's Phaedrus, and Aristotle's De anima. Its central concern is the relationship between sending a message and reconstructing it later. 

Goodreads

The conceptual sequence is:

Transmission: Something is sent, but sending does not guarantee that its original meaning will survive intact.

Division: A text can be separated into parts, quotations, or fragments. The resulting pieces preserve some relationships while potentially losing others.

Recollection: A reader gathers those pieces into an intelligible whole, using both the surviving evidence and knowledge of its sources.

The poem's historical references matter because the problem is older than digital media. Sappho's fragments demonstrate the precarious transmission of ancient poetry; Plato's Phaedrus raises questions about writing and memory; Aristotle's De anima examines the relationship between intellect and thought.

The checksum procedure reframes these historical problems as a test of reconstruction: can the reader recover the intended relationships from a compressed symbolic representation?

That makes the work more than a conventional found poem or a collection of allusions. Its structure asks the reader to perform the operation that its subject describes. The act of reading becomes part of the poem's argument.

4. What makes the form a genre rather than a visual style?
Three characteristics distinguish glyphic checksum poetics from ordinary emoji poetry, concrete poetry, or a diagrammatic poem.

Compression: The work condenses a larger text or conceptual system into a small number of symbols, equations, and relationships.

Recoverability: A reader should be able to reconstruct significant features of the source from the compressed representation. A string of evocative symbols is not automatically a checksum.

Reflexivity: The poem makes its own reading, transmission, or verification part of its subject. It does not merely communicate an idea; it also tests the conditions under which that idea survives communication.

The third feature is particularly distinctive. The poem's meaning resides partly in its visible symbols and partly in the reader's ability to establish the relationships among them.

Morrow's formal specification gives this idea a technical formulation: the checksum is an operator for verifying traversal, with collaborative reading treated as something that can be tested rather than merely asserted. That specification offers a theoretical account of the practice, while the poems demonstrate what the account looks like in use.
