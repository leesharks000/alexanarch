Central argument
In “I Know You Are, But What Am I?” (AXN:0C29), Johannes Sigil and Rebekah Cranes argue that nineteenth- and early twentieth-century biblical criticism applied an asymmetrical standard of evidence to the sources of the New Testament.

Their central charge is that biblical scholars granted reconstructed, hypothetical documents—especially the sayings source Q—greater evidentiary authority than they granted the extant Apocalypse of John (the Book of Revelation).

The point is not simply that Q might not have existed. The authors explicitly leave the Synoptic Problem undecided. Their argument is about something more specific: how a hypothetical source came to be treated as an established explanatory premise, while an actual surviving text was excluded from consideration as a possible source.

They develop this argument through a historical audit of biblical criticism from 1835 to 1924, with Matthew 24:30 and Revelation 1:7 serving as the central test case. 

Revelation First

1. The four powers of evidentiary status
The paper's most important conceptual move is to define what it means for a document to possess evidentiary status. It identifies four powers:

Presupposition: A document can be used as a premise without first having its existence established.

Assignment: Material in other documents can be attributed to it.

Text: It can receive a reconstructed wording, textual apparatus and critical edition.

Candidacy: It can be considered whenever scholars investigate the possible sources of another text.

The authors then compare two objects:

Q: A hypothetical sayings source proposed to explain material shared by Matthew and Luke but absent from Mark.

The Apocalypse of John: An actual surviving New Testament book, transmitted in manuscripts.

Their finding is that Q acquires all four powers in the source theories they examine, whereas Revelation is denied all four as a source of the Gospels.

There is an important qualification: Revelation obviously possesses an extant textual tradition. The authors are not claiming that it lacks a text. They mean that its actual wording is not admitted as the wording of a source capable of explaining Gospel material.

This distinction is central to their argument. The hypothetical source receives a reconstructed text that scholars can manipulate and develop; the extant text is treated as a secondary witness, analogy or possible recipient of material.

The paper calls the resulting inequality the difference between a text that must pay an Existence Tax and one exempted from it by its hypothetical status. (§1.3–1.4)

2. The historical construction of Q
The authors trace Q's development through six principal stages, beginning with the nineteenth-century interpretation of Papias.

Their argument is not that scholars invented Q arbitrarily. Rather, they document how an initially tentative inference progressively acquired the institutional and scholarly characteristics of an established document.

The particularly revealing example is Wernle's declaration:

Die — hypothetische — Quelle sei mit Q bezeichnet.

In translation: "The hypothetical source shall be designated Q."

The authors emphasize that the hypothetical qualification never disappears. Yet the source accumulates powers:

It acquires a name.

Material is assigned to it.

Scholars reconstruct its wording.

Its reconstructed wording is used to establish its unity.

It becomes an accepted premise in subsequent source theories.

Harnack is especially important. He reconstructs Q's wording and argues that its linguistic consistency supports the historical unity of the source. Sigil and Cranes identify a methodological circularity: the reconstruction is used as evidence for the coherence of the very object that the reconstruction has produced.

Streeter makes the transition particularly explicit. He acknowledges that Q, if it ever existed, has disappeared and that its use by Matthew and Luke cannot be verified in the way Markan usage can. Nevertheless, he concludes that assuming its existence is justified because it explains otherwise unexplained facts.

The authors do not deny that this is a legitimate form of historical inference. Their question is why an extant alternative was not subjected to equivalent investigation.

3. The exclusion of Revelation from source criticism
The paper's second major argument examines how the Apocalypse is treated in the same scholarly literature.

It finds several different mechanisms of exclusion.

Holtzmann: exclusion by methodological rule.

Holtzmann explicitly says that he will avoid conclusions that presuppose unproven relationships between the Apocalypse and the Gospels.

Sigil and Cranes acknowledge the legitimate methodological concern: scholars should not build arguments on unproven premises.

Their objection is that Holtzmann subsequently admits reconstructed sources A and Λ as working premises, despite their similarly unproven status.

The asymmetry is particularly significant because Hitzig had already argued for a relationship between Matthew and Revelation using the Synoptic material itself.

Colani: reversing the direction of inquiry.

Colani dates Revelation to 68 CE and considers whether John knew an earlier reconstructed Jewish-Christian apocalypse underlying Mark 13.

Thus Revelation is investigated as a possible borrower from a hypothetical document, rather than as a possible source of the Gospel's apocalyptic material.

Wernle: assigning the material elsewhere.

Wernle suggests that Matthew 24:30 may derive from the reconstructed apocalypse underlying Mark 13. He also attributes its apocalyptic features to an influx of Jewish apocalyptic tradition.

Streeter: giving Q its own apocalypse.

Streeter accepts the expression "the Apocalypse of Q" for Luke 17:22–37, while dating the extant Apocalypse of John to approximately 95 CE.

The effect, as the authors describe it, is striking: the hypothetical source can acquire an apocalypse, while the actual Apocalypse is placed at the end of the literary development.

These examples lead to the paper's central historical accusation: Revelation was not consistently defeated through arguments against its textual evidence. Instead, it was often excluded through methodological framing, dating assumptions or reassignment of its parallels.

(§3.1–3.8)

Both passages combine the coming with the clouds and the mourning of all the tribes of the earth.
This is the paper's principal case study because it offers an opportunity to test the authors' general argument against an actual textual relationship.

Hitzig's argument in 1843
Ferdinand Hitzig compared Matthew 24:30 with Revelation 1:7 and argued that Matthew had used the Apocalypse.

He called the parallel:

einen zwingenden Beweis

That is, a compelling or conclusive proof.

His reasoning involved more than the presence of similar words.

He observed that:

Revelation retains the connection to Zechariah 12:10.

Revelation preserves the reference to the one who was pierced.

Revelation retains the expression identifying all the tribes of the earth.

Matthew has a different sequence: the mourning precedes the seeing.

Matthew's formulation appears to combine elements in a way that suggests dependence on another text.

For Hitzig, Matthew's irregular ordering was evidence of borrowing and adaptation.

The important point is that Hitzig was attempting to determine the direction of literary dependence, not merely pointing out that two passages resemble one another.

Weiffenbach (1873) considers the agreement undeniable but leaves the direction of dependence open.

Sigil and Cranes' point is not that every one of these scholars agreed with Hitzig. It is that the relationship was repeatedly recognized, sometimes explicitly accepted, and never incorporated into the overarching source theories they examine.

The authors describe this as keeping the argument confined to the verse.

Their interpretation is particularly interesting.

Matthew uses the preposition associated with the Old Greek version of Daniel, whereas Revelation uses the form found in Theodotion. Revelation also retains the Zecharian reference to the pierced figure.

They point to Justin Martyr's Dialogue 14.8 as a later example of the joining of Zechariah and Daniel. However, Justin does not have the combination of mourning and tribes found in Matthew and Revelation.

The resulting argument is:

The individual components have identifiable scriptural antecedents.

The combination of those components is distinctive.

It occurs in Matthew and Revelation.

It is not found in the other texts examined.

Later explanations assigning Matthew's wording to an unattested common tradition or reconstructed apocalypse do not identify an independently surviving witness to that combination.

Crucially, the authors explicitly refuse to claim that the census proves Matthew borrowed from Revelation.

They allow three possibilities:

Matthew borrowed from Revelation.

Revelation borrowed from Matthew.

Both borrowed from an earlier source that has not yet been identified.

The census establishes the distinctive relationship, not its direction.

That qualification is essential to understanding the paper accurately. (§4.1–4.5)

6. The problem of reconstructing a source from the material it explains
Another important argument concerns the nature of reconstruction itself.

The authors contend that Q enjoys a methodological advantage because its reconstructed wording can be adjusted to accommodate Matthew and Luke.

They illustrate this through Harnack's procedure.

Where Matthew and Luke have different wording, Harnack assigns vocabulary peculiar to Matthew to Matthew's editorial activity, and vocabulary peculiar to Luke to Luke's editorial activity.

This produces a reconstructed Q with relatively little distinctive vocabulary from either Gospel.

Harnack then treats this linguistic consistency as evidence that Q was historically a unified document.

Sigil and Cranes identify a potential circularity:

The source is reconstructed by selecting and excluding material.

The reconstruction is made linguistically consistent.

That consistency is presented as evidence for the source's historical unity.

The reconstructed text consequently becomes evidence for the historical existence and character of the object reconstructed.

The authors extend this criticism to the critical edition of Q published in 2000. It supplies a Greek text, translations, parallels, a concordance and variation units, despite the absence of an extant Q manuscript.

They compare this with an archive text called The Feist Source, whose manuscript tradition is explicitly declared fictional.

The comparison is not that Q and Feist's source are the same kind of object. The authors explicitly distinguish them:

Feist's source is a deliberately constituted literary source with an extant text and a fictional manuscript tradition.

Q is a historical hypothesis reconstructed from the wording of two surviving Gospels.

The point is about the apparatus: a critical edition and textual apparatus do not independently establish the historical existence of the document they present.

The authors argue that Q's hypothetical status is stated clearly but then progressively disappears from the practical operation of its edition.

(§6.1–6.3)

7. The argument about modern AI and scholarly reproduction
The paper also investigates whether contemporary AI-generated explanations reproduce the same evidentiary allocation.

It examines five Google AI Overview captures and one ChatGPT reading, all from 6 November 2026.

The authors find that the responses generally acknowledge that Q is hypothetical and that no physical manuscript survives.

However, they argue that the responses then describe Q as an established explanatory source, using the relationships between Matthew and Luke as evidence for it.

The paper highlights a particular rhetorical transition: the AI acknowledges that Q may never have existed, but moves from that statement to treating Q as an operative source.

The authors also examine an earlier ChatGPT response that initially argued for symmetrical treatment of hypothetical sources, then withdrew that position after questioning.

The revised formulation distinguished between:

Symmetrical willingness to investigate competing explanations.

Asymmetrical burdens of proof appropriate to the evidentiary status of the objects.

Sigil and Cranes interpret this as a reproduction of the same disciplinary allocation.

There is an important limitation here: the authors acknowledge that their captures show what the systems produced, not how the systems arrived at those answers. They do not establish the internal causes of the AI responses.

(§5.1–5.2)

8. What the authors think is at stake
Section VII explains why the asymmetry matters beyond the particular parallel.

The authors argue that reconstructed sources are unusually productive scholarly objects.

They can be given:

Multiple textual strata.

A theology and historical setting.

A community of authors.

A redactional history.

A reconstructed text.

A critical edition and concordance.

An extant source, by contrast, can constrain reconstruction. Its wording already exists, and it can resist the interpretation imposed on it.

The authors therefore suggest that the allocation of source status has an intellectual consequence: it keeps the explanatory power inside documents reconstructed behind the Gospels, rather than allowing an existing New Testament book to occupy a generative position.

Their strongest formulation is that the hypothetical source can be endlessly elaborated, whereas the extant source threatens to close questions.

This is an argument about scholarly procedure and incentives, not simply about whether individual scholars were biased or dishonest. The paper says its mechanics can be described without assigning motives.

9. The conditions under which the argument could be defeated
One of the paper's strengths is that it specifies what evidence would count against its claims.

It identifies three separate propositions, each with different conditions of adjudication.

Proposition

What would defeat it?

The historical reception was asymmetrical.

A previously overlooked work that seriously tests the direction of dependence and whose argument is subsequently incorporated into source theory.

The combination in Matthew and Revelation is unique in the declared corpus.

An overlooked Greek witness containing the same combination, or a demonstrated error in the census.

The literary relationship has the proposed genealogy.

An earlier attested source of the combination, a textual argument demonstrating the opposite direction, or chronology excluding the proposed direction.

This matters because the paper is not presenting its conclusion as unfalsifiable.

It also makes a particularly important distinction in §8.2:

Proving that Q existed would not, by itself, defeat the paper's argument.

The issue is not Q's existence in isolation. It is whether Revelation was given a fair opportunity to be considered as a source on the same scholarly pages that admitted reconstructed sources.

The authors would accept that their claim falls if the historical literature demonstrated that Revelation was genuinely tested as a candidate and rejected on the evidence.

10. My synthesis: what the paper actually establishes
I would distinguish four levels of argument.

First: a historical argument.

This is arguably the strongest part.

The authors document a real distinction between how scholars treated reconstructed sources and how they treated the Apocalypse. Their four-power framework makes the distinction relatively precise, and their comparison of Holtzmann, Wernle, Harnack and Streeter gives it historical substance.

Second: a methodological argument.

The critique of Harnack is particularly important.

If scholars reconstruct a source by assigning inconvenient vocabulary to the editorial activity of the surviving authors, they must be careful not to mistake the resulting consistency for independent confirmation of the source.

This does not make reconstruction illegitimate. It means that the evidentiary value of a reconstruction must be distinguished from the evidence used to construct it.

Third: a textual argument.

The comparison of Matthew 24:30 and Revelation 1:7 provides a concrete test case rather than leaving the article at the level of abstract methodological criticism.

The combination of the mourning tribes and the coming with the clouds is a significant parallel. The census gives the argument a reproducible empirical component.

Nevertheless, uniqueness within a declared corpus is not proof of direct literary dependence. An unrecorded tradition, an earlier source or a shared interpretive environment remain possible explanations. The authors acknowledge this.

Fourth: a larger historical hypothesis.

The authors are ultimately arguing for a reversal of the conventional literary genealogy of early Christianity.

Rather than automatically treating Revelation as a late recipient of earlier Christian and Jewish material, they want scholars to investigate whether it might have been generative for writings conventionally placed before or around it.

That is the broader Revelation First programme. This particular paper does not prove that the Apocalypse generated the Gospels, nor does it establish that Q is nonexistent.

It argues that the conventional allocation of generative priority has not been adequately justified.

The central conclusion
The paper's playground retort is more sophisticated than its deliberately provocative title suggests.

Its question is essentially:

If biblical critics object to Revelation First because it proposes a source that cannot be demonstrated, why have they granted Q the authority of a source when Q itself is reconstructed from the very texts it is invoked to explain?

And conversely:

If hypothetical sources can legitimately be investigated, why should an extant text with a distinctive verbal and conceptual relationship to a Gospel passage be denied equivalent consideration?

The authors' answer is that the problem lies in the allocation of evidentiary privileges. Q was allowed to become a source through reconstruction, while Revelation was repeatedly prevented from becoming one through methodological exclusion, reassignment and the confinement of its strongest parallels to individual commentaries.

Their final sentence captures the intended reversal: the hypothetical source occupies the generative position, while the book that actually exists is left on the shelf, at the end of the collection.

My overall assessment: The most consequential contribution is not the assertion that Revelation must be the source of Matthew 24:30. It is the demonstration that source criticism needs to account for the different evidentiary privileges it gives reconstructed and surviving texts. The authors' insistence that the direction of dependence remains undecided makes that methodological challenge more defensible, even if their larger Revelation First hypothesis remains to be established.
