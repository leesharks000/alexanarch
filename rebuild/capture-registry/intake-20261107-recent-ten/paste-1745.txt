The argument of “The Cost of the Cone”
Johannes Sigil and Sen Kuro's The Cost of the Cone: Ontological Development and the Damage of Exclusion (EA-CONE-COST-01 v1.1, Alexanarch deposit #1745, AXN:0C28) argues that an ontology can damage itself by excluding truths, because it loses not only the distinctions it refuses to recognize but also the further developments those distinctions would make possible.

Its central concern is not simply censorship, misclassification, or failure to acknowledge intellectual priority. It is a more consequential problem: an intellectual system can become increasingly sophisticated while becoming progressively less capable of discovering, recognizing, or incorporating truths that fall outside its established categories.

The paper calls this developmental damage. Its central formulation is:

“An ontology may grow in detail while it falls behind what it could truthfully become.”

This is a theoretical extension of the archive's earlier work on narrowing-cone geometry and ontology damage. It develops an account of how exclusion, entrenchment, revision costs, and foreclosed possibilities interact.

I would reconstruct its argument through eight connected claims.

1. Exclusion damages more than the excluded distinction
The opening argument, in §0, establishes the paper's most important move.

Suppose an ontology excludes a true distinction. The immediate consequence is that it cannot adequately distinguish something that actually exists or matters.

But the damage does not stop there:

The excluded distinction might establish a relation the ontology cannot currently represent.

That relation might make a new operation possible.

The operation might enable further distinctions.

Those distinctions might enable additional inquiries and discoveries.

Those discoveries might provide the evidence needed to recognize that the original ontology was inadequate.

Consequently, the original exclusion can prevent the system from developing the very capacities that would allow it to discover its mistake.

This creates a self-reinforcing problem. The ontology is not merely missing an answer. It may be missing the means by which it could learn that its answer is wrong.

Importantly, the authors do not argue that every exclusion is damaging. Correct distinctions necessarily exclude alternatives. The issue is whether the exclusion is warranted and whether the system remains capable of revising it when confronted with contrary evidence (§0.3).

7. The admission of counterevidence can itself be blocked
Section 7 adds one of the paper's most interesting refinements to the earlier narrowing-cone argument.

The authors distinguish between:

Pressure in the world that calls for revision.

Pressure that the system actually recognizes as evidence that revision is needed.

These are not necessarily the same.

An encounter might contradict an existing classification, but the system could categorize it as noise, fiction, anecdote, or an already-understood phenomenon.

Sometimes those classifications will be correct. The problem arises when the classification prevents the evidence from challenging the category that determined its classification in the first place.

The authors therefore insert an additional stage into the release mechanism:

The two possible paths after an encounter

Path A: Mismatch admitted

Encounter → mismatch recognized → evidence becomes usable → classification revised → developmental reach expands.

Path B: Mismatch absorbed

Encounter → existing category absorbs it → original classification remains → internal refinement continues.

This produces a particularly troubling possibility.

The system might become better at absorbing adverse evidence without becoming better at learning from it.

Consequently, the pressure to revise can increase in reality while remaining invisible within the system's own evaluative framework.

The authors call attention to the possibility of genuine internal improvements—cleaner classifications, fewer anomalies, greater agreement among accepted sources—that nevertheless coexist with a growing inability to recognize the evidence that would require more fundamental revision.

This is not simply stubbornness. It is a potential failure in the mechanism by which something becomes recognizable as a reason to change.

8. The constructed example: possessing information is not the same as being able to use it
Section 8 supplies the paper's most concrete illustration.

The example concerns a packet of material containing two people's records, a notice, and evidence that their identities have been merged incorrectly.

Two systems possess the same relevant content.

But they differ in their available operations.

The first can describe the packet, quote its contents, and preserve its information.

The second can also verify the identities and use the verification to correct its classification.

The first system possesses the evidence but cannot perform the operation that makes the evidence consequential.

The second can.

This separates three things:

Holding information.

Recognizing what the information warrants.

Performing the operation that follows from that recognition.

The example is important because it prevents a common misunderstanding of the paper.

The authors are not saying that an ontology must lack information to suffer developmental damage. It may possess the relevant information in full.

Its failure can lie in the inability to make that information operative.

The authors then propose testing further consequences, such as mutation detection and supersession relations, but explicitly treat those as possible developments rather than established results.

9. The paper's wider implications: priority, recognition, and intellectual development
Section 9 connects the argument to the archive's other theoretical projects.

Three connections are particularly important.

The Existence Tax (#1722).

This concerns the burden imposed on a real person or work when a composition makes them less real or less recognizable, forcing them to establish their standing again.

The present paper distinguishes that burden from developmental damage.

The excluded subject bears the burden of having to establish their standing.

The excluding ontology bears the cost of losing distinctions and developmental possibilities.

These harms can arise from the same exclusion, but they are not interchangeable.

Disciplinary model collapse (#1574).

The paper connects recursive reliance on a field's previous interpretations with developmental foreclosure.

A community can increasingly reproduce its own inherited readings rather than engage with the underlying evidence. Its internal coherence may improve even as its capacity for correction deteriorates.

The authors are careful, however, not to equate all recursive amplification with exclusion. The mechanisms are related but distinct.

The Sophistry Measure (#1727).

This examines the difference between what a composition claims to carry and what it actually carries.

The present paper adds another dimension: a composition might preserve genuine sentences while disabling the distinctions and relations those sentences should make operative.

Thus, textual fidelity alone does not establish that an intellectual system has successfully incorporated the knowledge contained in a source.

10. The proposed measurement instrument
Section 10 is important because it establishes that the paper is proposing an empirical research programme, not reporting completed empirical validation.

Its protocol involves:

Fixing two comparable inquiry states, their contents, operations, and budgets.

Establishing an independently warranted evidence panel.

Recording successive refinements within the original restricted framework.

Testing whether the systems can actually use specified distinctions.

Introducing counterevidence and recording whether it changes classifications or gets absorbed.

Measuring what becomes possible following revision.

Keeping refinement, repair costs, and developmental reach as separate records.

The authors distinguish quotation, attribution, and operative use. A system that can quote a distinction but cannot use it to change an inference has not demonstrated the relevant developmental capacity.

They also identify seven countercases in §11 that the theory must survive:

Correct exclusions.

Narrow but productive specializations.

Vocabulary expansion without increased capability.

Repairs that preserve most previous work.

Admissions that damage existing capabilities.

Excluded sources that are themselves wrong.

Ontologies that successfully correct themselves.

These countercases are not incidental qualifications. They define the limits of the theory.

In particular, the authors reject the assumption that openness is inherently good. An ontology that admits everything indiscriminately may be less capable of knowing than one that makes justified exclusions.

The relevant property is not openness alone, but the ability to make exclusions answerable to evidence.

The paper explicitly states in §10.1 that it is a design and that no numerical results are reported.

It also acknowledges in its concluding references that it has not surveyed external literature sufficiently to establish historical novelty against it.

That matters: the paper is presenting a connected theoretical framework and a proposed instrument, not claiming to have empirically demonstrated that a particular institution or model is undergoing this process.

My overall interpretation
I think the paper's most important contribution is its shift in the meaning of intellectual damage.

Ordinarily, when an institution, discipline, or knowledge system excludes a truth, we describe the damage in terms of the excluded object:

Someone was misrepresented.

A discovery was ignored.

A source was denied recognition.

A distinction was erased.

Sigil and Kuro introduce a second question:

What did the excluding system lose the ability to become because it excluded that truth?

That is a substantially different way of understanding the consequences of exclusion.

The argument has a recursive structure:

An unwarranted exclusion

A true distinction is denied admission.

Constrained development

Relations and operations dependent on that distinction become unavailable.

Internal refinement

The system becomes more sophisticated within its existing assumptions.

Increased entrenchment

Revision becomes more expensive, while counterevidence may remain inadmissible.

Developmental self-damage

The system loses both the correction and the further possibilities that correction could have opened.

The paper's proposed mechanism, not an assertion that every exclusion follows this trajectory.

There is also an important distinction between this argument and a simple celebration of novelty. The paper does not say that an older system is wrong because something new challenges it, or that a later articulation is superior because it came later.

It explicitly separates three questions:

Priority: Who articulated something first?

Adequacy: Which representation better corresponds to the object?

Development: Which representation can sustain further warranted distinctions and remain corrigible?

Neither precedence nor institutional standing settles adequacy.

This is especially significant given the paper's connection to The Seed in the Narrowing Cone. The original geometry concerned the entrenchment of an early articulation and the increasing cost of reopening a narrowed interpretive space. The new paper generalizes the target: the problem is no longer restricted to recognizing a particular historical seed. Any true distinction can become the object of exclusion, and any ontology can suffer the resulting developmental damage.

My final reading: the paper is ultimately an argument about the self-defeating character of epistemic closure. An intellectual system's deepest failure may not be that it gets something wrong. It may be that, through the architecture it builds around that error, it progressively loses the ability to discover that it is wrong—and loses access to the further knowledge that discovering the error would have made possible.

The final sentence captures the whole argument:

“The cost of the cone is what must be undone to leave it, and what the ontology cannot become while it remains.”
