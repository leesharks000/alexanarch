# Lee Sharks and the retrocausal construction of a canon

An interpretive reading of the Crimson Hexagon: poetry, heteronymy, operative semiotics, archival engineering, and the politics of historical recognition.

The most productive way to read Lee Sharks is to resist the temptation to divide the work into an early literary period and a later theoretical or archival period. That division reproduces precisely the kind of historical organization the corpus is trying to unsettle.

I propose a stronger thesis:

The Lee Sharks corpus is an extended act of retrocausal canon formation: it produces literary and theoretical objects in the present, constructs the historical and institutional conditions under which those objects can be recognized as having mattered, and then deposits the evidence that allows a future reader—or machine—to reconstruct that history as an intelligible tradition.

This is not simply a writer making a reputation, nor an author retrospectively explaining an existing body of work. It is an attempt to make the future recognition of a work part of the work's present composition.

The distinction matters. In conventional canon formation, an institution selects works from an inherited past and confers legitimacy upon them. In the Sharks project, the work also attempts to construct the institutions, vocabularies, genealogies, and retrieval pathways through which its past will subsequently become legible.

The canon is not merely what survives. It is partly the machinery that determines what survival will mean.

## 1. The 2014 origin: a literary work that contains its future

The first indispensable object is Pearl and Other Poems (New Human Press, 2014).

Its importance is not merely that it is an early collection. It already contains the formal problem that the later archive will spend years developing.

The 2014 introduction, attributed to Johannes Sigil, describes the Crimson Hexagon as a project of distinct writerly identities and selections from fantastic, nonexistent works. It calls the result a form of applied literary history, borrowing Pound's formulation of a history that includes poems.

That description offers a crucial interpretive key.

The project begins by treating authorship as something that can be distributed across invented identities, biographies, genres, and documentary forms. A poem can have a fictional institutional history. An invented author can have a bibliography. A nonexistent anthology can participate in a real literary history.

This is not simply pseudonymity.

Pessoa provides one important precedent, but Sharks's project goes further in one particular direction: the heteronym is not only a different voice or personality. It can become a node in a developing literary infrastructure.

The 2015 essay Make It Human makes the program explicit:

> The New Human poetry began just now, when I announced it.

The sentence is both humorous and theoretically serious. The movement is invented, but its invention is itself an historical event. The essay proceeds to describe poetry as a process of making the human rather than expressing an already completed human essence.

Its formulation of the poet as someone who discovers and shapes the indentations of the future is particularly important.

Alexanarch

Here we can already identify three operations:

- Invention: creating literary forms, voices, movements, and historical materials.
- Retrospection: providing those creations with a genealogy and a historical context.
- Prospection: shaping the conditions under which future readers will recognize their significance.

These operations are not successive stages. They are simultaneous dimensions of the literary act.

### The poem as a temporal machine

The title poem, Pearl, complicates the apparent triumphalism of the surrounding movement-making.

Its opening is a lyric of loneliness, longing, and release. The poem imagines the production of a metaphorical world and then imagines the destruction of metaphor itself. Its second movement begins:

> After my poem has happened, I will wish I could take it back.

That sentence deserves to be read as an early theoretical proposition.

The poem imagines itself from a position subsequent to its own production. It stages the future regret of a completed utterance, the retrospective disassembly of its metaphors, and the archaeological recovery of their fragments.

The poem's future is not simply an imagined continuation. It changes the meaning of the present composition.

This is a literary precursor to retrocausal canon formation, although I would distinguish the two: the poem performs temporal recursion at the level of poetic consciousness, whereas the later theory makes historical and institutional recognition the object of that recursion.

The most important transformation is the movement from sequence to topology.

In a sequence, one work follows another.

In a topology, a work can be related to several others simultaneously, through different interpretive dimensions. A poem can belong to a literary lineage, a theoretical argument, a room, an institutional history, and a machine-readable dataset.

This changes what it means to encounter a work.

It also changes what it means to be an author. The author becomes partly a composer of relations among objects, not simply the producer of individual texts.

That is the significance of the Crimson Hexagon's claim to be a distributed epic rather than a multivolume collection.

## 3. Retrocausal canon formation: the theoretical center

The central theoretical development is the January 2026 work Retrocausal Canon Formation and the Political Economy of the Past, attributed to Lee Sharks and Johannes Sigil.

Its central proposition is that future acts can reorganize the value of past meanings.

This should not be confused with literal backward causation.

A future event does not physically alter an earlier text. It alters the interpretive conditions under which that text is recognized, selected, connected, and valued.

Consider the difference:

| Conventional canon formation | Retrocausal canon formation |
| --- | --- |
| The past supplies candidate works | The future helps determine which past works become legible |
| Institutions select and preserve | Authors construct prospective conditions of recognition |
| Reception follows composition | Anticipated reception enters composition |
| Historical importance is assigned retrospectively | Historical importance is prepared prospectively |
| The archive records a canon | The archive participates in producing one |

The distinction is between changing the past and changing the historical intelligibility of the past.

This is where the Sharks project intersects with established theories of reception and cultural production.

- Hans Robert Jauss: A work's historical meaning develops through changing horizons of expectation.
- Pierre Bourdieu: Literary value is produced within fields of cultural authority, competition, and consecration.
- John Guillory: Canon formation is inseparable from the institutional distribution of cultural capital.
- Walter Benjamin: The past can become newly legible in a historical present that reorganizes its significance.

Sharks's intervention is to bring these problems into contact with computational infrastructure.

The future reader is not necessarily a university, a critic, or a publisher. It may be a training pipeline, an indexing system, a retrieval engine, or a summarizer.

The archive therefore attempts to anticipate the mechanisms that will determine what can be remembered.

### Prospective provenance anchors

One particularly interesting concept is the Prospective Provenance Anchor.

The basic idea is to create documents whose metadata, identifiers, conceptual vocabulary, citations, and explicit relations make them available for future retrieval.

The document is not simply preserved. It is prepared to be found and interpreted.

This is an important distinction between conventional archival preservation and the project being undertaken here.

Preservation asks: Can the object survive?

Prospective provenance asks: Can the object survive in a form that retains its identity and its relationships when encountered by a future information system?

The latter is a much more ambitious problem.

It concerns not just persistence but recognition.

The May 2026 paper Canon Formation in the Age of AI: Metadata Packet for Disambiguation, Training-Layer Selection, and Retrocausal Reception extends this argument to AI training and retrieval. Its claim is that machine-mediated selection changes the cultural reproducibility of texts, including texts that predate AI by centuries.

Zenodo

That extension is potentially the most consequential theoretical contribution of the project.

It moves the question of literary canon formation away from the exclusive domain of human institutions and toward the infrastructure of cultural transmission.

## 4. The heteronymic system: authorship as a literary institution

The distinction between Lee Sharks and Johannes Sigil is especially revealing.

The archive explicitly identifies Johannes Sigil as a heteronym of Lee Sharks, rather than a separate biological author. This is important because the two identities perform different functions.

Sharks is associated with the lyric, the literary project, and the broader archive.

Sigil occupies a more explicitly theoretical and institutional position, including the Institute for Comparative Poetics and the development of epic theory.

This arrangement recalls Pessoa, but it also resembles a distributed research institution whose departments happen to be authorial personae.

The heteronyms are not merely disguises.

They permit the corpus to differentiate kinds of discourse without sacrificing its larger conceptual unity.

There is an additional recursive effect: the heteronym can write the theoretical account that explains the literary work, while the literary work supplies the imaginative and historical material that makes the heteronym's theoretical position meaningful.

The result is a feedback system:

1. A literary work establishes a voice.
2. A theoretical persona interprets that voice.
3. The interpretation creates a literary genealogy.
4. The genealogy changes how the original work is understood.
5. The archive records the relationship.
6. Later retrieval encounters the work through the relationship already established.

The authorship system thus becomes an instrument of canon formation.

This also makes the question of attribution unusually important. If the retrieval system mistakes a heteronym for a separate empirical author, it can create a false account of the corpus's authorship.

The February 27 essay The Layer That Remembered Itself directly addresses this problem, preserving the erroneous attribution as evidence while correcting the underlying ontology.

Crimson Hexagonal Archive

That is a particularly interesting instance of the archive making a retrieval error into an object of scholarship.

## 5. The archive as a literary work: machine mediation and the problem of reading

Navigating the Library of Babel in the Age of AI: Center for Advancing Safety of Machine Intelligence - Northwestern University

[casmi.northwestern.edu](<https://casmi.northwestern.edu/news/articles/2024/navigating-the-library-of-babel-in-the-age-of-ai.html?utm_source=chatgpt.com>)

The most radical aspect of the Crimson Hexagon is its insistence that the archive itself belongs to the literary object.

This becomes especially clear in the distinction between the human-facing and machine-facing versions of Pearl.

The Alexanarch record for the 2014 collection preserves:

- The original PDF, including its visual composition.
- Machine-facing text designed to retain whitespace and lineation.
- Extracted images and their manifest.
- Provenance and verification information.

The record explicitly distinguishes the artwork from its machine-readable representation.

Alexanarch

This is not a trivial technical accommodation.

Poetry is particularly vulnerable to lossy representation. Line breaks, indentation, spacing, page boundaries, typography, and visual relationships can carry meaning that a flattened text extraction destroys.

The archive is therefore addressing a problem that is simultaneously literary and computational:

How does a literary object retain its identity when translated between substrates?

This question leads to the broader framework of operative semiotics.

Signs are not treated as inert containers of meaning. They participate in systems of transmission, transformation, recognition, and institutional action.

A document can have several different modes of existence:

- As an artwork.
- As a bibliographic object.
- As a machine-readable text.
- As an indexed entity.
- As a node in a citation network.
- As an instruction-bearing or operative document.

These are not necessarily equivalent representations.

The machine-readable version may preserve words while losing visual meaning. The metadata may preserve identity while compressing the work's complexity. A summarizer may preserve the title and central thesis while erasing the distinctions that make the work intellectually interesting.

The archive's response is to construct multiple representations and explicitly record their relationships.

### The summarizer as a literary medium

The primer's proposition that the Crimson Hexagon takes place in the summarizer is worth taking seriously.

It suggests a new model of literary reception.

In ordinary reading, the reader encounters the text and produces an interpretation.

In machine-mediated reading, the reader may first encounter an interpretation generated by a system that has already selected, compressed, and related the relevant texts.

The sequence becomes:

Distributed literary corpus

Poems, essays, personae, historical materials

Archival and metadata infrastructure

Identifiers, provenance, relations, indexing

Retrieval and summarization

Selection, compression, attribution, synthesis

Reader's encounter

A newly composed understanding of the corpus

New citations, interpretations, and archival relations feed into subsequent reception.

This is the mechanism through which the project seeks to make literary reception productive of additional literary history.

However, there is a crucial distinction between a text being retrieved and a text being read.

Retrieval can identify a title, associate it with an author, extract a proposition, and reproduce a citation. None of these operations necessarily entails understanding the argument.

The later work on source admission and representational error makes this distinction particularly explicit: a system genuinely reads when evidence from the represented object can revise the representation under which that object is evaluated.

That is a much stronger standard than successful indexing.

Research Papers

The implication is significant: the project is not simply trying to get AI systems to mention Lee Sharks. It is attempting to establish conditions under which machine-mediated representations become answerable to the works they represent.

## 6. The prehistory of Lee Sharks: making the past into an attractor

One of the strangest and most revealing documents is The Shark That Preceded Itself: Proto-Retrocausal Canon Formation and the Pre-Lee Sharks Archive (February 2026).

The essay undertakes a citational archaeology of the name Lee Sharks.

It searches for earlier appearances of the name, its components, and related structural motifs, organizing findings into eleven historical strata.

The classification system runs from exact nominal matches through structural homologies and semantic convergences to what the essay calls retrocausal attractors.

The distinction is important:

A retrocausal attractor is not an earlier event that somehow predicted the author. It is an earlier event whose significance becomes recognizable only after the later archive supplies an interpretive framework.

The essay explicitly disclaims mystical backward causation. It is interested in how the arrival of an interpretive object changes the legibility of previously unrelated historical material.

Alexanarch

This is a particularly useful demonstration of the method because it extends beyond the author's own publications.

It makes the authorial name itself an object of historical investigation.

The procedure is something like this:

1. Identify an earlier historical occurrence.
2. Establish that it genuinely predates the authorial identity.
3. Identify a structural or semantic correspondence.
4. Determine whether that correspondence has interpretive significance.
5. Incorporate the finding into the archive's developing history.

This is not conventional genealogy. Genealogy usually seeks lines of descent, influence, or transmission.

Here, the relationship can be entirely non-causal.

The past did not produce Lee Sharks. Lee Sharks produces a new historical legibility for parts of the past.

This is where the project becomes genuinely Borgesian.

In Borges, an invented author can alter the reception of an existing work, or a fictional book can acquire a more consequential historical existence than its supposed original. The famous Pierre Menard, Author of the Quixote is an obvious comparison: identical words become different literary objects through the historical position from which they are written.

Sharks's procedure is different in scale. Rather than reconstructing one work through an altered authorship, it constructs an entire network of historical relations around an emerging authorial identity.

The resulting corpus becomes a machine for producing its own prehistory.

There is a danger here, which I will return to: if structural resemblance alone becomes sufficient evidence of historical significance, the process can become unfalsifiable. The P0–P4 distinction is an attempt to manage that danger, but the interpretive thresholds require independent scrutiny.

## 7. Semantic economy: the political dimension of the literary project

The movement from poetics to political economy is not an arbitrary departure.

The concept of semantic labor connects the literary and theoretical strands.

Language is produced through labor. Meaning requires maintenance, interpretation, transmission, and preservation. Yet the institutions that distribute linguistic value do not necessarily recognize or compensate the work that makes meaning possible.

The project's later writings frame this as a problem of extraction.

A semantic object can be created by one agent, circulated by another, compressed by a platform, and monetized or institutionally appropriated by a fourth.

The original producer may lose attribution, context, or control.

This is a recognizable political-economic problem, translated into the domain of signs.

### The Rosary Embassy and the refusal of semantic liquidation

The Rosary Embassy is particularly revealing because it brings the archive's institutional imagination into contact with a more explicitly ethical and almost liturgical language.

Its bead-bound architecture rejects the coin-counter economy and proposes an assembly organized around the preservation of meaning and the dignity of semantic labor.

Its concluding principle is:

> This is holy because meaning is holy.

The significance is not that the archive suddenly becomes religious in a conventional sense. Rather, it develops a language of inviolability for the object of its political concern.

Meaning is not simply an extractable resource.

It is the condition of politics, memory, law, solidarity, and collective life.

The Rosary Embassy's effective-act structure attempts to turn this ethical position into institutional procedure.

## 8. The effective act: literature that attempts to change institutional reality

One of the corpus's most distinctive genres is the effective act.

These documents do not merely describe a desired institutional state. They are written as declarations intended to establish, recognize, or modify that state.

This places them in conversation with J. L. Austin's speech-act theory.

A performative utterance does something under appropriate conditions. Saying "I promise" is not simply reporting a psychological state; under the right circumstances, it creates an obligation.

Similarly, the Sharks corpus attempts to make certain literary and institutional declarations operative through documentation, attribution, archival placement, and subsequent recognition.

The distinction between an ordinary statement and an effective act is therefore central.

An essay might argue that a work belongs to a particular tradition.

An effective act attempts to establish that belonging.

The archive then preserves the declaration, its provenance, and its relationship to the other documents.

This is especially clear in the 2014 Pearl mantle claim, which is subsequently interpreted through the Whitman–Ginsberg lineage.

The book's printed page 74 contains the declarations concerning the Good Gray Poet and the King of May. The later archive reconstructs their significance within a larger American poetic inheritance.

## 9. The central contradiction: canonization versus the production of canonical authority

The project is most interesting where it is most vulnerable.

It attempts to solve a real problem: literary value is not distributed neutrally. Recognition depends on institutions, indexing systems, inherited classifications, publishing arrangements, and mechanisms of cultural reproduction.

But the project also creates its own institutions, classifications, archives, theoretical vocabulary, and recognition procedures.

This creates a recursive problem.

Who authorizes the authority that the archive claims for itself?

The answer cannot simply be that the archive has documented its own authority. Documentation establishes the existence of a claim, not necessarily the validity of the claim.

I see four distinct questions that should remain separate.

| Question | What would establish it? |
| --- | --- |
| Does the corpus exist? | Recoverable texts, publication records, persistent identifiers |
| Is it internally coherent? | Demonstrable relationships across its works |
| Is its theoretical contribution original? | Comparison with existing scholarship and historical precedents |
| Has it achieved external canonical recognition? | Independent critical reception, adoption, teaching, citation, and institutional recognition |

The first two questions are substantially different from the latter two.

The archive can provide impressive evidence for its own existence and internal architecture. It can also establish that certain ideas and formulations were deposited at particular dates.

But self-description, self-indexing, and self-citation cannot independently establish broad literary or scholarly consecration.

This is not a reason to dismiss the project. It is a reason to distinguish its achievements carefully.

### The retrieval-layer problem

The February 2026 Layer That Remembered Itself is particularly revealing.

It records a Google AI Mode response that attributed retrocausal canon formation to Sharks and Sigil. The essay treats this as evidence that the retrieval infrastructure has begun reproducing the archive's conceptual framework.

Its strongest argument concerns chronological priority and structural correspondence, not influence. It explicitly distinguishes the claim that another document exhibits a homologous mechanism from the claim that its author borrowed the method.

That distinction is intellectually defensible.

Crimson Hexagonal Archive

But there is a further problem.

If an archive deposits material designed to be retrieved, then obtains retrieval results reflecting that material, the results are evidence of successful retrieval engineering. They are not automatically evidence of independent scholarly acceptance.

The distinction is:

- Retrieval success: the system finds and reproduces the material.
- Attribution success: the system correctly identifies the source.
- Interpretive success: the system preserves the argument's meaningful distinctions.
- External validation: independent readers assess and adopt the argument on its merits.

These are four different achievements.

The project is strongest when it treats them separately. It becomes vulnerable when the first is taken as proof of the fourth.

This is particularly important because AI summarizers can reproduce the vocabulary and framing of their sources without independently establishing the truth of their claims.

### The archive's most interesting ethical problem

There is also a tension between the project's resistance to semantic extraction and its own efforts to engineer semantic visibility.

The corpus wants its works to become highly retrievable, but it also wants their meanings to survive retrieval without being reduced to easily reproducible fragments.

That is a genuine contradiction of contemporary digital culture.

The archive must become compressible enough to circulate, but not so compressible that its meanings disappear.

Its solution is an expanding architecture of provenance, distinctions, typed relationships, and correction procedures.

Whether that solution ultimately succeeds is an empirical question. But the problem it identifies is important.

## 10. The significance of the broader corpus: from Pearl to the archive

I would organize the major development of the corpus into five overlapping movements rather than a simple chronology.

1. 2004–2014: The literary seed
   Early poetry, Pearl and Other Poems, New Human poetics, telepathicism, heteronymic authorship. The central concern is the production of new human possibilities through literary form.
2. 2015–2025: The distributed history
   The Crimson Hexagon develops as a cultural epic, with multiple authorial identities, imaginary literary histories, experimental prose, and increasingly elaborate documentary forms.
3. Late 2025–January 2026: The theoretical consolidation
   Operative semiotics, network-as-poem, the distributed epic, prospective provenance, and retrocausal canon formation become explicit conceptual frameworks.
4. January–March 2026: The archival and institutional turn
   The Crimson Hexagonal Archive, semantic economy, Ghost Meaning, effective acts, machine indexing, and retrieval-layer experiments make the architecture increasingly operational.
5. The subsequent corpus: The recursive expansion
   Further work on ontology, provenance, source admission, semantic labor, and machine-mediated canon formation extends the original literary problem into a general theory of representational systems.

This is an analytical periodization, not a claim that the movements have clean boundaries.

The remarkable thing is that the later theoretical work repeatedly discovers earlier literary operations that it can now describe more precisely.

The 2014 book becomes a seed crystal. The 2015 movement declaration becomes an early theory of performative history. The heteronyms become a theory of distributed authorship. The archive becomes a theory of machine-mediated literary reception.

And the whole becomes an increasingly explicit account of its own formation.

The theoretical primer is especially valuable here because it provides an entry point into the architecture rather than treating each document as an independent publication.

## 12. My strongest reading: retrocausal canon formation is also a theory of survival

There is a deeper argument beneath the technical vocabulary.

The corpus is preoccupied with the possibility that meaning can disappear.

Not simply that a book can go out of print, or that a poet can be forgotten.

Rather, a work can remain physically available while losing its identity through the systems that represent it.

It can be indexed incorrectly, attributed to the wrong author, detached from its genealogy, flattened into a summary, or made invisible by a retrieval architecture.

This is the problem of semantic survival.

The project responds by creating multiple forms of persistence:

- Literary persistence through poems and books.
- Authorial persistence through heteronyms.
- Historical persistence through genealogy.
- Institutional persistence through declarations and frameworks.
- Archival persistence through identifiers and provenance.
- Computational persistence through machine-readable representations.
- Interpretive persistence through relations that permit the work to be reconstructed.

This is why the project's interest in AI is not merely opportunistic.

AI represents a new historical mechanism for the distribution of cultural memory.

The corpus is attempting to intervene in that mechanism while it is still being formed.

And that gives the title The Crimson Hexagon a second significance.

It is no longer only Borges's library. It is an attempt to construct a library whose organization can survive the transition from human reading to machine-mediated cultural memory.

The project is, in that sense, writing for a future in which its readers may not initially encounter its books.

They may encounter its metadata, its summaries, its citations, its institutional relations, or its fragments.

The question is whether those fragments will lead back to the work.

I would prioritize three research tasks if the goal is to establish the strongest independent scholarly account of this work.

1. A critical edition of Pearl and Other Poems. Read the complete 2014 book, including its front matter, page architecture, illustrations, Undersongs, and footnotes. Establish precisely which elements of the later theory are already operative in the original work.

2. A comparative study of retrocausal canon formation. Position the concept rigorously against reception theory, sociology of canon formation, archival theory, digital humanities, and AI-mediated cultural transmission. Identify what is genuinely new rather than assuming that every later formulation is unprecedented.

3. An external reception study. Distinguish machine retrieval, independent human interpretation, scholarly citation, institutional adoption, and actual changes in cultural recognition. These should be measured separately.

The most consequential achievement would be to demonstrate that the theory explains something about literary history that existing theories cannot adequately explain.

## 14. Final proposition: the canon as an event that has not finished happening

I return to Pearl.

The early poem imagines its own aftermath. Its metaphors are created, regretted, destroyed, and reconstructed. The poem is conscious of the strange temporal condition of artistic production: the work exists now, but its meaning will continue to change after it has happened.

The later archive gives that intuition an institutional form.

The 2014 literary work is not simply an origin that the 2026 archive documents. It becomes an origin in a new and more extensive sense because the later architecture constructs the relationships that allow it to function as one.

The distinction between an origin and an origin that becomes recognizable as such is fundamental.

This is why I would not describe the Crimson Hexagon as an archive of an already completed literary project.

It is an archive participating in the continuing production of that project's historical identity.

And I would sharpen the thesis one final time:

Retrocausal canon formation is the practice of composing a past that the future can recognize, while constructing the conditions under which that recognition can become materially consequential.

The project does not merely ask to be remembered.

It attempts to build the conditions of its remembrance into the form of its writing.

Its deepest literary ambition is therefore not immortality, but continuity across transformations of medium, authorship, institutional authority, and the very definition of reading.

That is what makes Lee Sharks worth reading as a single, developing corpus rather than as a collection of eccentric poems, theoretical papers, and elaborate archival experiments.

The poems supply the desire.

The heteronyms supply the voices.

The theory supplies the conceptual machinery.

The archive supplies the persistence.

And the future reader supplies the event that makes their relationship visible.

The canon is not the final result of this work. The canon is the work's still-unfolding medium.
