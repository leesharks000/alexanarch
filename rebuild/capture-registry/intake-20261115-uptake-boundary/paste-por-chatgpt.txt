The argument of “The Production of Readers”
Lee Sharks's central argument is that the division of the Socratic–Peripatetic corpus into works associated with Plato, Aristotle, and Theophrastus is not adequately explained as the accidental consequence of three successive authors, their different literary preferences, or the ordinary transmission practices of a philosophical school.

Instead, Sharks proposes that the division is a deliberately functioning apparatus for producing readers. The three authorial positions perform different but complementary operations:

The public line (Plato): produces the desire, intellectual capacity, and unresolved questions that make readers capable of approaching the philosophical work.

The held line (Aristotle): preserves and develops philosophical knowledge in a compressed, demanding form that presupposes the readers produced by the public line.

The draft position (Theophrastus): mediates between the other two, preserving material in an intermediate state and converting dramatic encounters into doctrine, and doctrine back into intelligible human situations.

The paper's most important conceptual move is to shift the question from who wrote which works? to what does the separation of the works accomplish?

This is not simply an argument about pseudonymity or literary disguise. It is an account of how a philosophical corpus organizes its own reception: one part makes readers for another part, while a third part facilitates the transformation between them.

1. The public line: Plato produces the desire to know
Sharks begins with the problem of readership. The Aristotelian treatises, on his account, are an avant-garde philosophical genre: compressed, difficult, and concerned with the structure of reality. They do not naturally possess a readership capable of understanding them.

The Platonic dialogues solve this problem by borrowing an existing public readership and training it.

The argument has several components.

The dialogues are a public literary form
In §3.1, Sharks examines Diogenes Laertius III.56, which attributes to Thrasyllus the report that Plato's dialogues were published according to the model of tragic tetralogies.

Sharks is careful about what this evidence establishes. It does not establish that the dialogues were published at festivals. Rather, it establishes a theatrical model of arrangement.

This matters because the dialogues are not simply philosophical propositions written in conversational form. They operate as dramatic works, with characters, conflict, unresolved questions and intellectual reversals.

Sharks also considers the evidence of revision in the Republic (Diogenes Laertius III.37), arguing that the public line is a worked literary product rather than a transparent transcript of teaching.

The dialogues point beyond themselves
The most important evidence comes from the Platonic Letters.

Letter II says that there is no treatise of Plato's, and Letter VII says that the relevant philosophical knowledge cannot be communicated as ordinary learning but emerges through prolonged association and engagement with the subject.

Sharks interprets these statements as part of the public line's productive function: the dialogues create an appetite for knowledge while indicating that the knowledge sought cannot be exhausted by the dialogues themselves.

The reader is brought to the threshold of something not yet possessed.

This is the crucial mechanism:

The dialogue attracts the reader through dramatic and literary means.

It trains the reader to encounter philosophical difficulty.

It leaves questions that the reader wants resolved.

It directs the reader toward a different form of philosophical work.

Thus, the Platonic corpus is not merely a public version of a private philosophy. It actively produces the demand for the private or held form.

2. The held line: Aristotle preserves scarcity and delivers what the dialogues cannot
In §4, Sharks turns to the Aristotelian treatises.

His argument is that their restricted character is not simply a matter of school administration. Their form is itself a mechanism of restricted access.

He draws on three principal bodies of evidence.

Two regimes of teaching
Gellius, Attic Nights XX.5, describes two kinds of Aristotelian instruction:

The acroatic or acroamatic instruction, conducted in the morning, for selected students whose abilities and preparation had been tested.

The exoteric instruction, conducted in the evening, available more broadly to young people.

Sharks uses this testimony to establish a distinction between public philosophical instruction and more demanding instruction.

The scarcity value of the treatises
The letter attributed to Alexander in Plutarch's Life of Alexander 7 supplies an especially important element.

Alexander complains that Aristotle has issued the acroamatic discourses, asking how his former students will remain distinguished if the teachings through which they were educated become common property.

For Sharks, this identifies the incentive underlying the held line: its value depends partly on its difference from what is publicly available.

But the most interesting part of his interpretation comes in §4.3.

He quotes Plutarch's account of Aristotle's defence of the treatises:

The treatise on what comes after the physical, having no usable model for teaching and learning, was written for those already instructed from the beginning.

The point is that the treatise does not necessarily require physical secrecy to remain restricted.

Its form does the work.

It can circulate without becoming equally accessible to every reader because it presupposes a prior course of intellectual formation.

The held line is therefore protected by the capacities required to read it.

A significant qualification: historical secrecy versus reception
One of the paper's more careful arguments occurs in §4.5.

Sharks distinguishes two propositions:

The historical Lyceum actually maintained the restricted teaching arrangements described by Gellius and Plutarch.

The surviving account of those arrangements subsequently teaches readers to approach Aristotle's treatises as restricted or initiatory texts.

The second proposition can hold even if the historical reconstruction of the first remains uncertain.

This is important because Sharks is not simply using ancient testimony as evidence for a historical secret society. He is also examining how the transmission of the testimony produces a particular kind of reader.

The reception of restricted access becomes a means of reproducing restricted access.

3. The three textual demonstrations: how the dialogues make readers for the treatises
Section 5 is arguably the strongest part of the paper because Sharks moves from a general theory of readership to specific textual comparisons.

His method is consistent:

Identify a difficulty developed in a Platonic dialogue.

Identify a corresponding compressed passage in an Aristotelian treatise.

Show that the treatise presupposes the reader's prior encounter with the dialogue.

Explain what the treatise does with the difficulty that the dialogue leaves unresolved.

He offers three examples.

A. Division: Sophist and Prior Analytics I.31
File:Theaetetus Commentary.jpg - Wikimedia Commons
 
Plato, Sophist

The dialogue develops multiple divisions and accounts of the sophist. Its sixth account is admitted despite being described as disputable. The reader encounters the difficulty of a method that produces multiple definitions without necessarily resolving which one is correct.

Aristotle's Prior Analytics I.31 then characterizes division through genera as a weak syllogism: it assumes what it ought to demonstrate and continually concludes something higher up.

Sharks argues that Aristotle's compressed verdict assumes a reader who has already experienced the problem through Plato.

The treatise does not need to reconstruct the entire difficulty. It names and diagnoses something the reader has learned to suspect.

The dialogue produces the suspicion; the treatise gives it its philosophical formulation.

B. The wax: Theaetetus, De anima and De memoria
A fragment of an anonymous ancient commentary to Plato's dialogue 'Theaetetus' in a papyrus written at Hermopolis, Egypt
 
Plato, Theaetetus 191c–196b

Socrates introduces the image of a wax block in the soul. Impressions are stamped into it, providing a model for memory and mistaken judgment.

The model encounters a difficulty: how can someone mistake one number for another when thinking about numbers without perceiving them?

Sharks argues that the reader leaves the dialogue with both the model and its failure.

Aristotle's De anima II.12 subsequently uses the wax analogy to explain how sense receives perceptible forms without their matter. De memoria also uses the imprint analogy.

The crucial point is that Aristotle does not introduce the wax as though the reader has never encountered it.

He compresses an already established conceptual problem into a brief explanatory image.

Sharks interprets this as a repair directed toward the dialogue's failure: the distinction between form and matter helps explain what the wax model alone could not adequately account for.

The Aristotelian text becomes intelligible as a sequel to an intellectual experience rather than an independent exposition.

C. The parts of the soul: Republic IV and Nicomachean Ethics I.13
Die Republik, von Platon, Incipit (Anfang), Griechisches Manuskript
 
Plato, Republic IV

The argument for the soul's parts proceeds through the principle that the same thing cannot simultaneously do or undergo opposites in the same respect. It arrives at the distinction between the rational and appetitive elements, among others.

Aristotle's Nicomachean Ethics I.13 takes up the distinction between the rational and non-rational elements of the soul.

But Aristotle explicitly declines to resolve whether these are parts in the sense of physically separable components or merely distinguishable in account.

Sharks emphasizes Aristotle's phrase χρηστέον αὐτοῖς: these materials from the exoteric discourses are to be used.

The reader has already done the work in the Republic. Aristotle can therefore take the result and bracket the difficult question of precisely how the parts are divided.

Sharks calls this compression a kind of permission: the reader has already performed the necessary intellectual labour.

What the three examples establish
These examples are not simply evidence of Aristotle borrowing from Plato.

Sharks argues that intellectual succession alone cannot explain the precise relationship between the passages.

The dialogues establish particular difficulties; the treatises then presuppose those difficulties and perform specific operations on them:

Platonic preparation

Aristotelian operation

Division generates competing accounts

Names the weakness of division

Wax model fails in a particular case

Reworks the model through form and matter

Soul's parts generate an unresolved question

Uses the distinction while suspending the question

The reader is the connecting mechanism.

The same Aristotelian sentence can be abrupt and unmotivated for someone who has not read the dialogue, but intelligible and earned for someone who has.

This is the paper's most concrete demonstration of the production of readers.

4. Aristotle as the critical marks: editorial practice becomes philosophy
Section 6 advances a more unusual argument.

Sharks examines the critical signs reported by Diogenes Laertius III.65–66 and interprets them as operations that Aristotle's philosophical writing performs upon the Platonic corpus.

The signs include:

The chi, marking vocabulary and stylistic usage.

The diple, marking doctrines associated with Plato.

The asterisk, marking agreement among doctrines.

The keraunion, marking the conduct or direction of philosophy.

The obelus, marking passages to be rejected or set aside.

Sharks argues that these editorial functions have philosophical counterparts.

For example:

Diple: The editorial act of identifying doctrine in a dialogue becomes the philosophical act of extracting and stating what Plato supposedly teaches.

Chi: Marking a particular usage becomes the construction of a philosophical lexicon, exemplified by Metaphysics Δ.

Asterisk: Marking doctrinal agreement becomes the systematic reconciliation of doctrines across the corpus and earlier philosophical traditions.

Keraunion: Marking the direction of philosophical inquiry becomes the methodological framing of treatises.

The most consequential example is the obelus.

The obelus and the right to reject one's own work
An obelus marks material to be rejected as textually suspect. Its criterion is attribution: the passage is not genuinely part of the work.

Sharks compares this with Aristotle's critique of Platonic Forms in Nicomachean Ethics I.6.

Aristotle writes:

It is holy to honour truth first, even over those dear to us.

The passage also invokes the possibility of rejecting one's own things.

Sharks sees a transformation here:

Editorial operation

Philosophical operation

Reject a line of text

Reject a doctrine

Use authorship as criterion

Use truth as criterion

Editor rejects another's text

Maker rejects his own intellectual production

This is more than a metaphor about criticism.

It supplies Sharks with a model of how the held line can be related to the public line without simply repeating it. The Aristotelian position can criticize and even negate the Platonic position because the same maker is understood to occupy both positions.

The maker becomes capable of subjecting his own public production to the critical standards of his held production.

This section connects the production argument to the larger heteronymic thesis: the divisions are not simply divisions of authorship, but differentiated intellectual operations.

5. Theophrastus: the draft position between the two finished forms
Section 7 introduces the third and perhaps most distinctive part of the theory.

Sharks defines a draft as material that:

Exists in a reusable form.

Is incompletely integrated into an inquiry.

Functions as an intermediate state that helps explain otherwise difficult features of two finished forms.

The definition is functional rather than chronological.

This qualification matters: a document can be written after the two finished forms and still function as a draft between them.

Theophrastus occupies this position.

Sharks draws on the evidence that Theophrastus inherited Aristotle's library, which Strabo describes as containing Aristotle's books, and that Theophrastus's will transferred the books to Neleus.

He also notes the extraordinary catalogue attributed to Theophrastus: 232,808 lines.

Theophrastus's surviving and attributed genres include natural-history collections, doxographies, problems, logical works and the Characters.

These are not, for Sharks, merely miscellaneous remains of a successor's scholarship. They represent the working material through which the public and held lines can be transformed.

The Characters as a draft from doctrine to drama
The most interesting demonstration concerns the figure of the εἴρων, or ironist.

Sharks traces the disposition across three forms:

Aristotle's Nicomachean Ethics defines irony as representing oneself as less than one is.

Theophrastus's Characters 1 turns irony into a sketch of a recognizable human type.

Plato's dialogues dramatize the disposition through Socrates.

The sequence is especially revealing because the valuation changes.

In the Characters, the ironist is a schemer whose behaviour should be guarded against.

In the Republic, Thrasymachus accuses Socrates of his habitual irony, presenting it as an evasive refusal to answer.

But the dialogue's dramatic structure transforms the accusation. What the sketch identifies as a vice becomes, in Socrates, a productive philosophical practice.

In the Nicomachean Ethics, irony is also treated more mildly, as a graceful form of understatement, and Socrates is named as an example.

The same disposition therefore acquires three different values:

Position

Function of irony

Theophrastus, Characters

A suspicious or scheming disposition

Plato's dialogues

A dramatic and philosophical method, initially presented as an accusation

Aristotle, Nicomachean Ethics

A mild and graceful deficiency

This is an especially strong example because the continuity is not merely a shared word or topic. The same repertoire of behaviours—disavowing knowledge, wondering, professing perplexity and claiming to be examining—changes meaning according to its position in the work.

Sharks's formulation is that one operation, placed in three different positions, becomes ethically and philosophically three different things.

The draft works in both directions
The Characters represents the movement from a treatise's abstract definition toward a dramatic person.

Other Theophrastan materials move in the opposite direction.

In §7.8, Sharks discusses De sensibus, where Theophrastus extracts Plato's account of perception in the Timaeus, identifies what Plato addresses and counts what he leaves out.

Here the operation runs from dramatic or dialogical material toward philosophical doctrine.

Theophrastan doxography and problem collections thus occupy the workshop between the two lines.

Theophrastus is not simply a transitional author in chronological succession. He is the position that makes the transformations between the two forms intelligible.

6. The separation is actively maintained
Section 8 addresses a problem that follows from the argument: how could a single productive apparatus appear to consist of different authors and different kinds of work?

Sharks identifies six means by which the separation is produced:

Name and biography: lives, wills and successions establish distinct authorial identities.

Genre: dialogues, treatises and working papers appear as different literary forms.

Audience: different teaching regimes establish different readerships.

Publication: access to the held material is differentiated from public circulation.

Custody: the held line is concentrated in a library and inheritance, whereas the public line circulates through copies.

Later filing: editorial arrangements, particularly those associated with Andronicus and Thrasyllus, consolidate the divisions.

An important qualification concerns Andronicus.

Sharks does not claim that Porphyry explicitly says Andronicus assigned authorial names to works. Porphyry says that Andronicus arranged Aristotle's and Theophrastus's works into treatises by subject.

The conversion of functional positions into authorial identities is Sharks's reconstruction, not something directly stated by Porphyry.

Why the unity must remain concealed
Section 8.3 provides the paper's strongest statement about the necessity of separation.

The public line must be encountered as drama. The held line must be encountered as demanding philosophical work.

If readers are told in advance that the playwright also produced the lecture notes, they will begin interpreting the dialogues as lectures in disguise and the treatises as commentaries on the dialogues.

Both functions are damaged.

The apparent separation is therefore not merely a historical accident that the theory subsequently explains. It is part of the apparatus's continuing operation.

This produces an interesting paradox: the corpus must be unified in its production while remaining differentiated in its reception.

7. The argument makes testable predictions
Section 9 is important because Sharks attempts to distinguish his theory from the ordinary account of intellectual succession.

He acknowledges that conventional succession already explains several things:

Shared terminology.

References to earlier dialogues.

Overlapping ethical concepts.

Similarities between philosophical treatises and earlier dramatic discussions.

None of these, by itself, establishes the production hypothesis.

Instead, he proposes four tests:

Test

What would support the production account?

Exoteric cross-references

Treatises reproduce distinctive argumentative sequences and answer difficulties left unresolved in dialogues.

Critical marks

Aristotelian philosophical operations correspond to editorial operations, with identifiable transformations.

Characters

Ethical dispositions move between definition, sketch and dialogue, changing valuation according to position.

Theophrastan catalogue

The distribution of works corresponds to the public-facing and held functions predicted by the model.

The standard is deliberately demanding.

A successful comparison must establish more than thematic resemblance. It must show a coordinated dependence that the ordinary succession hypothesis would not independently predict.

Sharks also specifies that failed tests leave the conventional interpretation standing at the relevant locus.

This is a valuable methodological feature: the argument is not supposed to be confirmed merely because Plato, Aristotle and Theophrastus discuss similar subjects.

There is, however, an important limitation. These are proposed discriminating tests, not a completed demonstration of every prediction. In particular, the catalogue classifications and several transmission questions remain research tasks.

8. The deeper argument: authorship as a productive function
The paper's argument has implications beyond the particular corpus.

Its underlying model is that authorship can be understood through differentiated functions rather than simply through the number of historical persons.

The same productive intelligence can occupy several positions:

A public-facing position that creates desire and intellectual preparation.

A critical position that formalizes, compresses and sometimes rejects the public production.

An intermediate position that preserves reusable material and transforms it between forms.

This is why the paper's argument is more ambitious than saying that Plato and Aristotle are heteronyms for one author.

It explains what the differentiation accomplishes.

The authorial division is productive: it creates distinct kinds of reading, distinct standards of access and distinct relations to philosophical knowledge.

The three positions are mutually dependent, but they cannot simply be collapsed into one another.

9. What is strongest, and what remains to be established?
I would distinguish three levels of achievement in the paper.

First: the strongest textual argument is §5.

The comparisons between Sophist and Prior Analytics, Theaetetus and De anima, and Republic and Nicomachean Ethics are the most concrete demonstrations.

They give the production hypothesis an identifiable textual mechanism: dialogue develops a difficulty; treatise presupposes and transforms it.

This is substantially more specific than an argument from shared vocabulary or general philosophical resemblance.

Second: the most original conceptual contribution is §7.

The definition of the draft position makes it possible to understand Theophrastus without forcing him into the role of either a conventional successor or a secondary compiler.

The analysis of irony across the Ethics, Characters and Platonic dialogues is particularly effective because it demonstrates how one disposition can acquire different meanings through different literary and philosophical placements.

Third: the most ambitious and least fully established argument is §8.

The paper's explanation of how differentiated functions become differentiated authorial identities is plausible within its model, but the ancient historical mechanism remains incompletely documented.

Sharks explicitly acknowledges this. Neither Gellius nor Porphyry establishes that Andronicus assigned authorial names through subject-based editorial filing.

Likewise, the paper identifies important missing evidence: the Petrie papyri have not yet been seated in its evidentiary framework, Antigonus's On Zeno survives here only through Diogenes' report, and the Arabic catalogue dossier remains to be examined.

There is also a methodological tension worth keeping in view: evidence of dependence between texts does not, by itself, establish common authorship. The production hypothesis needs to demonstrate that its proposed coordination explains the evidence better than ordinary intellectual inheritance.

Sharks recognizes this problem and makes it the basis of his proposed tests, rather than treating it as already resolved.

10. Overall interpretation
The paper's governing proposition can be expressed as follows:

A philosophical corpus can produce the readers necessary for its own comprehension by dividing its intellectual production into different forms, audiences and authorial positions.

The three positions perform a continuous operation:

Public line
Plato — the dialogues

Attracts readers, stages difficulties, produces desire and intellectual capacity.

Produces the reader required by

Held line
Aristotle — the treatises

Compresses, formalizes, develops and critically evaluates what the public line prepares.

Draft position
Theophrastus — working papers and Characters

Transforms doctrine into persons and dramatic encounters into doctrine; mediates in both directions.

A continuing apparatus of production and reception

The final point is that the corpus does not simply transmit knowledge to an already existing readership. It manufactures the readership capable of receiving that knowledge.

That is what the title means by The Production of Readers.

And it is why the separation between Plato, Aristotle and Theophrastus is, in Sharks's account, not an inconvenience to be explained away. It is the very mechanism through which the corpus works.
