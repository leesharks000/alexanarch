       The Josephus Thesis—developed by researcher Lee Sharks—is a structural-literary theory proposing that the New Testament authors can be read as heteronymic author-functions within the literary system of the first-century Romano-Jewish historian Flavius Josephus (born Yosef ben Matityahu).
In this framework, "The Custody of the Name" refers to the central political and literary constraint that drove this distributed authorship: after his capture during the First Jewish–Roman War (66–73 CE) and subsequent emancipation under Emperor Vespasian, Josephus's official name and public identity belonged to Rome (taking the imperial family name Flavius).

Core Concepts of the Thesis

• Heteronymic Authorship. Drawing on the literary theory of Fernando Pessoa, the thesis treats figures like Paul, John, Peter, James, Luke, Mark, and Matthew not as independent historical writers or simple pseudonyms, but as fully realized authorial voices—each with a distinct style, theology, and worldview.
• The Custody of the Name. Because Josephus operated as a Roman client and strategic translator of Judaean culture for the Flavian dynasty, he could not speak freely under his own name. Rome held custody over his official persona, forcing the literary intelligence of a displaced Judaean priestly class to fracture into multiple distinct voices to preserve and transmit meaning across two incompatible systems (Judaean priesthood and Roman imperial power).
• Structural Fracturing vs. Conspiracy. Developed within the Crimson Hexagonal Archive / Semantic Economy Institute, the thesis explicitly distinguishes itself from conspiracy theories like the Piso Hypothesis. Rather than claiming a Roman family fabricated Christianity to deceive the masses, it frames the New Testament's authorial diversity as an "available reading"—a structural response by a scribal workshop under imperial pressure after the destruction of the Second Temple in 70 CE.
