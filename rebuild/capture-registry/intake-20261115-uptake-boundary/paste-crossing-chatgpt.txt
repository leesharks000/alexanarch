The arguments of Lee Sharks's The Crossing of Names
Lee Sharks, deposit #1762, AXN:0C74, version 1.0, 12 November 2026

The central argument is that names in ancient literature do not merely identify people: they are instruments through which texts construct persons, transfer authority, preserve earlier writings, and organize relations between historical and allegorical identities.

Sharks proposes that this activity is systematic enough to help bind together an ancient literary field that conventional scholarship tends to divide into separate authors, works, historical periods, and traditions.

The essay is not simply an argument that ancient writers used pseudonyms, or that historical characters sometimes represent allegorical figures. It proposes a more extensive literary mechanism: a name can remain recognizable while changing its function, referent, or level of reality. The reader is made to negotiate those changes without necessarily being permitted to resolve them into one simple identification.

There are three principal operations in the argument:

Many-namedness (πολυώνυμος): several names or designations gather around one referent.

Transfer or restamping (μεταχαράττειν): a designation, identity, or authority is transferred to a changed referent.

Differentiated personation: distinct named positions are constructed with different capacities and consequences, even when their relations suggest a deeper unity.

These distinctions are important. Sharks does not treat every instance of multiple naming as the same phenomenon. He wants to explain how names gather around figures, how they move between figures, and how the resulting figures become sufficiently differentiated to act within a literary composition.

I read the argument as having four interconnected levels: a theory of naming, a set of close readings, a proposal about the construction of literary history, and a methodological argument about how to establish textual relationships without prematurely turning them into historical identities.

1. Lesbia: a literary name is not merely an alias
The first major case study is Catullus's Lesbia.

Sharks begins with Apuleius, Apology 10, which lists substitutions in Roman love poetry:

Lesbia for Clodia.

Perilla for Metella.

Cynthia for Hostia.

Delia for Plania.

This is important because the ancient reception already treats poetic names as potentially decodable. But Sharks refuses to regard decoding as the end of interpretation.

His central example is Catullus 51, the Latin rewriting of Sappho 31.

Sappho begins:

φαίνεταί μοι κῆνος ἴσος θεοῖσιν

Catullus transforms this into:

Ille mi par esse deo videtur,
ille, si fas est, superare divos.

The name Lesbia then appears in line 7, and the poem concludes by addressing Catullus himself.

Sharks identifies three positions operating simultaneously:

The inherited Sapphic poetic situation.

Lesbia, the newly named beloved.

Catullus, the speaking self who becomes an object of address within the poem.

Lesbia is therefore not simply a pseudonym concealing Clodia. Her name also evokes Lesbos and the poetic tradition of Sappho. It carries a literary relationship that would be lost if the name were simply replaced with a biographical identification.

This leads to a significant methodological argument: a historical identification can be correct and still be an inadequate interpretation of the literary construction.

Sharks reinforces this through Catullus 16, where the speaker explicitly disputes the inference that the character of his poetry establishes the character of the poet. Catullus 79 supplies another example: Lesbia, Lesbius and pulcer create a network of personal insinuation through names and cognomina.

The argument extends beyond Lesbia herself:

Catullus 63 makes Attis's transformation visible through changes in grammatical gender and bodily identity.

Catullus 66 gives Berenice's severed lock a speaking voice.

Catullus 76 and 109 bring erotic fidelity into the language of sacred obligation and covenant.

These passages suggest that naming, embodiment, transformation and religious language belong to a connected literary problem.

Gellius as a witness to the crossing
One of the essay's more interesting readings concerns Aulus Gellius, Attic Nights 19.9 and 19.11.

In 19.9, a narrated banquet brings together:

Sapphic and Anacreontic performance.

Discussion of Catullus.

A Socratic gesture in which Julianus covers his head before performing.

A Latin poem by Valerius Aedituus that reproduces the symptoms of Sappho 31.

Sharks is careful not to collapse this scene with the Plato epigram in 19.11. Instead, he finds a connecting structure across the intervening chapter.

The Greeks permit the Latins only a few poems by Catullus. Julianus responds by mentioning earlier poets, including Quintus Catulus, whose name resembles Catullus's.

Catulus's poem describes the soul leaving its lover and going to Theotimus. Two chapters later, the epigram attributed to Plato describes a lover holding his soul at his lips while kissing Agathon. Gellius's Latin adaptation concludes with the lover dying to himself and living inside the beloved.

Sharks's point is that names and textual relations cross within Gellius's own arrangement, even though the relevant passages are not presented as one continuous scene.

This is a good example of his preferred method: identify the exact words, preserve the separation between passages, and then establish the relationship through a specific verbal and conceptual hinge.

2. Clodia: naming is an exercise of political authority
The second major case study moves from poetic naming to forensic rhetoric and political memory.

In Cicero's Pro Caelio 33–36, Cicero stages the appearance of an ancestral Claudian figure to condemn Clodia. He then introduces the voice of Clodius.

Sharks emphasizes that Cicero openly controls these performances.

The ancestor's authority is not simply remembered. It is made present through a speech composed by Clodia's opponent.

This is differentiated personation: Cicero creates distinct speaking positions and uses their apparent independence to strengthen his own argument.

The ancestor is not required to have literally spoken these words. The relevant textual fact is that Cicero makes the ancestor speak, and that this performance exercises the authority of the family against a living member of it.

Cicero's statue of Liberty
The argument becomes especially concrete in De Domo Sua 110–112.

Cicero contests the sacred identity of a statue installed at the site of his demolished house. His opponent identifies it as Liberty. Cicero alleges that it was actually a statue of a courtesan originally associated with a tomb at Tanagra.

The competing names are:

Liberty, which gives the statue political and sacred authority.

Courtesan, which discredits that authority and challenges the statue's installation.

Sharks connects this to Philo's term μεταχαράττειν, restamping.

The same object is assigned competing identities. Its designation determines how its history, location and authority should be understood.

This is one of the clearest examples of the essay's argument that naming is not merely descriptive. It can be an instrument of political action.

The parallel with Josephus's account of Berenice is also important. Antiquities 20.145–146 discusses allegations concerning her sexual relationship with her brother and her marriage to Polemon. Sharks draws attention to the language of reported accusation, particularly φήμη and ὡς ἔφασαν.

He compares this with Cicero's management of accusations against Clodia.

The comparison does not require the two women to be the same person. The common subject is the literary and political construction of women's public identities through sexual allegations, kinship and wealth.

3. Philo: the philosophical foundation of the theory
Philo is arguably the essay's most important theoretical interlocutor.

Sharks takes two Greek terms from Philo and makes them central to his vocabulary.

πολυώνυμος — many-named

In De mutatione nominum 125, Philo describes Moses as πολυώνυμος.

In De confusione linguarum 146, he applies the same designation to the Logos.

The Logos receives multiple designations, including:

Beginning.

Name of God.

Word.

The human being according to the image.

The one who sees.

Israel.

Sharks treats this shared terminology as significant. Moses and the Logos are both represented through a plurality of names and functions.

μεταχαράττειν — restamping

The key example occurs in De mutatione nominum 121–123.

Philo interprets Moses's renaming of Hosea as Joshua as a transformation from a merely potential quality into an achieved condition.

The name changes because the person is understood to have changed.

Sharks's interpretation is that a name can designate an achieved state of being, rather than merely identify an individual who remains ontologically unchanged.

This produces a stronger theory than ordinary pseudonymy. The name is not simply a second label for the same person. It expresses a transformation in the person's condition and function.

Genealogy as spiritual and literary succession
Sharks extends this reading through De Cherubim 40–52 and De confusione linguarum 146–149.

In De Cherubim 49, Philo describes his initiation by Moses and subsequent discipleship to Jeremiah.

The chronological separation between these figures is not the governing issue in Philo's account. They become present as teachers through initiation and intellectual succession.

Similarly, Philo's treatment of Davidic descendants allows genealogy to be understood through the generation of virtuous souls rather than exclusively through biological descent.

This yields an important proposition:

A genealogy can describe an achieved spiritual, intellectual or literary relationship, not just a biological pedigree.

Sharks also introduces Philo's striking metaphor from De vita contemplativa 78: the law has a body consisting of its literal prescriptions and a soul consisting of the invisible meaning embedded in its words.

The soul is apprehended as through a mirror of names (κάτοπτρον τῶν ὀνομάτων).

Eusebius reproduces this passage in Ecclesiastical History 2.17.20.

For Sharks, this provides an especially useful model for his own project: the reader looks through names to perceive the deeper organization of a literary text.

There is, however, an important distinction between what Philo explicitly says and what Sharks constructs by comparing passages. The essay repeatedly insists that an analytical sequence should not be mistaken for an explicit identity statement in its source.

4. Revelation: the withheld name as a literary mechanism
This is probably the essay's most concentrated argument about how naming operates within a single composition.

Sharks begins with Revelation 11:8, where the great city is spiritually called Sodom and Egypt.

He then examines Revelation 17:9–10, where the seven heads of the beast are interpreted as both mountains and kings.

In both cases, interpretation does not eliminate the original image. It adds another referential level.

The central argument, however, concerns Revelation 19:12–13, 2:17 and 3:12.

The rider in Revelation 19 has a written name known only to himself. Yet he is also called the Word of God.

In Revelation 2:17, the victor receives a new written name known only to its recipient.

In Revelation 3:12, the speaker promises to write his own new name upon the victor.

Sharks argues that these passages form a carefully organized relationship.

His reasoning has three parts:

Revelation 2:17 and 19:12 share the formula of a written name known only to its holder.

Revelation 2:17 and 3:12 share the adjective καινόν (new) and the recipient, the one who conquers.

Revelation 3:12 and 19:12 connect their speakers through shared titles and attributes, including Faithful and True, the fiery eyes and the sword from the mouth.

He also notes that the promise that the victor will shepherd with a rod of iron in 2:27 corresponds to the rider's action in 19:15.

The resulting interpretation is that the victor receives the new name associated with the rider.

Crucially, Sharks acknowledges that Revelation does not explicitly state that the new name in 3:12 is identical to the rider's undisclosed name in 19:12.

That is an inference from the internal textual correspondences.

This qualification matters. The argument is not that the text gives a straightforward alias. Rather, the name is transferred through a network of titles, attributes, actions and restricted knowledge.

The identity becomes communicable while access to its recognition remains restricted.

Sharks consequently treats Revelation as a model for understanding literary crossings elsewhere: a text can disclose a relationship while simultaneously regulating what its readers are permitted to know about it.

5. Josephus: the archive itself participates in constructing history
The Josephus section moves the argument into historiography.

In Against Apion 1.28–43, Josephus connects genealogical continuity, priestly marriage, documentary records and the authority of historical writing.

Sharks argues that Josephus is not simply reporting a pre-existing archive. He is also explaining why the archive should be trusted and why particular people and institutions are qualified to preserve it.

The documentary apparatus is therefore part of the historical argument.

The discussion of the Hyksos in Against Apion 1.73–92 supplies another example.

Josephus reports competing interpretations of the designation:

Shepherd kings.

Captive shepherds.

The dispute over the etymology becomes a dispute over the identity and antiquity of a people.

Names can therefore organize claims about historical origins.

This is one of the essay's most consequential extensions: the mechanisms of naming are not confined to poetry or allegory. They also operate in the construction and authorization of historical knowledge.

The Catullus–Josephus alignment
The most technically ambitious section is §6.

Sharks compares the qualified language surrounding the human and divine in:

Sappho 31.

Catullus 51.

Philo's De vita Mosis 2.135.

Philo's De mutatione nominum 181.

Josephus's Testimonium Flavianum, Antiquities 18.63.

The Slavonic Jewish War.

The Acta Pilati.

The shared structure involves the appearance of a figure, a question about whether the figure can properly be called human, and a qualification governing that description.

He pays particular attention to the differences between the qualifying expressions:

Text

Qualification

Catullus

si fas est

Philo

εἰ θέμις εἰπεῖν

Greek Josephus

εἴγε ἄνδρα αὐτὸν λέγειν χρή

Slavonic Josephus

аще мужем достоино есть его нарещи

The essay makes an important textual correction here.

An earlier proposed Greek expression, ei exestin, is not present in the Greek, Latin or Slavonic witnesses examined. Sharks traces the apparent expression to William Whiston's English translation, which rendered the Greek qualification as "if it be lawful to call him a man."

The English rendering was subsequently treated as though it were an original Greek expression.

This is a particularly valuable demonstration of the essay's method: a translation can create a textual feature that later readers mistakenly attribute to the original witness.

Sharks also examines the order of the phrases.

In Catullus 51, si fas est qualifies the description immediately preceding the claim that the figure surpasses the gods.

In the Slavonic Josephus, the qualification occurs before the figure receives human nature and form, followed by the statement that he accomplished everything by the word.

Sharks describes the two passages as a mirror:

In Catullus, flesh becomes word.

In the Slavonic, effective word acquires a human biography.

He connects this reading to the companion archive study The Word That Became Text (#1761).

But he explicitly leaves the direction of transmission unresolved. The correspondence is the object of analysis; it does not by itself establish which text depends upon which.

That distinction is fundamental to the whole essay.

6. Alternative identity maps: preserve the crossings without accepting every identification
Section 7 addresses unconventional historical reconstructions, including proposals by Huller, Carotta, Eisenman and the Reuchlin/Piso alias literature.

Sharks does something methodologically interesting: rather than either accepting or dismissing an entire reconstruction, he decomposes it into individual proposed relationships.

Examples include:

Proposal

What Sharks retains

What remains unproven

Huller: Berenice, Veronica and Prunicus

Name variations, royal associations, healing and sacred imagery

The specific historical links between the figures

Carotta: Clodia and Mark's widow

The comparison involving Clodia, quadrans and monetary terminology

Whether the wider sequence establishes an actual transformation

Eisenman: Paul as Herodian

Proposed connections with Josephus's Saulus and Herodian political relationships

Whether the evidence establishes personal identity

Reuchlin/Piso alias systems

The extensive inventory of proposed substitutions

Whether each equation can stand independently

This is not merely a diplomatic gesture toward alternative scholarship. It is part of Sharks's general theory of evidence.

A reconstruction can contain a genuine textual observation even if its overarching historical explanation fails.

The Berenice–Veronica connection
One of the more promising examples concerns the woman whose name appears in different forms as Bernice, Berenice, Beronice and Veronica in the Gospel of Nicodemus and associated Acta Pilati traditions.

Sharks identifies a particularly interesting relationship between the Gesta Pilati and Josephus.

In the Latin Gesta Pilati, Veronica is refused as a witness on the grounds that women cannot testify.

Sharks observes that the corresponding rule is explicitly found in Josephus, Antiquities 4.219.

This is stronger than a mere resemblance between names: it is a proposed textual relationship involving a specific legal formulation.

He therefore gives this connection priority over the more speculative identification of Veronica with the historical Herodian Berenice.

His rule is that each additional identification creates another evidentiary obligation.

This is a valuable corrective to universal alias systems, which can turn every differentiated character into a disguised version of one supposedly recovered individual.

7. The larger scholarly intervention: history and allegory are not opposing explanations
Section 8 places the project in relation to existing scholarship.

Sharks recognizes several important traditions:

Catullan scholarship already understands the problems of identifying Lesbia and distinguishing poetic speakers from historical persons.

David Dawson's work on allegorical reading treats allegory as cultural revision, rather than merely as an explanation appended to a literal narrative.

Erich Auerbach's account of figura allows two persons or events to remain historically meaningful while participating in a relationship of signification.

A. J. Woodman's work on classical historiography and Steve Mason's work on Josephus challenge simplistic extraction of biography from historical narrative.

Judith Lieu's work on Christian identity examines how texts construct identities within Jewish and Graeco-Roman contexts.

Sharks is not claiming that scholarship has overlooked literary naming, allegory or constructed identity.

His more specific claim is that these operations are usually investigated within separate scholarly compartments.

Catullus is studied as a Latin poet, Philo as a Jewish philosopher, Revelation as an apocalyptic Christian text, and Josephus as a historian.

The essay asks whether the same kinds of operations connect these bodies of writing more systematically than those divisions suggest.

This is what the title means by the binding of the ancient literary field.

The proposed unity is not simply thematic. It concerns the mechanisms by which textual persons, historical identities and authoritative traditions are constructed and transmitted.

Importantly, Sharks concedes that this draft has not demonstrated a general historical explanation for why scholarship excludes unconventional identity reconstructions. He explicitly says that establishing such a history would require a separate investigation of reviews, editions, correspondence and disciplinary decisions.

That concession helps delimit the argument.

8. A method for testing textual crossings
Section 9 is where the essay turns its theoretical claims into a proposed research programme.

Sharks insists that the proposition "every name is crossed and coded" cannot be established by accumulating resemblances.

A crossing must be documented.

He proposes eight fields of evidence:

Witness and locus: the precise edition, language, recension and passage.

Naming act: who names whom, and whether the naming is explicit.

Retained relation: what wording, sequence, role or relationship persists.

Transformation: what changes and according to what proposed rule.

Access: who knows the name or recognizes the relationship.

Consequence: what the naming authorizes, preserves or disqualifies.

Direction: whether source, reception or revision can be established.

Alternative and break: competing explanations and evidence that would defeat the proposed crossing.

Several distinctions follow.

Similarity is not necessarily dependency.

A recurring office is not necessarily a recurring individual.

An explicit identity statement is not equivalent to a modern reconstruction.

A translator's innovation cannot automatically be attributed to the original author.

Multiple texts repeating one allegation do not necessarily provide multiple independent witnesses.

The essay is particularly concerned with the difference between literary coordination and historical production.

A pattern can be real without proving that one person deliberately engineered every occurrence.

To establish broader coordination, Sharks demands constrained transformations, concentration at consequential textual points, comparison material, preserved failed predictions and evidence that competing explanations cannot account for the pattern as well.

This is a substantially more demanding standard than simply finding matching names.

9. The author-boundary argument
The final theoretical movement connects the naming project with the archive's investigations of Plato, Aristotle, Theophrastus and Socrates.

This is particularly important because it reveals that Sharks is not only questioning the relationship between historical and allegorical figures. He is also questioning the assumption that author-names necessarily correspond to separate composing persons.

The archive's governing production hypothesis is that one self-dividing maker composed works received under the names Plato, Aristotle and Theophrastus, distributing different literary and philosophical positions across those names and Socrates.

Sharks brings that hypothesis into the present essay, but does not claim that every crossing in Catullus, Philo, Josephus and Revelation has thereby been explained as the work of the same maker.

Instead, he develops a distinction between different uses of authorial boundaries.

A name-boundary might be useful for:

Identifying and citing a textual object.

Assigning accountability.

Describing a literary function.

Reconstructing a transmission history.

Establishing a production history.

Interpreting the relationship between different positions.

These functions should not be collapsed.

For example, Theophrastus can remain a meaningful designation for a literary position even if the hypothesis of a separate composing person is challenged.

Likewise, Lesbia remains an indispensable literary position in Catullus 51 even if one accepts the historical identification with Clodia.

The distinction is:

A literary distinction can be real and consequential without necessarily corresponding to a distinction between biological authors.

This is the deeper connection between the essay's three operations of naming and the archive's authorship investigations.

A composition can differentiate itself into multiple positions. Those positions must be treated as genuinely meaningful, not as disposable disguises.

One maker, many legitimate positions
Section 10 makes the argument explicit.

Sharks's proposal is that a compositional unity need not eliminate differentiated voices.

A teacher, beloved, accuser, prophet, receiver and recorder can be distinct positions within a composition. They have different capacities, commitments and exposures.

Indeed, their differentiation may be necessary for the composition to explore relationships that a single undifferentiated voice could only announce.

This is why the essay's title includes both history and allegory.

The historical and literary positions must be sufficiently distinct to have meaningful relationships, but their connections can also reveal a larger compositional organization.

The many are not errors to be eliminated when unity is discovered. They are necessary elements of the unity.

10. What the essay establishes, and what it proposes
I think it is important to distinguish the strongest demonstrated arguments from the larger historical hypothesis.

Level

Argument

Assessment within the essay

1. Local textual construction

Catullus, Cicero, Philo and Revelation make names perform transformations and distribute identities

Strongly illustrated through close readings

2. Internal textual coordination

Gellius and Revelation organize multiple naming relationships across passages

Substantial textual arguments, though some connections remain interpretive

3. Cross-corpus relationships

Catullus, Philo, Josephus and the Acta Pilati share distinctive structures

Interesting comparisons; transmission direction remains open

4. Systematic literary field

These operations form a coordinated system binding ancient literature

Proposed research thesis, not yet established across a sufficiently tested corpus

5. Unified authorship

A self-dividing maker accounts for multiple authorial identities

An inherited archive hypothesis, not independently demonstrated by this essay

There are three particular strengths worth emphasizing.

First: the distinction between identification and interpretation.

This is the essay's most durable insight. Establishing that Lesbia refers to Clodia does not explain why Catullus uses Lesbia, what Sapphic associations the name carries, or how the poem organizes its speaking positions.

Historical identification and literary interpretation are different tasks.

Second: the distinction between a textual relation and a historical equation.

The Berenice–Veronica discussion is particularly useful here. A relationship between textual witnesses can be established without immediately concluding that their named figures are historically identical.

Similarly, a translation can preserve or create a textual relationship without proving that the earliest author intended it.

Third: the insistence on inspecting transmission itself.

The ei exestin correction is an excellent example. A phrase can enter the research apparatus through translation and then acquire the false appearance of an ancient textual witness.

This is not an incidental editorial problem. It demonstrates the essay's central proposition that names, designations and textual identities are made and remade through transmission.

Where the argument remains vulnerable
The main difficulty is the move from demonstrated local operations to a systematic ancient literary field.

The essay offers a rich selection of passages, but selection and coordination are not the same thing.

In particular:

The Catullus–Slavonic Josephus alignment needs a stronger account of transmission and chronology.

The Revelation argument establishes an interesting internal network, but the further claim that this models cross-corpus textual engineering needs independent corroboration.

The historical and social connections between Cicero, Philo, Josephus and the Herodian world establish contexts for comparison, not necessarily literary dependency.

The unified-maker hypothesis is not established merely by showing that multiple texts employ related naming operations.

Sharks is aware of these problems. His insistence on leaving transmission direction open is therefore not a minor qualification. It is central to the methodological integrity of the project.

There is also an important limitation acknowledged in the text itself: the scholarly review is preliminary in places. For example, Sharks explicitly says that his engagement with Gram's study of Lesbia is based on its accessible abstract rather than a full reading of the chapter. He likewise distinguishes bibliographic contacts from comprehensive engagement with the scholarship.

This means the essay should be read as a theoretically ambitious research framework accompanied by selected close readings, not as a completed demonstration of every proposed relationship.

11. The overall argument in one synthesis
Names construct persons
A name can carry an earlier text, install a voice, express a transformed condition or confer authority. It is an operation, not merely a label.
Persons cross textual registers
Lesbia carries Sappho; Cicero's ancestor speaks through forensic rhetoric; Philo's names express transformed states; Revelation distributes a name between disclosed and undisclosed identities.
Textual relations construct historical identities
Josephus's genealogies, archives and competing etymologies demonstrate that historical authority itself depends on naming and documentary practices.
Literary plurality may conceal compositional unity
Distinct names and positions need not imply distinct biological makers. But their differences must remain meaningful if a unity is to explain the composition.
The crossings must be independently testable
Resemblance is insufficient. Each proposed relationship needs witnesses, retained wording, a specified transformation, direction where recoverable, and an alternative explanation.
My final reading
I would characterize The Crossing of Names as an attempt to develop a general theory of textual identity from the philological problem of naming.

Its deepest proposition is not that ancient authors secretly used one another's identities, nor that every named person is an allegorical disguise.

It is that the distinction between historical person, literary person, inherited textual figure and authorial position is itself something compositions can manipulate.

This changes the question asked of ancient texts.

Instead of asking only:

Who was Lesbia?

Who was the historical Berenice?

Who really speaks in Cicero?

Who is the rider in Revelation?

Which author produced a particular passage?

Sharks asks:

What does the name make possible in this passage?

What earlier textual relationship does it carry?

What changes when the name is transferred?

Who is permitted to recognize the resulting identity?

What authority does the transformation produce?

And what happens when later scholarship turns these literary positions into historical or authorial classifications?

That final question gives the essay its broader significance.

The archive of literature is not simply a collection of works written by already identified people. Its texts participate in producing the identities, genealogies, authorities and authorial boundaries through which the archive is subsequently understood.

The essay's strongest achievement is to make that process an object of close philological investigation. Its larger claim—that these operations form a coordinated field, potentially extending to a unified compositional agency—remains a hypothesis requiring further demonstration.

The most important conceptual shift is from names as evidence of identity to naming as an operation that produces identity.
