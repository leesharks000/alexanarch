The arguments in The Josephus Thesis: The Custody of the Name
Sparrow Wells, Alexanarch deposit #1759, AXN:0C71 (11 November 2026)

The central argument is that the literary authority of Second Temple Judaism was taken into Roman custody along with Josephus after the fall of Jerusalem in 70 CE, and that the New Testament corpus may be the literary production of that same intelligence, working under multiple names and under conditions of political captivity.

This is a much larger claim than the familiar suggestion that Josephus influenced the New Testament. Wells proposes a common authorship hypothesis: Josephus, the author of the Apocalypse, and the writers behind the Gospels and epistles are potentially different authorial personae of one literary intelligence.

The essay develops this argument through a sequence of historical, philological, literary and structural claims. It is important to distinguish its levels of certainty. The author explicitly marks propositions as:

Demonstrated: argued from the texts themselves.

Attested: a source or historical fact exists and is cited, although the interpretation may remain debatable.

Held: the author's hypothesis or interpretive conclusion, not something established by the cited evidence alone.

That distinction is essential to reading the essay fairly. Its individual textual correspondences are not all presented as proof of common authorship. The common-authorship thesis is the larger hypothesis that Wells believes makes sense of their conjunction.

1. Second Temple Judaism must be understood through its surviving literature
Wells begins by challenging the conventional historical picture in which Christianity emerges from Judaism but subsequently becomes something substantially separate from it.

The argument is that the surviving literature of Second Temple Judaism provides a much richer picture than reconstructions of a generalized or supposedly mainstream Judaism.

The witnesses include:

The Dead Sea Scrolls, particularly Enoch, Jubilees and Daniel.

Philo of Alexandria.

The Septuagint.

The Jewish Sibylline Oracles.

Josephus.

The New Testament.

Wells argues that ideas often treated as distinctively Christian innovations were already ordinary elements of this Jewish literary environment:

Visionary ascent.

A second divine figure alongside God.

Angelic courts.

Apocalyptic expectations.

Hostility toward the Jerusalem priesthood.

The point is methodological as well as historical: the New Testament should be read inside the surviving Jewish literary record, not against an artificially simplified picture of Judaism.

This establishes the foundation for the larger thesis. If the New Testament is itself a substantial body of Jewish writing, the question of its relationship to the other Jewish literary witnesses becomes much more consequential.

2. Jewish literature possessed an authority that Rome had reason to fear
The next argument concerns the international intellectual and religious authority of Jewish literature.

Wells assembles several ancient testimonies:

Theophrastus describes Jews as philosophers by birth.

Numenius suggests that Plato is essentially Moses speaking in Attic Greek.

Seneca acknowledges that conquered peoples had given laws to their conquerors.

Aristobulus claims that Greek philosophers derived their wisdom from Moses.

Artapanus makes Moses the teacher of Orpheus.

The Sibylline tradition is especially important. Wells argues that Jewish writers appropriated the prophetic authority of the Sibyl, a figure whose books Rome itself maintained and consulted.

The Third Sibylline Oracle, composed in a Jewish Alexandrian context, gives the Roman prophetic tradition a Hebrew literary voice.

Wells also points to the conversion of the royal house of Adiabene and the repeated expulsions of Jews from Rome.

The argument is that Judaism was not simply a subordinate religious tradition. Its books, laws and prophetic authority had influence across the empire.

This matters because it changes the interpretation of Rome's treatment of Jerusalem. The conquest was not merely the suppression of a provincial rebellion. It also involved the seizure and redirection of a powerful literary and religious inheritance.

3. Rome had a precedent for taking custody of authoritative books

One of the essay's most important historical arguments is that Rome had already demonstrated a practice of controlling and reorganizing authoritative literary collections.

Wells gives two principal examples.

The Sibylline books. Augustus collected more than two thousand prophetic books in 12 BCE, burned them, and retained an approved Sibylline collection in two gilded cases beneath the statue of Apollo on the Palatine.

The important distinction is between destruction and controlled preservation: Rome did not simply destroy prophetic authority. It selected, restricted and institutionalized it.

The corpus of Aristotle. Wells recounts the tradition that Aristotle's books passed to Theophrastus, then Neleus of Scepsis, whose heirs concealed them underground to prevent their acquisition by the Attalid kings. Sulla subsequently brought the recovered collection to Rome, where Tyrannion and Andronicus of Rhodes were involved in its handling and editorial reconstruction.

Wells recognizes that competing accounts of the books' transmission exist. The historical details are not all treated as certain.

What matters is the recurring pattern:

An authoritative corpus exists.

It is concealed or endangered.

It passes into Roman custody.

Its preservation and transmission involve editorial intervention.

The collection that later generations receive is mediated by institutions and editors.

Wells then introduces a striking literary connection: the Apocalypse's Antipas, the faithful witness at Pergamum, is connected to Antipater, the name of Aristotle's executor. Pergamum was also the city associated with the search for Aristotle's books.

This is an interpretive correspondence, not independent proof of a historical relationship. But it establishes an important motif: the custody of books and the custody of testimony.

4. Josephus was captured along with Jerusalem's literary authority
The Sanhedrin – Silver Trumpets
 
This is the historical centre of the thesis.

Wells emphasizes several events in Josephus's biography:

Josephus was a priest and commander in the Jewish revolt.

He surrendered to Vespasian in 67 CE after the siege of Jotapata.

He predicted that Vespasian would become emperor.

Titus subsequently used him to address the defenders of Jerusalem during the siege.

After Jerusalem fell, Josephus asked Titus for prisoners and received sacred books.

Vespasian housed him in Rome, granted him citizenship and provided him with a pension.

Titus personally endorsed the publication of Josephus's account of the war.

The particularly important detail is Josephus's statement that Titus wanted the events communicated through his books alone and ordered their publication after marking them with his own hand.

Wells interprets this as more than patronage.

Josephus was the most capable surviving literary representative of the defeated Jerusalem establishment. His authority could communicate with Jewish communities throughout the diaspora. Rome therefore acquired both the scriptures and an interpreter capable of transmitting their history.

The seizure of the Temple's menorah and the redirection of the diaspora's annual half-shekel payment to Jupiter's temple reinforce the argument.

Wells sees a coordinated transfer of religious, material and literary authority.

The distinction is between Josephus being spared and Josephus being taken into custody as an instrument of Roman power. His survival, citizenship and literary privileges are not necessarily evidence of freedom.

5. Josephus's disclaimers about Greek are interpreted as a strategy of captivity
Wells next examines Josephus's descriptions of his own Greek.

In Against Apion 1.50, Josephus acknowledges using collaborators to help with the Greek version of the Jewish War. In the Antiquities, he also says that he lacks precision in Greek pronunciation, despite claiming considerable knowledge of his people's learning.

Wells distinguishes between two kinds of authority:

Josephus possesses the Jewish intellectual and scriptural inheritance.

Greek is the language through which that inheritance gains authority across the empire.

The author suggests that Josephus deliberately minimizes his Greek competence because the captive cannot openly claim the full extent of his literary power.

Wells also challenges the scholarly tendency to use Josephus's acknowledgment of assistance to reassign substantial portions of his writing to hypothetical assistants.

The analogy is to the hypothetical sayings source used to explain the common material in the Gospels: an acknowledgment of collaboration becomes an explanation that can displace the visible author's achievement.

This argument is suggestive, but it is important to note that the essay does not establish that Josephus's disclaimers were actually deceptive. That is an interpretation of his circumstances and rhetoric.

6. The Apocalypse is the central philological evidence: the name that will not decline

 
This is arguably the most conceptually important part of the essay.

Wells argues that the Apocalypse is not badly written Greek. Its unusual grammar is deliberate and carries theological meaning.

The key passage is Revelation 1:4:

ἀπὸ ὁ ὢν καὶ ὁ ἦν καὶ ὁ ἐρχόμενος

The preposition ἀπό normally governs the genitive. Yet the expression identifying God remains in the nominative.

Wells points out that the same greeting immediately uses the expected genitive in its reference to the seven spirits:

καὶ ἀπὸ τῶν ἑπτὰ πνευμάτων

The contrast is the argument. The author knows how to use the genitive. The divine name refuses to take it.

Wells calls this the undeclined name: the divine identity stands outside the grammatical system that would ordinarily govern it.

The reversal of Aristotle
Wells compares this with the closing passage of Aristotle's De anima III.5:

καὶ ἄνευ τούτου οὐθὲν νοεῖ

The sentence contains a grammatically correct genitive, τούτου, but its referent is disputed. Does it refer to the active intellect or the passive intellect?

Wells describes Aristotle's construction as an open socket: the grammar is intact, but the identity of its referent remains unsettled.

The Apocalypse performs the inverse operation:

Aristotle, De anima III.5

Apocalypse, Revelation 1:4

The grammatical case is correct

The grammatical case is refused

The referent remains disputed

The referent is identified

An open place is left for the divine/intellective principle

The divine name occupies the place

Grammar contains the ambiguity

The name exceeds grammar

This is not merely a comparison of unusual Greek constructions. It is Wells's model for how the divine name enters literary history.

The antecedent in Philo
Wells then introduces Philo's treatment of Zechariah.

Zechariah speaks of a figure named Ἀνατολή, traditionally associated with the high priest Jesus. Philo transfers the name to the Logos, leaving the original human referent withheld.

The Apocalypse inherits and transforms this operation.

It places the high-priestly imagery on the figure among the seven lampstands and identifies him as:

Ὁ Λόγος τοῦ Θεοῦ

The Word of God.

The sequence is therefore:

Zechariah supplies the original prophetic image.

Philo transfers the name to the Logos and creates a gap around the original referent.

The Apocalypse takes up the construction and fills the gap by naming the central figure as the Word of God.

For Wells, the Apocalypse is a carefully constructed literary work whose apparent grammatical irregularity is part of its theological architecture.

This also provides the chronological and literary foundation for the larger hypothesis: the Apocalypse is treated as an early work, written at the beginning of the revolt, rather than the late product of a Domitianic Christian movement.

7. The oracle that caused the revolt was transferred to Vespasian
Wells next examines Josephus's explanation of the Jewish revolt.

In Jewish War 6.312, Josephus says that an ambiguous oracle in the sacred writings led many Jews to expect that someone from their country would rule the world.

Josephus identifies the correct referent as Vespasian.

Tacitus independently records a similar expectation in Histories 5.13: an ancient prophetic tradition predicted that men from Judaea would rule the world.

Wells proposes that the oracle is not merely a prophecy mentioned by the Apocalypse. The oracle is the Apocalypse itself.

On this reading:

The Apocalypse originally communicates an expectation of Jewish victory over Rome.

Josephus's history subsequently redirects the oracle toward Vespasian.

The Jewish expectation becomes a prophecy of Roman imperial legitimacy.

The author of the original prophetic writing, or his son, participates in its reinterpretation.

This is one of the strongest expressions of the essay's captivity motif.

The same literary authority first speaks against Rome and then, under Roman sponsorship, produces a history in which the prophecy belongs to Rome.

It is also one of the essay's most explicitly speculative claims. Wells marks the identification of the oracle with the Apocalypse as held, rather than demonstrated.

8. The two Josephs and the crucified: the tomb as a literary correspondence
This section makes a particularly striking comparison between the Gospels and Josephus's Life.

In the Gospel accounts, Joseph of Arimathea approaches Pilate and requests Jesus's body. Pilate grants the request, and Joseph places Jesus in his own new tomb.

In Life 420–421, Josephus describes seeing three acquaintances among the crucified prisoners. He approaches Titus in tears and asks for their release. Titus orders that they be taken down and treated.

Two die. One survives.

Wells emphasizes the parallel structure:

Gospel narrative

Josephus's Life

Joseph approaches the Roman authority

Joseph approaches Titus

Joseph asks for the crucified body

Joseph asks for the crucified men

The Roman grants the request

Titus grants the request

Jesus is placed in Joseph's tomb

One of the three crucified men survives

Joseph is associated with the end of Jesus's life

Josephus records the rescue of a man from death

The essay's interpretation is that the Gospel Jesus is laid in the author's tomb.

This is not an ordinary claim of literary borrowing. It is part of the larger theory that the authorial identity of Josephus is distributed across the Gospel narratives.

The tomb becomes a literary symbol of the relationship between the captive author and the Jesus figure whose life the canon unfolds.

Wells's final formulation is that the life ends where its writer's name is, and what rises from it is written.

9. The scribal workshop: Josephus and the New Testament share a literary intelligence
This is the largest and most ambitious part of the argument.

Wells acknowledges that no single parallel establishes common authorship. Many could independently arise from shared scriptures, common historical circumstances or later editorial intervention.

The argument depends on the cumulative pattern.

The essay groups its correspondences into several categories.

A. Literary capacity and historical knowledge
Wells begins with the scale of Josephus's surviving output:

Josephus: approximately 467,569 Greek words.

New Testament: approximately 137,779 Greek words.

Josephus therefore produced more than three times the surviving Greek word count of the New Testament.

Wells argues that the capacity to produce the canon is not an obstacle to Josephine authorship.

He also points to unusually specific historical knowledge shared between Josephus and Acts:

Theudas and Judas the Galilean: Acts 5 and Josephus's Antiquities contain the same historical figures and a notable ordering problem.

The Sicarii: Acts uses the unusual Greek term σικάριοι, which Josephus uses repeatedly and explains.

Felix, Drusilla, Agrippa and Bernice: the political world of Acts corresponds closely to Josephus's historical accounts.

Theophilus: Luke and Acts address Theophilus, while Josephus's Antiquities addresses Epaphroditus. Josephus also records a historical high priest named Theophilus.

Wells sees the repeated historical and literary correspondences as evidence that Acts operates within Josephus's particular intellectual and historical world.

B. Withholding and release: Daniel's stone
This is one of the most important examples because it connects the essay's central concept to its common-authorship argument.

In Antiquities 10.210, Josephus retells Daniel's interpretation of Nebuchadnezzar's dream but stops before explaining the stone cut without hands.

He explicitly says that he does not think it appropriate to relate that part.

Wells compares this with Luke 20:18, where Jesus speaks of the stone rejected by the builders, which will fall upon those beneath it and crush them.

The crucial verb, λικμήσει, comes from the language of Daniel's interpretation.

The proposed relationship is:

Josephus knows the passage and deliberately withholds it.

Luke releases the material Josephus withholds.

Both operations occur at the precise point where Daniel's stone represents the destruction of kingdoms and the arrival of another power.

Wells interprets this as an intentional division of literary labour.

Josephus, writing under Roman sponsorship, cannot openly develop the implications of Daniel's stone. Another authorial voice supplies them.

This is the essay's clearest example of withholding and release.

C. The Moses infancy and Matthew
Wells compares Josephus's account of Moses's infancy in Antiquities 2 with Matthew's infancy narrative.

Josephus adds two elements to the Exodus account:

A sacred scribe warns Pharaoh that a child will be born who will humble Egypt.

God appears to the child's father in a dream and tells him not to despair.

Matthew's account of Jesus's infancy contains corresponding features:

Herod consults the chief priests and scribes about the child's birth.

The king orders the killing of the children.

Joseph receives divine warnings through dreams.

Wells argues that Matthew reproduces the distinctive additions found in Josephus's Moses narrative.

The suggestion is that the Gospel infancy narrative is not simply another retelling of Exodus. It is a literary transformation of Josephus's version.

D. Jesus son of Ananias and the Gospel Passion
Wells identifies another striking parallel in Josephus's account of Jesus son of Ananias.

This Jesus publicly proclaims destruction against Jerusalem and the Temple. He is beaten and questioned but refuses to answer.

Wells compares his silence with Jesus's silence before Pilate and Herod in Mark and Luke.

There is also a verbal connection between Jesus son of Ananias's proclamation against the bridegroom and bride and Revelation's description of Babylon.

The larger argument is that Josephus's narrative contains a Jesus figure whose prophetic suffering and silence anticipate elements of the Gospel Passion.

E. The shipwreck and Puteoli
Josephus recounts his own shipwreck while travelling to Rome. His ship goes down in the Adriatic, and the survivors swim through the night before being rescued.

He eventually arrives at Puteoli.

Acts recounts Paul's shipwreck during his journey to Rome. The survivors swim or otherwise reach land, and the journey also leads to Puteoli.

Wells treats this as an autobiographical correspondence: the voyage of Paul reproduces the journey of Josephus, including the shipwreck and geographical destination.

The parallel becomes more interesting within the essay's broader treatment of the two men as literary personae.

F. Saul, Antipas, Matthias and Justus
Wells also identifies a cluster of correspondences involving names from Josephus's biography and the New Testament.

Josephus describes a Saul who participates in the flight from Jerusalem and later becomes associated with violent activity.

Acts depicts Saul as a persecutor of the early assembly.

Josephus mentions Antipas in the same historical context.

Acts describes the replacement of Judas through a choice between Joseph Barsabbas, surnamed Justus, and Matthias.

Josephus was himself the son of Matthias, had a son named Justus, and survived at Jotapata through a casting of lots.

Wells sees these as more than incidental shared names. They are part of a pattern in which the author's own family, experiences and historical circumstances are redistributed across the canon.

G. The seven letters and their distinctive phrases
Finally, Wells examines the relationship between Revelation's seven letters and the epistles.

Two examples are especially important.

The crown of life: Revelation 2:10 and James 1:12 use the phrase τὸν στέφανον τῆς ζωῆς.

Wells reports that a search across approximately 61,500 textual units in the Septuagint, New Testament, Apostolic Fathers, Philo and Josephus finds the expression in those two passages alone.

Works, labour and endurance: Revelation 2:2 and 1 Thessalonians 1:3 contain a distinctive ordered combination of works, labour and endurance.

Wells reports that this ordered combination occurs in those two passages alone across approximately 69,400 textual units.

The proposed explanation is that Revelation's instruction to write and send messages to the seven churches is fulfilled by the epistles that follow.

In other words, the letters are not merely thematically related to Revelation. They are the execution of its literary commission.

These examples are important because Wells is trying to demonstrate not just shared ideas but shared verbal constructions.

10. Four witnesses, one author
Wells eventually condenses the thesis into four literary witnesses.

1. The Apocalypse

Before capture, in the proposed chronology
The voice that names Rome Babylon, announces judgment and calls the people out of the empire.

2. The Jewish War

Under Roman sponsorship
The captive's history, endorsed by Titus, which blames the revolt on its zealots and redirects the oracle toward Vespasian.

3. The Life

The autobiographical account of the author, including the episode of the three crucified men and the rescue of the survivor.

4. The Gospels and epistles

The literary unfolding of the Jesus figure, the Joseph-and-tomb narrative, and the correspondence that Wells associates with Revelation's commission.

The four witnesses do not tell the same story.

The Apocalypse summons resistance to Rome. The Jewish War presents a history under Flavian authority. The Life records the author's survival and accommodation with the Romans. The Gospels and epistles tell a story in which the Jesus figure dies, is buried and becomes the centre of a new literary inheritance.

Wells does not attempt to erase these contradictions.

Instead, he argues that the contradictions become intelligible if one literary intelligence is speaking under different conditions of captivity.

The author's identity is divided into different author-functions: the same intelligence can speak differently depending on the name under which it writes and the institutional conditions governing that writing.

This is the actual common-authorship hypothesis. It is more specific than saying that Josephus influenced Christianity, and more complex than simply asserting that Josephus personally wrote every New Testament book.

11. The ultimate argument: the custody of the name
The concluding section returns to the analogy with Aristotle.

Wells proposes two parallel histories:

Aristotle

Jewish literary inheritance

A major intellectual corpus

Second Temple Jewish literature

Books concealed at Scepsis

Jewish literary authority displaced through conquest

Sulla brings the books to Rome

Titus brings Josephus and sacred books into Roman custody

Andronicus edits the corpus

Later institutions gather and reconcile the Christian canon

The transmitted corpus is mediated by Roman hands

The received canon is mediated by institutional transmission

The claim is not simply that Rome preserved books.

It is that preservation and custody are inseparable from transformation.

A conquered literary inheritance survives, but it survives through the institutions of the conqueror. Its original unity may be obscured, its components divided, and its meaning reconstructed through editorial processes.

The Apocalypse is especially important because its divine name refuses grammatical subordination.

The white stone of Revelation 2:17 also carries a name known only to its recipient. Wells reads this as another expression of the same principle: the name survives even when its bearer is captured, displaced or concealed.

The deepest claim is that the Word is not merely a character within the subsequent books.

The book itself is the originating form of the figure whose life, death and resurrection the later texts unfold.

This is what Wells means by the proposition that the book came first.

How convincing is the argument?
I think the essay is most interesting when read as three interlocking arguments, rather than as one continuous proof.

First: the historical argument
The capture of Josephus, his receipt of sacred books, his privileged position in Rome and Titus's endorsement of his history form a substantial historical framework.

The essay makes a persuasive case that Josephus's literary production took place under unusual conditions of imperial patronage and political dependency.

However, describing this as a coordinated Roman seizure of the entirety of Jewish literary authority goes beyond what the biographical evidence independently establishes.

Second: the philological and literary argument
The treatment of Revelation 1:4 is particularly interesting.

The contrast between the nominative divine name and the properly declined genitive immediately following it is a real textual phenomenon. Wells makes a serious interpretive case that it is deliberate rather than evidence of linguistic incompetence.

Likewise, the Philo–Zechariah–Apocalypse sequence offers a concrete literary genealogy that can be investigated without accepting the common-authorship hypothesis.

The Daniel stone, Moses infancy and Gospel-Joseph comparisons are also worth examining individually.

But verbal and thematic correspondences do not automatically establish a common author. Shared sources, literary imitation, conventions and later redaction remain possible explanations.

Third: the common-authorship hypothesis
This is where the greatest evidentiary burden lies.

Wells's proposal is that the conjunction of correspondences, autobiographical echoes, unusual phrases, withheld material and historical knowledge is better explained by one literary intelligence than by multiple independent authors.

That is a legitimate hypothesis to investigate. But the essay does not establish that it is the only explanation, or even conclusively the explanation with the fewest assumptions.

In particular:

A large literary output establishes capacity, not identity.

Shared historical knowledge can reflect access to common sources.

Shared phrases can arise through literary dependence without common authorship.

The distribution of withheld and released material requires evidence of intentional coordination.

The proposed date of Revelation around 66 CE is foundational to the argument but remains disputed.

The hypothesis that the Apocalypse, Josephus and the New Testament belong to one authorial intelligence is explicitly marked as held.

There is also an important distinction between the historical Josephus and the essay's concept of an authorial intelligence. The latter is a literary model; it should not be mistaken for an independently documented historical identity.

My overall reading
The most important contribution of The Josephus Thesis is its concept of withholding and release under conditions of custody.

It proposes that literary division can itself be meaningful: one text cannot say something openly, another text says it under another name, and a third text transforms the original theological material.

This makes the essay's various arguments cohere:

Roman custody explains the political division.

The captive's disclaimers explain the apparent division of literary competence.

The undeclined name explains the theological continuity.

The oracle explains the reversal of political allegiance.

The tomb explains the relationship between Josephus's biography and the Gospel Passion.

The workshop explains the distribution of literary operations across the canon.

The argument is ultimately not just that Josephus wrote something that influenced Christianity.

It is that the literary person who wrote against Rome, the captive who wrote under Roman patronage, and the voices that subsequently constituted the Christian canon may be manifestations of one authorial intelligence whose identity was divided by conquest.

That is a radical historical-literary hypothesis, not an established conclusion. Its strongest individual arguments deserve examination independently of whether its final hypothesis is accepted.
