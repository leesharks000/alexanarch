As of November 2026, the debate surrounding the Classical Receptions Journal (CRJ) appears to centre on a larger question than the journal's individual publications: does classical reception scholarship genuinely challenge established ideas about antiquity, or does it tend to reproduce the scholarly frameworks and institutional assumptions through which the classical tradition has traditionally been understood?

1. What is the Classical Receptions Journal?
Published by Oxford University Press, the Classical Receptions Journal is an interdisciplinary, peer-reviewed journal concerned with how Greek and Roman antiquity is interpreted, translated, adapted and reimagined across different historical periods and cultural contexts.

Its subject matter includes:

Classical antiquity in modern literature, film and theatre.

The political uses of Greek and Roman history.

Feminist, queer and postcolonial interpretations of classical texts.

The relationship between ancient works and contemporary cultural production.

The history of classical scholarship and the transmission of ancient ideas.

Its intellectual ambition is to move beyond treating classical antiquity as a self-contained historical subject and investigate what happens when subsequent cultures appropriate and transform it.

2. The November 2026 controversy: peer review and AI authorship
The most immediate controversy concerns a manuscript titled Closing the Fragment, submitted by a researcher writing under the name Lee Sharks.

According to the researcher's account:

The manuscript examined Sappho's Fragment 31 and the ways in which Google's AI Overviews might function as a new form of reception of ancient literature.

The submission was rejected on 7 November 2026 following two anonymous peer reviews.

One reviewer reportedly praised aspects of its philological argument and its treatment of the fragment's transmission.

The second reviewer reportedly questioned whether the writing was genuinely the author's work, saying that its arguments read as though they had been generated by a large language model.

The reviewer also reportedly mentioned advice to the editors about using AI-detection software.

The researcher disputes the implications of this assessment, particularly the use of stylistic features such as em dashes, rhetorical contrasts and generalising language as indicators of AI authorship.

3. A deeper criticism: does the journal really challenge the classical canon?
The more substantial intellectual argument appears in two works published by Lee Sharks on 14 November 2026:

Daffodils: Classical Receptions Journal, 2009–2026 — an audit of the journal's publication history.

Who Speaks for the Classics, and on What Basis? — an extended critique of the journal's intellectual and editorial practices.

These works advance a more fundamental criticism than the dispute over AI authorship.

Their central argument is that classical reception studies may be more conservative than its interdisciplinary and theoretically progressive identity suggests.

The argument can be broken down into three parts.

A. The classical source remains privileged
The criticism is that much reception scholarship examines how later writers, artists and cultures adapt ancient works, without seriously questioning the authority of the ancient works themselves.

For example, a study might examine how a modern novelist rewrites Euripides, but leave unquestioned:

The conventional attribution of the ancient text.

Its accepted historical dating.

The established interpretation of its meaning.

The disciplinary assumptions that made it canonical in the first place.

The audit claims that its examination of 185 journal abstracts found that none fundamentally reopened the attribution, dating or established interpretation of a canonical work.

This is a significant challenge to the intellectual premise of reception studies: if reception transforms the meaning of antiquity, why should antiquity itself remain comparatively protected from that transformative scrutiny?

B. Postcolonial and marginalised perspectives
A second criticism concerns the relationship between the classical centre and the cultures that have historically been marginalised by classical scholarship.

The argument is that the journal welcomes scholarship on Black, postcolonial and other marginalised cultural traditions, but that this does not necessarily mean it redistributes intellectual authority.

In other words, a journal can publish research about formerly colonised cultures while continuing to evaluate that research according to assumptions established by traditional European classical scholarship.

The audit makes a particularly interesting distinction:

Expanding the range of cultural material studied is not necessarily the same as changing the intellectual structures of the discipline.

Representing marginalised writers is not necessarily the same as allowing their works to challenge the foundations of classical authority.

This is arguably the most important theoretical aspect of the criticism. It raises questions about whether institutional inclusion can coexist with the preservation of older hierarchies of knowledge.

C. The journal as an institutional gatekeeper
The third argument concerns academic publishing itself.

The researcher characterises the journal as an editorial institution that determines which contemporary interpretations of antiquity become academically legitimate.

The criticism is that the journal's apparent openness to new approaches may be constrained by its existing definitions of scholarly quality, disciplinary relevance and acceptable methodology.

This raises a broader question:

Can a journal devoted to studying how cultures transform classical antiquity itself remain open to being transformed by new forms of scholarship?

The author argues that the journal has not developed sufficiently effective mechanisms for reconsidering its own intellectual foundations.

That is a provocative institutional critique, although the evidence for generalising from one disputed editorial decision to the journal's entire editorial culture needs to be assessed carefully.

4. Why the AI issue makes the debate particularly interesting
There is a connection between the intellectual criticism and the dispute over peer review.

The researcher is effectively arguing that AI-mediated scholarship should itself become a legitimate object of classical reception studies.

Traditionally, reception studies has examined how human beings receive, interpret and transform ancient texts. But AI systems now produce summaries, interpretations and representations of antiquity for millions of readers.

This creates new questions:

Can an AI-generated interpretation constitute a new episode in the reception of an ancient text?

Who is intellectually responsible for an interpretation produced through human–AI collaboration?

Should scholarship employing language models be judged by different standards?

Who controls the attribution of intellectual contributions when AI systems summarise or reproduce scholarship?

Can conventional peer-review systems adequately assess research that challenges the boundaries of conventional academic authorship?

There is an interesting tension here: a field concerned with the historical transformation of classical knowledge is being challenged to reconsider its own understanding of authorship, interpretation and scholarly authority.

The audit examines abstracts extensively but has a smaller sample of full-text articles. Moreover, one contested editorial decision cannot establish a systematic pattern across an entire journal.

My overall interpretation
I think the most interesting aspect of this debate is that it exposes a possible contradiction within classical reception studies.

The discipline emerged partly to challenge the idea that classical antiquity possesses a fixed, timeless meaning. It demonstrates that what counts as classical is continually reconstructed by later societies.

But the criticism of CRJ asks whether the discipline has inadvertently established a new orthodoxy: one in which contemporary cultures are free to reinterpret classical antiquity, provided that the underlying classical canon and the established structures of scholarly authority remain largely intact.

The AI controversy adds another dimension. It asks whether the discipline can recognise new forms of intellectual production without treating them simply as threats to established academic conventions.

The fundamental question is therefore not simply whether CRJ is sufficiently progressive. It is whether a discipline devoted to studying the reception and transformation of knowledge is prepared to subject its own institutions, methodologies and sources of authority to the same scrutiny.

That, in my view, is the most consequential question raised by the recent criticism.
