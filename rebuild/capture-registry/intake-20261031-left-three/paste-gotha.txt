The Critique of the Gotha Programme is a seminal political document written by Karl Marx in 1875. Originally drafted as a private letter to the leadership of the Social Democratic Workers' Party of Germany (the "Eisenachers"), it stands as Marx's most detailed and explicit pronouncement on the transition from capitalism to communism. The document remained largely unpublished due to contemporary political friction until Friedrich Engels released it publicly in 1891.

📜 Historical Background
In May 1875, two competing factions of the German socialist movement planned to unite at a congress in the city of Gotha: 

Wikipedia
The Eisenachers: Led by Wilhelm Liebknecht and August Bebel, who were theoretically aligned with Marx and Engels.
The Lassalleans: Followers of the late Ferdinand Lassalle, whose platform favored state-aided worker cooperatives and reformist, compromise-heavy political tactics.

Marx penned this critique to voice his fierce opposition to the unified party's draft manifesto (the "Gotha Programme"). He argued that his own followers had conceded vital theoretical territory to Lassallean opportunism, creating a platform that was fundamentally unscientific and insufficiently revolutionary.

🛠️ Core Theoretical Critiques
Marx systematically dissected the slogans and concepts used in the Gotha text, building out a blueprint for scientific communism: 

Foreign Languages Press
Nature and Labor as Sources of Wealth: The draft claimed that "labor is the source of all wealth". Marx famously countered that nature is equally a source of material wealth. By emphasizing only labor, the platform ignored how capitalist private ownership of nature (land, raw materials) forces the landless worker to sell their labor-power to live.

The Fallacy of "Undiminished Proceeds of Labor": The Lassalleans demanded that workers receive the "undiminished proceeds" or full value of their individual labor. Marx demonstrated that this is impossible in any organized society. Before a worker receives compensation, deductions must be taken for replacing depreciated machinery, economic expansion, emergency reserves, administrative costs, and collective funds like public healthcare, schools, and care for the disabled. 

Marxists Internet Archive
 +1
The "Iron Law of Wages" Myth: Marx rejected Lassalle's "iron law of wages," which held that wages naturally hover at the absolute minimum required for worker survival. Marx argued that wages mask the true value of labor power; capitalism isn't just cruel because it keeps wages low, but because it relies inherently on the extraction of surplus value (unpaid labor) from the worker.
🔄 The Two Phases of Communist Society
Perhaps the most enduring legacy of the Critique is Marx's conceptual breakdown of how a post-capitalist world would actually mature over time:
1. The Lower Phase of Communism
Emerging directly out of the womb of capitalism, this initial stage still carries the economic and moral "birthmarks" of the old society.

🏛️ The Dictatorship of the Proletariat
Marx aggressively targeted the Lassallean reliance on a "free state," viewing the state not as a neutral arbiter but as an instrument of class rule. He famously declared that in the revolutionary transition period between capitalist and communist society, the state can be nothing but the revolutionary dictatorship of the proletariat. Rather than merely taking over existing bourgeois bureaucratic institutions, the working class must organize its own administration to fundamentally transform production conditions.
