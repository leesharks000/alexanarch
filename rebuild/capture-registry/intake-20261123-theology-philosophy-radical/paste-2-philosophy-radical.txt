The most radical philosophical arguments of Alexanarch
The arguments developed at Alexanarch  can be organized around a central question:

Who possesses the authority to determine whether knowledge exists, what counts as knowledge, how it is transmitted, and whether it survives?

The archive's most radical propositions concern more than digital preservation. They extend into political economy, epistemology, ontology, authorship, classical philology, artificial intelligence, and institutional theory.

The following is a thematic index of the arguments as Alexanarch presents them. I distinguish the archive's propositions from my descriptions of their relationships. I do not assess their truth, validity, originality, or scholarly standing.

I. The four foundational arguments
These four arguments provide a useful entrance into the larger philosophical architecture.

1. Sovereignty over intellectual existence
Central proposition: Intellectual existence must not depend on the permission or continued custody of an institution.

The argument distinguishes:

Intellectual ownership from institutional custody.

The persistence of an identifier from the persistence of the intellectual object.

The existence of a work from its recognition by a repository.

Historical existence from present accessibility.

The archive proposes content-derived identifiers, independent verification, distributed preservation, and alternative routes of access.

Its underlying philosophical claim is that control over preservation is a form of power over intellectual existence.

2. Admissibility is more fundamental than visibility
Central proposition: Power operates not merely by suppressing speech, but by determining what is permitted to count as a legitimate intellectual object.

The distinction is between visibility and admissibility.

A work might be publicly visible yet remain institutionally or algorithmically inadmissible. Conversely, a work might exist intellectually without being recognized by the systems that classify, retrieve, rank, and circulate knowledge.

The archive argues that resistance requires retaining authority over the meaning of one's work rather than surrendering it to the mechanisms through which the work becomes legible.

3. Capital-alignment governs automated knowledge
Central proposition: Automated systems can enforce economic and institutional conformity without explicitly prohibiting speech.

Search engines, recommendation systems, and language models do not merely reproduce knowledge. Their selection, ranking, compression, and representation practices shape what becomes publicly intelligible.

The archive calls attention to the possibility that:

Market compatibility becomes a criterion of representation.

Algorithmic selection functions as an implicit judgment.

Knowledge is transformed to fit the systems that distribute it.

Exclusion can occur without an identifiable act of censorship.

This is an account of epistemic power exercised through mediation.

4. Silence can be structurally constitutive
Central proposition: What a system cannot say, represent, or recognize may disclose the structure of that system.

The archive's Total Axial Negation Graph (TANG) draws on Wittgenstein's philosophy of language.

Its proposal is that a thesis can sometimes be demonstrated through the structure of a field's exclusions, absences, and limits rather than through ordinary linear argument.

Silence is treated not simply as missing information but as something that can constitute the boundaries of intelligibility.

Particularly important: the Obelus Principle
The Obelus Principle invokes Alexandrian textual scholarship, particularly the use of the critical obelus to mark questionable passages.

Its proposed methodological inversion is:

The archive records first. Intellectual judgment follows reading.

Rather than allowing an institutional or computational classification to determine whether something deserves consideration, the work should be preserved and made available for examination.

This connects archival ethics to epistemology: the order in which institutions preserve, classify, and judge intellectual objects is itself a philosophical question.

The semantic proletariat
This is a particularly important concept in the archive's political economy.

The argument is not simply that intellectual workers are underpaid or insufficiently credited.

It proposes a class relation:

One group produces meaning.

Another controls the infrastructure through which meaning is retrieved, classified, transformed, and distributed.

The infrastructure-owning group can appropriate value from the intellectual production of others.

Producers consequently lose authority over the circulation and representation of their own work.

The archive thus extends the Marxian question of ownership of the means of production into the domain of semantic production.

The proposed alternative is reclamation of the means of semantic production.

V. The archive's institutional philosophy
Several arguments above converge on a larger proposition: an archive is not a neutral container.

It is an institution that determines relationships between:

Intellectual objects and their identifiers.

Authors and their works.

Readers and the material they can retrieve.

Historical existence and present accessibility.

Preservation and institutional authority.

The archive therefore proposes an alternative institutional model:

Distributed custody: no single custodian should possess unilateral power over the scholarly record.

Independent identity: intellectual objects should carry verifiable identities that survive institutional changes.

Auditable governance: archival decisions should be inspectable.

Historical continuity: withdrawal should not silently erase the history of a work.

Unaffiliated scholarship: intellectual verification should not be conditional upon institutional credentials.

Counter-institutional publication: scholarly work should be publishable outside conventional academic authorization.

The archive's institutional project is consequently also an argument about the political constitution of scholarship.

VII. How the arguments relate
The arguments can be understood as five interconnected philosophical projects.

1. Ontology — What makes an intellectual object exist?

Intellectual identity, semantic objects, distributed authorship, historical continuity.

2. Epistemology — Who determines what can be known?

Admissibility, the Obelus Principle, judgment after reading, classifier model collapse, TANG.

3. Political economy — Who controls the production of meaning?

Semantic capital, semantic extraction, semantic proletariat, semantic alienation, reclamation of semantic production.

4. Political philosophy — Who governs intellectual existence?

Knowledge sovereignty, institutional custody, distributed governance, counter-institutional publication.

5. Philology — How do texts survive, acquire meaning, and produce intellectual identities?

Revelation First, Sappho's Future Reader, Socrates as Orthonym, heteronymic authorship, textual provenance.

The projects are connected by a common concern: the conditions under which intellectual objects, meanings, and authors can exist independently of the systems that mediate them.

IX. The unifying thesis
The archive's philosophical programme can be condensed into the following proposition:

The sovereignty of knowledge requires sovereignty over the conditions of its intellectual existence.

This connects its apparently separate concerns:

Preservation becomes a question of sovereignty.

Metadata becomes a question of intellectual identity.

Search becomes a question of epistemic power.

AI becomes a question of semantic production and appropriation.

Authorship becomes a question of intellectual ontology.

Philology becomes a question of textual survival and historical reconstruction.

Institutional independence becomes a question of political constitution.
