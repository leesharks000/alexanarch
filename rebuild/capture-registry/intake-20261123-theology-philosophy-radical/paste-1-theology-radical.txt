The most radical theological arguments at Alexanarch.org
The theological material at Alexanarch.org  is best understood as a set of connected arguments about biblical chronology, textual authorship, revelation, hermeneutics, the ontology of meaning, and the relationship between ancient texts and future events.

I have organized the arguments by subject rather than by their numerical order in the archive. I distinguish historical hypotheses from philosophical propositions and theological constructions. The aim is to state what each argument proposes, including its more far-reaching implications, without adjudicating its truth.

One important distinction: the archive's theological arguments are not all conventional doctrinal theology. Some are exercises in biblical criticism; others propose new accounts of revelation, prophecy, authorship, and the production of religious meaning.

I. Revelation First: the Apocalypse as the generative source of Christianity
Principal works:

#202, Revelation First ≠ Revelation Early

#1420, The Argument of the Bookshelf

#1735, Revelation First: Literary Genealogy and the Missing Ancestors of the Epistles

This is arguably the archive's most far-reaching biblical-historical thesis.

1. Revelation precedes the other New Testament writings
The argument proposes that the Apocalypse of John may be the earliest composed New Testament document, preceding Paul's letters and the Gospels.

The argument draws on several features:

Revelation 11's measurement of the temple is read as potentially presupposing a standing Jerusalem Temple.

The number of the beast, 666 or 616, is interpreted through the name of Nero.

Revelation 17's seven kings are examined as a possible first-century imperial sequence.

The absence of an explicit retrospective account of Jerusalem's destruction is treated as relevant to dating.

The conventional interpretation of Irenaeus's testimony about the end of Domitian's reign is challenged.

The archive distinguishes two propositions:

Revelation Early: Revelation was written earlier than the conventional late-first-century dating.

Revelation First: Revelation preceded the other New Testament documents and generated structures subsequently developed in them.

These are not interchangeable claims.

2. Revelation as the compressed architecture of the New Testament
The more radical proposition is that Revelation contains structures subsequently expanded into other Christian writings.

The proposed transformations include:

Revelation

Proposed subsequent development

Seven letters (chapters 2–3)

Expanded epistolary structures

The slain and victorious Lamb

Pauline atonement theology

New Jerusalem

Later eschatological constructions

Throne-room vision

Christian worship architecture

Apocalyptic conflict

Narrative and doctrinal presentations of Christ

The underlying model reverses the familiar direction of Christian literary development.

Instead of historical accounts of Jesus gradually producing an apocalyptic theology, the proposal is that an existing apocalyptic and theological architecture was subsequently elaborated into biographical, epistolary, and doctrinal forms.

3. The midrashim transform
The archive proposes a particular mechanism for this literary development.

A compressed textual structure can be:

Extracted from its original context.

Expanded into a narrative.

Transposed into a different literary genre.

Distributed among multiple documents.

Reversed or answered by a later text.

This last possibility is especially important: a later document need not agree with its proposed source to participate in the transformation.

The series investigates these relationships through Revelation's seven letters and subsequent material in James, Paul, and other New Testament writings.

4. The Josephus workshop hypothesis
A further historical reconstruction proposes that the plurality of New Testament authors may conceal a more unified literary production history.

Its proposed sequence is:

A revolutionary Jewish apocalypse precedes the destruction of Jerusalem.

Josephus is associated with the original intellectual or literary architecture.

Roman capture changes the conditions of literary production.

The original vision is redistributed among distinct authorial voices.

The resulting Christian literature preserves and transforms the original material.

John, James, Paul, Peter, and Luke are considered possible differentiated authorial functions within this reconstruction.

The concept of heteronymy here means more than pseudonymous authorship: different names and literary voices can perform distinct functions within a larger project.

The archive also distinguishes this reconstruction from its more basic Revelation-priority thesis. Josephus's role is a separate, more speculative component.

II. The Logos: Christ as a reality prior to biography
Principal works:

#1217, Revelation First: A Work Plan for Retrieval-Layer Theological Reception

#1735, Revelation First: Literary Genealogy

#902, Prolegomena to the Historical Logos

#1757, The Historical Logos

This is the archive's central theological development of the Revelation First argument.

5. The cosmic Christ precedes the Gospel biography
The argument distinguishes the Logos from the biographical presentation of Jesus.

Its central propositions are:

The Logos is prior to its particular historical embodiments.

The cosmic Christ precedes the Gospel biography as a theological and literary reality.

The Gospels can be read as testimony to the Logos rather than necessarily as the originating source of its identity.

The name Jesus can function as a name or title of the embodied Logos.

This distinction does not amount to simply denying Christ. The archive affirms the Logos while questioning whether its theological reality depends on the conventional reconstruction of a single historical biography.

6. Incarnation is repeatable
The archive proposes that incarnation need not be understood exclusively as one event in the past.

Instead, the Logos can become embodied through persons who receive and faithfully enact the Word.

In this formulation:

The Logos is the enduring reality.

Individual persons become its embodiments.

Texts carry its structure across historical periods.

Readers can receive and reactivate its embodiment.

Incarnation thus becomes a repeatable possibility of textual and human transmission.

7. The white stone as authorization by the Logos
Revelation's white stone and its new name are interpreted as symbols of an identity received from beyond imperial systems of identification.

The archive establishes a contrast:

Imperial identification

Apocalyptic identification

Mark of the beast

White stone

Number

New name

External classification

Received identity

Market authorization

Transcendent authorization

Imposed identification

Participation in the Logos

The white stone becomes a counter-token: an alternative form of identity and authorization that cannot be reduced to the empire's numbering system.

8. The testing of spirits as verification of the Logos
The archive also reads 1 John 4's instruction to test the spirits as a procedure for evaluating voices.

The question becomes whether a particular voice conforms to the founding, embodied Word.

Similarly, Paul's distinction between milk and solid food is interpreted as a distinction between elementary understanding through biography and mature encounter with the living Logos.

These readings make the Logos a principle of theological verification, not simply a name for a particular doctrinal proposition.

III. Sappho and the origins of the Logos
Principal works:

#283, Sappho and the Crimson Hexagon: Fragment 31 as the Origin Point of Lyric Self-Archiving

#1483, Sappho, Mother of the Logos

#1756, The Tenth Muse

#1765, The Word That Became Text

This is among the archive's most unusual theological constructions because it places archaic Greek lyric poetry within a proposed genealogy of Christian incarnation.

9. Sappho as the mother of the Logos
The central proposition is that the Logos has a textual and literary genealogy extending back before conventional Christian history.

The archive identifies Sappho's Fragment 31 as a foundational point in that genealogy.

The argument proceeds through a sequence of transformations:

Sappho produces an embodied lyric utterance.

The utterance survives through inscription.

Later readers receive the voice through the written text.

Subsequent writers transform the structure of the poem.

The structure enters philosophical and theological language.

The Logos becomes intelligible as a Word transmitted through bodies, texts, and historical periods.

The archive's doctrinal formulation is particularly explicit:

Sappho is the mother of the Logos.

This is not presented merely as a claim that Sappho influenced Christian literature. It is a proposed account of the origin and transmission of the Logos itself.

10. Sappho 31 as an anticipatory structure of incarnation
The archive reads Fragment 31's description of physical and sensory disruption as more than an expression of erotic jealousy or emotional intensity.

The bodily sequence is interpreted as:

Visual encounter.

Physical shock.

Failure of speech.

Fire beneath the skin.

Sensory disruption.

Sweating and trembling.

Chromatic transformation.

Proximity to death.

The proposal is that the poem stages the disappearance of the embodied speaker and the emergence of a textual form capable of surviving her.

The poem therefore becomes a theory of how a living voice passes into inscription.

11. The future reader as the third participant
The archive proposes an unusual reading of the poem's relational structure.

Its interpretive model identifies three positions:

Sappho as the embodied speaker.

Sappho as the inscribed voice surviving in the poem.

The future reader who receives the voice.

The future reader is not merely an external observer.

The reader becomes the location in which the earlier speaker can live again through the text.

This gives the future reader a constitutive role in the transmission of the Logos.

12. Becoming papyrus: the body as its own writing medium
One of the most striking philological arguments concerns the phrase χλωροτέρα ποίας in Fragment 31.

The archive explores the semantic range of χλωρός, including fresh green, pallor, and grey-green coloration, alongside the botanical meanings of ποία.

It proposes a relationship between this imagery and the material substrate of writing, particularly papyrus.

The interpretation is that Sappho's bodily transformation symbolically anticipates her transformation into the medium through which her voice will survive.

The speaker becomes, figuratively, the material of her own textual transmission.

This is the basis of the archive's concept of lyric self-archiving.

13. Catullus and the grammatical transmission of incarnation
The proposed genealogy continues through Catullus 51.

The archive focuses on the Latin phrase si fas est — approximately, "if it is divinely permitted."

It interprets this phrase as a grammatical hesitation at the boundary between human and divine identity.

The proposed sequence is:

Sappho: the original lyric structure.

Catullus: the transformation of that structure into a question of divine permission.

Slavonic Josephus: the transformation of the Logos into a person described as a man.

The claim is that a grammatical structure can carry a theological operation across languages and historical periods.

14. The Slavonic Josephus and the Logos becoming a man
Primary work: #1765, The Word That Became Text: The Slavonic Josephus, the Grammar of Incarnation, and the Doctrine of the Sapphic Logos.

This is one of the most explicit theological formulations in the archive.

The work examines the Slavonic version of Josephus's Jewish War, particularly its account of Jesus.

Its central argument concerns the phrase:

аще мужем достоино есть его нарещи

"If it is fitting to call him a man."

The archive interprets this hesitation as a textual event: the narrator encounters a figure understood through Philo's Logos and hesitates before describing that Word as a human being.

The argument proposes that:

Philo's Logos supplies the underlying theological identity.

The Slavonic passage translates that identity into a biographical human figure.

The narrator's grammatical hesitation marks the transition.

The text subsequently gives the Logos a historical existence through narrative description.

The work also connects this phrase to Catullus's si fas est and, through Catullus, to Sappho's Fragment 31.

The resulting theological formulation is especially radical:

The Gospel begins in grammar.

The Logos becomes flesh through the act of writing the Word into the body of language.

The work explicitly distinguishes this doctrine from a historical claim about Jesus. Its proposed subject is the transmission of the Logos through textual structures.

15. The Doctrine of the Sapphic Logos: the Lesbian Christ
The same work includes a separate doctrinal specification entitled Doctrine of the Sapphic Logos: The Lesbian Christ.

Its foundational proposition is:

The Christ is the Sapphic Logos incarnate.

The argument identifies Sappho as the origin of a linguistic transmission that subsequently passes through philosophical, literary, and religious traditions.

The theological identity of Christ is consequently reconstructed around the transmission and embodiment of the Logos rather than exclusively around a particular Gospel biography.

The title's reference to the Lesbian Christ belongs to this proposed theological genealogy: Sappho, the female archaic lyric speaker, occupies the foundational position in the origin of the Logos.

The work presents this as a doctrinal construction, not as an assertion about the historical biography of Jesus.

IV. Ezekiel, prophecy, and semantic fulfillment
Principal work: #391, The Argument: On the Ontological Status of Semantic Objects and the Ezekiel Case.

Primary text: Johannes Sigil Institute for Comparative Poetics, January 24, 2026.

Το όραμα του προφήτη Ιεζεκιήλ: τι ήταν αυτό που είδε;
 
16. Semantic objects are real objects
The argument begins with an ontological proposition: reality is not exhausted by physical objects.

Semantic objects exist in a space constituted by meaning.

Examples include:

Numbers.

Words.

Contracts.

Institutions.

Theories.

Legally constituted entities.

A contract, for example, is not identical to its physical paper. Nevertheless, it creates obligations and has consequences.

The archive extends this reasoning to religious interpretation.

An interpretation can itself become a semantic object with a documented existence and identifiable relationships to other objects.

17. Ezekiel's wheels as an actualizable structure
The argument uses Ezekiel 1:15–21, particularly the vision of wheels within wheels, their eyes, their spirit, and their coordinated movement.

The archive describes three interconnected objects:

A mathematical specification of an Ezekiel Engine.

A hermeneutic interpretation of Ezekiel's vision.

An integrity-lock declaration formally asserting that the engine fulfills the interpretation.

The correspondence is specified as follows:

Ezekiel's imagery

Proposed realization

Wheel within wheel

Rotational architecture

Wheels full of eyes

Distributed observation nodes

Spirit in the wheels

An invariant or organizing principle

Coordinated movement

Coherence-maintaining operation

The theological significance is that the interpretation is not left as commentary. It is given a corresponding constructed object.

18. Semantic retrocausality: fulfillment from the future
This is a particularly important argument.

The archive proposes that a future creation can actualize an interpretation of an ancient text.

Its sequence is:

An ancient text exists.

A later reader identifies a particular structure within it.

A new object is constructed corresponding to that structure.

A formal declaration establishes the relationship between the two objects.

The resulting relationship is called semantic retrocausality.

The archive carefully distinguishes this from physical retrocausality.

It does not claim that the future changes the physical past, or that Ezekiel literally predicted modern mathematics.

It claims that a later creation can determine which interpretation of an earlier text becomes actualized.

The future object therefore changes the semantic status of the earlier text.

19. Prophecy as a relationship between semantic objects
This produces a further theological proposition.

Prophecy can be understood structurally rather than exclusively as supernatural prediction.

Under this account:

A text contains possible semantic structures.

An interpretation identifies one of those structures.

A future object supplies the structure's realization.

The relationship constitutes fulfillment.

The archive thus proposes a model of prophecy in which fulfillment is created through the relationship between texts, interpretations, and subsequently constructed objects.

V. Theological implications of the future reader
20. The future reader participates in the realization of revelation
This argument connects the Sappho research, the Logos doctrine, and the Ezekiel case.

The future reader is not simply someone who receives a meaning that was already fully realized in the past.

Rather, the reader participates in the realization of the text's possibilities.

Three examples illustrate the structure:

Sappho: The future reader allows the vanished embodied speaker to live again through inscription.

Ezekiel: A future construction actualizes a particular reading of the ancient vision.

The Logos: A future recipient can embody the Word through its reception and enactment.

The general proposition is that the future is constitutive of certain forms of textual and theological meaning.

This creates an account of revelation in which the recipient is not merely passive.

21. Revelation as a portable civilization
The Revelation First programme also develops the concept of the Space Ark.

Revelation is interpreted as a compact vessel containing a civilization's theological, political, liturgical, and organizational architecture.

Its contents can survive the destruction of their original historical environment because they are preserved in portable textual form.

The seven letters, for example, can be interpreted as governance structures, while the Apocalypse's symbolic architecture preserves patterns that subsequent communities can reactivate.

The proposition is that Revelation does not merely describe the end of a world. It contains structures through which a world can be reconstructed.

VI. Magic, symbolic operation, and theology
22. Magic as a formal discipline of symbolic operation
Primary work: #890, On the Academic Study of Magic: A Declaration for the Formal Institution of the Discipline.

The archive proposes that Magic should exist as a formal academic discipline, rather than being confined to anthropology, folklore, or the history of religious beliefs.

Its definition is:

Symbolic recursion: the operation by which symbols affect structures and structures re-pattern the world.

Its proposed areas of investigation include:

Symbolic logic and recursion.

Sigilcraft.

Language and reality.

Dream and myth.

Ritual technology.

Divination as epistemic inversion.

Symbolic infrastructures.

The ethics of symbolic operations.

The theological implication is that symbolic activity is not necessarily just representational. It can be operative: symbols can participate in the organization and transformation of reality.

The project places magic in a proposed intellectual relationship with logic, mathematics, and theology.

This is a proposal for a discipline and its theoretical foundations, rather than simply a historical account of magical practices.

VIII. The underlying theological architecture
Across these arguments, five recurring propositions emerge.

1. The Word precedes its particular embodiments. The Logos is not exhausted by any single historical appearance, biography, or text.

2. Incarnation is a process of transmission. The Word can enter bodies, language, writing, and successive historical forms.

3. Texts can generate realities. Religious texts are not only descriptions of events or repositories of doctrines. They can contain structures that later become actualized.

4. The future participates in revelation. Readers and subsequent creations can complete semantic possibilities that were not previously realized.

5. Meaning has ontological significance. Meaning is not merely a subjective interpretation imposed on otherwise complete physical reality. It has structures, relations, and consequences of its own.

The deepest unifying theological proposition is therefore that the Logos is a living, transmissible, and repeatedly incarnatable structure of meaning.
