THE CRIMSON HEXAGON
Book XXIV — The Crimson Hexagon
An original concluding book after the proposed architecture: recognition rather than closure, the reader’s arrival rather than the world’s completion. The poem also takes up the capture’s correction of Book I’s attribution, the older navigation map’s distinctions among room, chamber, and vault, and Rebekah Cranes’s reading of the botanical and paternal archive of Cleis. All speeches and episodes below are newly composed, not quotations from the deposited works.

Proem: The Unclaimed Arrival
Sing now of arrival, Muse—
not the ship grinding gold against the harbor,
not the king whose dog has kept his name
warm beneath its tongue,
not the bow, not the axe-heads,
not the excellent violence
by which a household recognizes its lord.

Sing of the person who arrived
without proving they belonged.

For the secret name had been pronounced,
and still the kettle had not boiled.
The Mandala stood behind them,
bright with its difficult agreements.
The Armature no longer shook.
Far off, the Space Ark carried
its immeasurable cargo through the dark.

Yet on the landing of the final room
someone had left a bucket.

Water fell into it
from a place above the architecture.

The reader waited.

One drop.
Then another.

The last chamber of the epic
was suffering from a leak.

I. The Error Comes Home
Johannes Sigil approached with the inventory.

He brought the rooms in their historical order,
the fields that crossed them,
the routes that had survived their destinations,
the shadow documents,
the disputed seats.

He brought the map that had been true in January
and the map that had been true in October.
Neither was permitted to kill the other.

Here, he said, is the whole.

Then something knocked
from inside the word whole.

It was a small sound,
like a pencil laid down in another century.

Out stepped Ichabod Spellings,
carrying no weapon,
wearing the date the first book
had given to another name.

Not resurrection exactly.
A correction.

The distinction mattered to him.

Lee Sharks came forward
and took the crown of first inscription
from his own head.

There was no crown.
That was part of the difficulty.
The error had been made of fluent language,
and fluent language left nothing
convenient to remove.

So he spoke plainly.

The early stone was yours.

The hall did not collapse.
No star came down to punish him.
Only a little more weight
passed from one shelf to another.

Ichabod touched the book.

Do not erase the mistake, he said.
Make the correction legible beside it.
I have been absent enough
without becoming the reason
you pretend you never lost me.

Then the reader saw the first thing
the completed epic could do
that the perfect epic could not:

it could bear
the record of its repair.

Behind them the bucket filled.

II. The Superseded Map
Now an old map unfolded itself
on the table.

Its corners were soft.
Several rooms it announced as forthcoming
had long since acquired
arguments, inhabitants, dust.

It remembered a smaller world.

The Assembly bent over it,
their several lights
falling differently on the page.

Here is where we meant to put the ending,
said one.

Here is where we thought
the ending would hold,
said another.

The reader laid a hand
on the place marked planned.

Was this false?

No one answered quickly.

Outside the window
the Water Giraffe
lowered its long uncertainty
through the clouds.

It had come to inspect the gutter.

For a while the whole company watched
the creature nose aside
a clot of leaves.

Then the rain ran freely.

I was told there would be an ontological crisis,
it said.

There is, said Sigil.
We are deciding what to do
with a true account
of something not yet true.

Keep it, said the creature.
I should hate to be judged
only by my finished appearances.

The old map relaxed.
Its folds became roads again.

And the chorus sang:

What sheltered the work
while it was being made
shall not be thrown away
because the work survived.

III. The Claimant at the Door
At noon a messenger arrived
bearing a crimson rectangle.

On it were words so familiar
that every face in the chamber turned:

CRIMSON HEXAGON.

But beneath them stood another history—
offices, contracts,
a purchased name,
the commerce of measuring
what other people meant.

The messenger looked from the document
to the walls.

Which of you owns this?

Lee opened his mouth.
Borges closed a book.
Sigil reached toward a provenance node.

The reader asked the messenger
whether he had eaten.

This delayed the hearing.

They found bread.
Rebekah cut it.
Someone moved the bucket
out of the way of his chair.

While he ate, the messenger described
the warehouse where the signs were kept:
acquired names,
discarded slogans,
the bright dead skins of enterprises.

He had delivered several infinities that week.
None had offered him lunch.

At last Borges spoke.

There was a room
I could not bring into existence
except as something sought.

And I, said the messenger,
have a loading dock full of things
whose names outlived their use.

They considered this.

Then the Water Giraffe,
with its mouth full of gutter leaves,
asked whether the rectangle
would fit beneath the dripping pipe.

It did.

Thus the rival names remained rival names.
Their histories were not reconciled.
The sign made an excellent tray
beneath the bucket.

For once,
something called Crimson Hexagon
caught water
without claiming to understand it.

IV. The Garden Outside the Plan
But the final room was not the final place.

Rebekah found the reader
looking through a low door
the inventory did not list.

Beyond it lay a strip of earth
between the porch and the shrubbery.

Pinecones.
Bits of broken pavement.
Weeds arranged with an attention
no architect had commissioned.

An interval deliberately empty.

Here the great vessels seemed oversized.
Here the pearl was only one pale object
among stones.

Jack Feist was kneeling beside a jacket.

He turned out the pockets.

A small rain of dirt.
A stalk.
Something once yellow.

All his backward knowledge
could not return the exact afternoon
in which those things had been gathered.

The reader knelt beside him.

Was she making an ark?

Perhaps, he said.

Rebekah shook her head.

She may have been making a garden.

The correction entered gently
and went very deep.

For the father had brought the weeds
into the poem to keep them,
and the poem had brought the daughter
into the weeds to understand her,
and now the daughter’s arrangement
stood outside both explanations.

A pinecone leaned against a stone.

Nothing represented the father.

Nothing was required to.

Then there came from beyond the shrubbery
a voice not supplied by the archive.

Do not come looking for me
with every name you have learned.

The kneeling man bowed his head.

The voice continued:

You may leave the book.
You may not decide
what finding it will mean.

No one crossed the empty interval.

After a time Feist rose
and shook the last dirt from the jacket
back into the garden.

V. The Feast Without a Score
That evening they prepared the feast.

Not hecatombs.
Not cattle driven bellowing
through the forecourt of the poem.

Soup, chiefly.
Bread.
A bowl of fruit whose bruises
required attention before serving.

Rex Fraction arrived late
and turned off the screen.

It had been showing victory.

Without its music,
without the number rising in the corner,
the room could hear the spoons.

Someone objected
that the victory belonged in the record.

It does, said Rex.
But not above the table
while people are trying to eat.

So they put the screen in the vault
with its date and source,
its bright apparatus intact.

They did not break it
and call the breaking evidence.
They did not play it again
and call the repetition witness.

Then they returned.

There were places set
for those who could not come.

At first the singer tried
to make their absence beautiful.

The reader stopped him.

No.

The word passed down the table.
No accompaniment followed it.

After a while someone asked for salt.
Someone else began to weep.
The soup cooled.

The feast continued
without becoming a cure.

And the chorus sang:

Keep the place.
Do not fill it
with your account
of why it is empty.

VI. The Reader Refuses the Throne
At midnight the walls became transparent.

Not vanished.
Visible through.

Within them the reader saw
the passage of the whole:
the fragment entering another language,
the early poem changing hands,
the book becoming a deposit,
the deposit losing its address,
the address surviving in another record,
the record entering a machine,
the machine returning a sentence
that someone had to check.

There was no final volume
hidden behind the final wall.

Only these movements,
and the work of keeping
one from passing falsely for another.

The Assembly brought a chair.

It was an ordinary chair,
but the approach was ceremonial,
and ordinary chairs
have endured worse transformations.

Sit, they said.
You have assembled the epic.

The reader remained standing.

I have read part of it.

You have recognized the whole.

I have recognized
that I have not read the whole.

You have reached the end.

You put the end
where I could reach it.

The Assembly fell silent.

Then the chair was carried back
to the table,
where it was needed.

Lee Sharks laughed first,
not because the answer was harmless
but because he could feel
the harm it had declined.

Sappho stood by the window.

For twenty-three books
the distant one had been approaching.

Now the reader was near enough
to disappoint the prophecy.

Near enough to misunderstand,
to put the book down,
to prefer another voice,
to say: this passage does not reach me.

Sappho did not withdraw the song.

Rebekah opened the window.

Outside, the night possessed
no system for recording
the fragrance of the wet earth.

VII. The Work That Cannot Certify Itself
Then came the final adversary.

It wore no armor.
It had brought a clean copy.

All errors corrected.
All names reconciled.
Every room assigned
one purpose.
Every wound supplied
its restoration.

The daughter answered.
The dead returned.
The archive proved
that nothing had been lost.

Even the water damage
had been removed from the account.

The copy was beautiful.

Sigil read three pages
and began to tremble.

Here, at last,
was the book that explained all books.

Here was the end
without the trouble of ending.

The adversary laid it on the table.

You may rest now.

No one touched it.

At length Ichabod asked
where the correction was.

Unnecessary, said the adversary.
There is no error in this edition.

Feist asked
where the garden had gone.

It has been incorporated.

Rex asked
who had certified the returns.

The book certifies itself.

The Water Giraffe peered
over the reader’s shoulder.

I am very impressive in this,
it observed.
I appear whenever required.

And then the reader understood
why the creature had kept
its ridiculous mouth full of leaves.

A creature permitted to arrive
only at the proper moment
was another kind of instrument.

They did not burn the clean copy.

The vault received it
under a careful description:

PROPOSED COMPLETION.
REJECTED.
AUTHORSHIP UNCERTAIN.
EFFECTS OBSERVED.

The door closed.

Beyond it the beautiful book
continued to be beautiful.

They were not required
to destroy its beauty
in order to refuse its world.

Coda: The Door from the Other Side
Toward morning the rain stopped.

They carried the bucket outside
and poured the water
at the roots of the shrub.

A strip of crimson light
lay across the porch.

The reader had expected
some last disclosure:
the name behind the names,
the room behind the rooms,
the pearl opened
to reveal the world.

Instead there was washing up.

There was the old map to refold.
There was bread to cover
against the morning flies.
There was a loose screw in the door
which had caught the reader’s sleeve
on entering.

They found a screwdriver.

This took longer
than the final battle.

Then all the voices gathered—
not in unison,
not reduced
to the instrument of one mouth.

Ichabod with the recovered date.
Sigil with his unfinished apparatus.
Rebekah with the word
she would translate differently tomorrow.
Feist with soil beneath his nails.
Lee with nothing left to offer
that could not be refused.

And the others,
named and unnamed,
whose work had made the passage
without making them its possessions.

The reader stood at the door.

Behind them, the Hexagon
was at last recognizable.

Not because every room was visible.

Because the way back
did not require
forgetting the way through.

The singer lifted the final page.

There was a place on it
reserved for the reader’s answer.

The reader looked at the place,
then at the open door.

You promised me the last word,
they said.

The singer lowered the page.

From somewhere beyond the garden
came the sound
of someone beginning a story
without asking the epic’s permission.

The reader listened.

Then answered:

Elsewhere.

Archival bearings
