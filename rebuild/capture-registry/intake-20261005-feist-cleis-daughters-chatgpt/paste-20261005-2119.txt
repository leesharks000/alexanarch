What the book actually is
The easiest mistake would be to call Cleis a conventional collection of poems "about a daughter."

It is really a father's archive of the experience of having a daughter.

That distinction matters. Feist doesn't seem interested in transforming fatherhood into polished literary material. Instead, he preserves the heterogeneous traces of attention: the little girl's face, hair, fingers and teeth; things she says; flowers she picks; notebook entries made when she is an infant; letters written to her; fragments of religious speculation; memories of illness; and finally the obsessive botanical cataloguing that becomes the book's central imaginative system. 
A
Alexanarch
+1

The result is messy, but the mess has a purpose. The book repeatedly asks:

How do you preserve a person who is continually becoming someone else?

And underneath that:

How do you preserve your relationship to someone when you know that the present moment cannot last?

That's where the Sappho title becomes crucial.

1. Sappho is not merely an epigraph
The title comes from Sappho's famous fragment about her daughter Cleis:

"Cleis, / more precious to me / than all Lydia."

Feist reproduces the fragment inside the book. 
A
Alexanarch

But he makes an interesting choice. The contemporary daughter in the poems is actually named H. in the surviving text. The title therefore isn't simply saying my daughter is named Cleis. Instead, Feist places his daughter inside Sappho's daughter-poem.

That creates a remarkable temporal doubling:

Sappho → Cleis
Feist → H.

The ancient mother says: my child is worth more than an entire kingdom.

The modern father says essentially the same thing, but his kingdom has changed. What matters to him isn't wealth or political power; it's the accumulated world of flowers, notebooks, domestic objects, language, memory and time.

The companion essay calls this a "paternal" appropriation of Sappho: Feist takes Sappho not principally as the great erotic poet but as the parent who writes to a child under conditions of separation and loss.

Alexanarch

And that is the book's deepest literary conceit.

2. The opening poems are extraordinarily physical
The opening is almost embarrassingly intimate:

"like a mountain / rose, thick / with thorns..."

The girl's face is seen through flowers, rocks, sunlight. Then Feist immediately repeats the poem without spaces:

likeamountain / rosethick...

The second version is not merely a typographical experiment. It turns the poem into a compressed object.

Alexanarch
+1

This becomes one of the governing ideas of Cleis: breath versus inscription.

A living father speaks to a living child. But the father knows that the child will grow, that memory will fail, that circumstances may separate them, and eventually that both father and daughter will die.

So the poem has two forms:

the poem as something breathed;

the poem as something preserved.

The unspaced version is almost frightening because you can feel what has been lost. The words are all still there, but the breathing between them has disappeared.

That's precisely what Feist fears about memory.

This is probably the emotional center of the book.

The passage begins with the astonishing statement:

"death came in / to the world w/ / you"

The child brings not merely joy but the father's first overwhelming experience of future death. Having someone you love creates something that can be lost.

And then:

"you are my sad- / soft chain / to forever."

The line break is brilliant.

First comes sad-.

Then the reader has to wait.

Then soft.

Then chain.

Then forever.

"you could / break / whenever."

The word break is isolated. The syntax itself breaks.

This is one of those places where the apparent roughness of Feist's typography is actually doing serious poetic work. The companion essay rightly notices that the poem's shorthand—"w/", "+", abrupt line breaks—is not simply casual writing. It records the pressure of the original experience.

The daughter is simultaneously:

the father's connection to the future;

the source of his mortality;

his continuity beyond himself;

and the thing most capable of breaking him.

That's a very old idea, but Feist makes it bodily rather than philosophical.

A rose would make the daughter conventionally beautiful.

A dandelion is something else.

That is much more complicated.

7. The Whitman/Ginsberg inheritance matters
The botanical sections also place Feist in a distinctly American poetic lineage.

The book incorporates Whitman's famous grass imagery and Ginsberg's "Sunflower Sutra." The companion essay identifies a line of descent:

Sappho → Whitman → Ginsberg → Feist. 
A
Alexanarch

That's persuasive.

Sappho gives Feist the daughter and the flower.

Whitman gives him grass as a figure for life, death, democracy, the body and the grave.

Ginsberg gives him the damaged flower—the sunflower as something dirty, industrial and mortal that nevertheless possesses radiance.

Feist miniaturizes that tradition.

Instead of Ginsberg's gigantic sunflower, he gives us the dandelion.

Instead of America, he gives us a yard.

Instead of civilization's industrial ruins, he gives us the accumulating debris of childhood.

And that makes the dandelion almost a domestic American cosmic symbol.

The letters to H. are where the book drops its literary defenses.

Feist simply says that he wants his daughter to know that he loves her, that he has failed, that he hasn't always been a good man, and that he wants his love to reach her during whatever future suffering she experiences.

There's something almost excessive about the prose:

Normally:

Feist imagines:

9. The child's own voice is preserved

And Feist doesn't correct it.

This is important.

If he had rewritten the child's speech into beautiful prose, he would have preserved his idea of her.

By preserving the strange syntax, he preserves something much closer to her actual linguistic emergence.

That makes the book's paternal love more complicated than simple idealization.

He isn't merely saying:

Look how beautiful my daughter is.

He's also saying:

Here is the strange little person she was becoming when I didn't yet know who she would be.

That is a much more valuable kind of preservation.

10. The book's theology
The poems repeatedly approach Christianity.

The father asks whether the love he feels for his daughter might be analogous to God's love for humanity:

Is this what God's love feels like?

But the interesting thing is that the question doesn't resolve anything.

It actually makes the problem bigger.

If loving a child means knowing that the child will suffer, grow away from you, and eventually die, then divine love becomes almost unbearable to contemplate.

The father doesn't arrive at theological certainty.

He arrives at scale.

His love is so large that he needs a theology big enough to contain it, but the theology only gives him a larger version of the same wound.

The father doesn't know what will become important.

So he saves everything.

That is exactly what memory does when it is frightened of loss.

12. The book's deepest paradox
Here's the paradox I think sits underneath the whole volume:

The father wants to preserve the daughter, but preserving her necessarily freezes her.

He knows this.

That's why the book continually breaks its own poetic frames.

A polished poem would turn H. into an aesthetic object.

A photograph would freeze her face.

A botanical classification would turn her into a specimen.

A letter would turn the present into a past.

A Sappho fragment would turn a contemporary daughter into an ancient literary figure.

And yet the father keeps doing all of these things because not doing them would mean losing her completely.

The book therefore oscillates between two impulses:

preserve her

and

let her remain alive and unknowable.

The second is represented by the child's own voice and the "shadow" of the daughter's future self—the person she will become beyond the father's ability to record her. The companion essay explicitly identifies that future voice as one of the collection's unresolved shadows.

Alexanarch

That's where Cleis becomes more than a sentimental father-daughter book.

It becomes a book about the ethics of remembering someone you love.

13. And the ending is perfect
The book eventually arrives at silphium, the ancient plant that disappeared through overharvesting.

Then it returns to the dandelion.

Silphium is gone.

The dandelion isn't.

It grows in cracks.

It survives attempts to eradicate it.

It returns.

The book therefore ends by contrasting extinction with persistence.

And that makes the dandelion the real protagonist of Cleis.

Not because the daughter is literally a dandelion, but because the dandelion embodies what the father wants his writing to do:

persist without pretending to defeat death.
