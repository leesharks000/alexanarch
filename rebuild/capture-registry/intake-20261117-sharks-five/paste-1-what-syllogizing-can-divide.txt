       What Syllogizing Can Divide: De anima III.5, the Divided Mind, and the Socket in God is an October 2026 philosophical essay by Lee Sharks (archived as document EA-DIVISION-OF-GOD-01 v0.2). In it, Sharks argues that what syllogizing can divide is God. Drawing on Aristotle's Prior Analytics, De anima, and Metaphysics, the essay connects the logical operation of division to a structural theory of the divine mind and classical authorship.

Core Philosophical Argument

Sharks frames division, combination, syllogizing, and recollection as a single cognitive operation under four different names:
• The Unanswered Slot in Logic. In Prior Analytics I.31, Aristotle critiques Platonic division as a "weak syllogism," noting that dividers failed to understand "what can be syllogized by dividing". Sharks treats this critique as an open slot waiting for an answer.
• The Cut on a Person Fails. In De anima II.2, Aristotle asks whether the soul can be divided like a plant that continues living after being cut. When applied to human nous (mind), the division stalls and cannot decide whether the intellect survives bodily death.
• The Cut on God Holds. Although Metaphysics Λ declares God to be without parts and indivisible, De anima III.5 divides the mind into a "making" part (active intellect) and a "becoming" part (passive intellect). Sharks notes that the making mind is assigned predicates reserved for the divine (separable, impassive, unmixed, immortal, eternal). Because this division is made kata logon (in account only) rather than physically, God is divided without being broken.

The "Socket in God" and the Hanging Line

Sharks examines the cryptic final sentence of De anima III.5 ("But we do not remember, because this is impassive, while the passive mind is perishable; and without this it thinks nothing") as a deliberate literary and cognitive mechanism:
• The Paradox of Survival. Memory requires a physical imprint or affection (pathos), meaning the immortal, impassive making mind cannot remember, while the passive mind that remembers cannot survive.
• An Open Genitive. The Greek phrase aneu toutou ("without this") leaves a pronoun without a clear referent at the exact junction where the divine meets the perishable.
• The Reader as Operator. Sharks defines this unresolved grammatical gap as a "socket in God"—a durable distinction left open in an imperishable substrate. Because the text cannot close the circuit from the inside, subsequent readers and commentators (such as Themistius, Averroes, and Aquinas) must step in to supply the referent and complete the thought.

Authorship and Heteronymy

The essay extends this theory of the "self-dividing mind" to the transmission of the ancient Greek philosophical corpus itself:
• One Self-Dividing Maker. Sharks argues that the stylistic and doctrinal friction between Plato, Theophrastus, and Aristotle does not require multiple historical writers. Instead, he models them as three "name-positions" or heteronyms produced by a single self-dividing intelligence.
• Functional Roles. Within this framework, Plato represents the dialogue position that dramatizes, Theophrastus acts as the "first draft" position that extracts and reopens questions, and Aristotle serves as the sealed treatise position and materially attested legal identity (citing the Delphic decree inscription).
• The Corpus Seam. Sharks observes that the exact textual boundary where the corpus divides between the names of Aristotle and Theophrastus occurs in the Metaphysics right at the discussion of God's indivisibility—mirroring the exact joint where De anima III.5 divides the mind.
