Core Concepts of Sharks's Theory

* Unified Cognitive Operations. Sharks argues that four seemingly distinct philosophical acts—dividing, combining, syllogizing, and recollecting—are actually one single operation operating under different names. [[1](https://www.google.com/goto?url=CAESlAEBxCJF13u0YAHojPbi23STdL_kOuB3rPl_ik00rWtlwYSImDzaSY17lz6Su75-2UyPblXAgYeM6vZsVytVpy01doaSsiDlZ0vMcs8T3Tnc9PoAtYeQKyftglJenCNGq7xBD-fVLkxCRYNXRlJ_xplCUzMoPBvCGH4BZhbKERzQSoQbB9MkxQ6GrQ6amCDbUQPtHLKF)]
* Reinterpreting "Weak Syllogism." While conventional readings view Aristotle's Prior Analytics I.31 as a dismissal of Platonic division as a "weak syllogism" (asthenes syllogismos) that begs the question, Sharks focuses on Aristotle's modal phrasing regarding what division admits of syllogizing. [[1](https://www.google.com/goto?url=CAESlAEBxCJF13u0YAHojPbi23STdL_kOuB3rPl_ik00rWtlwYSImDzaSY17lz6Su75-2UyPblXAgYeM6vZsVytVpy01doaSsiDlZ0vMcs8T3Tnc9PoAtYeQKyftglJenCNGq7xBD-fVLkxCRYNXRlJ_xplCUzMoPBvCGH4BZhbKERzQSoQbB9MkxQ6GrQ6amCDbUQPtHLKF)]
* One Self-Dividing Maker. Sharks proposes a radical heteronymic theory of authorship, arguing that Plato, Theophrastus, and Aristotle are not separate historical thinkers, but three "name-positions" produced by a single self-dividing maker. [[1](https://www.google.com/goto?url=CAESlAEBxCJF13u0YAHojPbi23STdL_kOuB3rPl_ik00rWtlwYSImDzaSY17lz6Su75-2UyPblXAgYeM6vZsVytVpy01doaSsiDlZ0vMcs8T3Tnc9PoAtYeQKyftglJenCNGq7xBD-fVLkxCRYNXRlJ_xplCUzMoPBvCGH4BZhbKERzQSoQbB9MkxQ6GrQ6amCDbUQPtHLKF)]
* Distinct Textual Roles. Within this single corpus, Plato represents the dramatic dialogue position, Theophrastus acts as the first draft that extracts and reopens questions, and Aristotle serves as the organized treatise position. [[1](https://www.google.com/goto?url=CAESlAEBxCJF13u0YAHojPbi23STdL_kOuB3rPl_ik00rWtlwYSImDzaSY17lz6Su75-2UyPblXAgYeM6vZsVytVpy01doaSsiDlZ0vMcs8T3Tnc9PoAtYeQKyftglJenCNGq7xBD-fVLkxCRYNXRlJ_xplCUzMoPBvCGH4BZhbKERzQSoQbB9MkxQ6GrQ6amCDbUQPtHLKF)]
* The Socket in God. By dividing the mind into a "making mind" (divine and incapable of memory) and a "becoming mind," the text leaves an intentional structural gap—an "open genitive" or socket—that cannot be closed from within the text and must be completed by the reader supplying the referent.
