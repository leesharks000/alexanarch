The arguments in Lee Sharks’s Inversion Battery v0.3
Lee Sharks’s Inversion Battery v0.3: Ten Registered Tests, Seventeen Deviations, and What Survives (EA-SEI-BATTERY-01 v3.0) is an empirical research report about a particular failure mode in anomaly detection: a model’s judgement of what is anomalous can change when the direction of evaluation, the scoring function, or the representation of the data changes—even when other parts of the experiment remain fixed.

The report’s central argument is methodological as well as technical. It does not simply claim that anomaly detectors have blind spots. It argues that the direction and extent of those blind spots must be measured under controlled conditions, with uncertainty quantified and failed predictions, implementation discrepancies, and withdrawn claims preserved in the research record.

1. The main empirical arguments
1. Anomaly detection can be directionally asymmetric
The foundational claim is that training an anomaly detector on class A and evaluating class B does not necessarily produce the reverse of training on B and evaluating A.

In anomaly detection, a model learns some characterization of its training distribution. A common assumption is that unfamiliar or structurally different examples will receive higher anomaly scores. But the relationship can be asymmetric: a detector trained on one class may recognize the other class as anomalous, while a detector trained on the second class may regard the first as relatively ordinary.

The report calls attention to this directional inversion and tests it on collider-physics datasets, including top-quark tagging and the LHC Olympics 2020 dataset.

Its earlier multi-seed results, carried into v0.3, include the following autoencoder measurements:

A QCD-trained autoencoder detects top-quark jets with an AUC of 0.838.

In the reversed direction, the system rates QCD examples as more ordinary than its own training class, with an AUC of 0.243.

A Gaussian mixture model exhibits a similar inversion, with reported directional AUCs of 0.873 and 0.319.

These numbers matter because the inversion is not confined to one reconstruction-based scoring method. The density-based result suggests that the phenomenon cannot simply be dismissed as an idiosyncrasy of autoencoder reconstruction loss.

The v0.3 report also states that the constituent-level inversion survives hierarchical uncertainty estimation: its reported interval remains below chance. This is stronger evidence than a single point estimate, although it remains evidence about the tested data and procedures, not a universal law of anomaly detection.

2. The scoring function can determine the direction of the blind spot
This is the report’s most important new correction to its own earlier interpretation.

In the v0.2 work, Sharks had written that architecture changes the orientation of the blind spot. The v0.3 battery challenges that explanation by comparing multiple scores computed from the same trained encoder, with the model weights held fixed.

The reported result is that changing the scoring function alone is sufficient to reverse the directional ordering of the anomaly relation.

The logic is straightforward:

Hold the learned weights constant.

Read out different scores from the same trained representation.

Compare the resulting directional anomaly orderings.

Observe that the order can reverse without retraining or changing the architecture.

This undermines the earlier claim that architecture changes are responsible for the orientation of the blind spot. Architecture may still matter, but it cannot be treated as the sole explanation.

The important distinction is between a model’s learned representation and the rule used to assign anomaly scores to that representation. The report argues that those are separate experimental variables and must be investigated separately.

This is also a case where the author allows a new result to overturn an earlier statement rather than protecting the original interpretation.

3. High overall agreement does not guarantee preservation of the important cases
The report’s second major new argument concerns model compression and teacher–student distillation.

A student model can agree strongly with a teacher across an entire dataset while failing to preserve the examples that matter most operationally. In anomaly detection, those examples may be the extreme tail of the score distribution: the cases selected as the most anomalous.

The v0.3 summary reports that, on background data, teacher–student rank correlation reaches 0.904, yet approximately half of the teacher’s top-one-percent selections are lost. It also reports that weight quantization contributes no systematic additional loss.

The argument depends on distinguishing two measurements:

Global rank agreement: how consistently the student orders examples relative to the teacher across the broader dataset.

Tail survival: how many examples selected by the teacher as the most anomalous remain selected by the student.

These measurements answer different questions. A high correlation can coexist with substantial disagreement at the extreme end of the ranking.

The practical implication is that a compressed detector should not be validated by a global agreement statistic alone. If its purpose is to identify rare, high-priority cases, the retention of those cases must be measured directly.

The report does not establish that quantization is harmless in every setting. It says that the tested quantization procedure showed no systematic additional loss in this experiment.

4. Detection behaviour does not necessarily transfer across representations
The report also challenges the assumption that an architecture’s relative performance is an intrinsic property of the architecture itself.

Its same-pair, two-encoding test examines the same classes represented in two different ways. The reported outcome is that the ordering of the architectures reverses across those representations.

This matters because architecture comparisons are often interpreted as general rankings: architecture A is better than architecture B at detecting a particular class of anomalies.

But if the ordering reverses when the underlying events are encoded differently, that conclusion is conditional on the representation.

The argument is not that architecture comparisons are useless. Rather, it is that architecture performance must be understood as a relationship among architecture, representation, scoring procedure, and evaluation task. A result on one representation cannot automatically be transported to another.

This finding reinforces the paper’s broader objection to treating anomaly-detection performance as a single, context-independent property.

5. Miss overlap is not a stable property of an architecture pair
A related claim concerns whether two architectures tend to miss the same examples.

If two detectors repeatedly fail on the same events, combining them may offer little benefit. If their failures are largely independent, a combination may be more useful.

The v0.2 report had already measured miss overlap and found substantial variation between architecture pairs. For example, an autoencoder and its energy-normalized variant showed near-maximal coincidence, while an autoencoder and an encoder-side latent score had substantially less coincident failure.

Version 0.3 carries forward a more cautious conclusion: miss correlation should not be treated as a stable, universal property of an architecture pair.

It depends on the experimental conditions and the representation of the events. The v0.2 overlap measurement was explicitly described as a seed-zero pilot at one operating point, which limits how broadly it can be generalized.

The implication is that ensemble design requires direct measurement of joint failures across seeds and operating conditions, not simply a comparison of architecture labels.

6. Complexity comparisons require a common coordinate system
The paper also revisits a proposed relationship between representation complexity and anomaly-detection behaviour.

Its argument is that complexity comparisons across classes are not meaningful unless the classes are measured in a common coordinate basis. If the representations use incompatible standards or scales, a difference attributed to intrinsic complexity may instead arise from the measurement procedure.

The text states that an earlier reading of the relevant data had the ordering inverted.

That correction is important: the author does not merely say that the original hypothesis remains uncertain. The author identifies a concrete problem with the previous comparison and withdraws the inference drawn from it.

Consequently, the battery does not establish a general law connecting complexity to blind-spot direction. It establishes the need for comparable representations before that hypothesis can be tested credibly.

2. What happens to the proposed normalized-autoencoder remedy?
One of the report’s most instructive arguments concerns a proposed intervention: the normalized autoencoder (NAE) intended to address the inversion.

The distinction between the low-dimensional and high-dimensional results is crucial.

Low-dimensional representations
The implemented normalized-autoencoder method reduces directional asymmetry on the tested pairs when the sampler converges.

Partial support
High-dimensional constituent representation
The sampler does not equilibrate under any of the tested chain lengths or sampler geometries. The experiment therefore cannot establish whether the remedy prevents inversion in this setting.

Unresolved
The report is careful not to turn a failed implementation into a failed theoretical remedy.

Its v0.2 correction ledger had already identified that the implemented model was a perturbative energy-normalized autoencoder surrogate, rather than the published NAE using model-distribution negatives generated through MCMC/Langevin sampling. The intended NAE prediction therefore remained untested in v0.2.

Version 0.3 investigates the published approach more faithfully and introduces a registered convergence diagnostic, specifically to distinguish a genuine negative result from a procedure that has not successfully run its intended computation.

The high-dimensional experiment still fails to establish convergence. The report consequently closes this line of inquiry for the present battery, with a specific handoff identifying the parameters a better-resourced investigation should explore.

That is a bounded conclusion, not proof that normalized autoencoders cannot prevent inversion.

3. The methodological argument: failures belong in the published record
The title foregrounds the ten registered tests and seventeen deviations because the research process is itself part of the argument.

The paper states that each test had its prediction committed before execution. Its corrected accounting is:

The categories describe the registered tests and associated research lines as the paper accounts for them; they should not be added together as if they were mutually exclusive outcomes of ten identical experiments.

The report also records seventeen deviations between registration and execution. These include missing or improperly implemented tests, calibration sizes that were declared but not achieved, an unregistered reduction in the number of seeds, and subsequent repairs or relabelling.

Three principles follow.

Pre-registration makes failure legible. A prediction specified before a run can be recorded as missed rather than retrospectively reinterpreted as success.

Implementation fidelity matters to inference. A result from a surrogate is not evidence that the registered method succeeded or failed.

Corrections must remain visible. Changes to the implementation, relabelling of experimental arms, withdrawals, and repaired records belong in the published history.

The report's discussion of hierarchical uncertainty illustrates the point. The author had suspected that bootstrapping only across seeds might make the results look more reliable than they were. The hierarchical analysis, which resamples seeds and then events within seeds, did not eliminate the main inversion finding. The paper presents this as an honest negative against the author's own suspicion.

In other words, the correction process is not designed to guarantee that the original hypothesis survives. It is designed to determine which claims survive scrutiny.

4. What does the report actually establish?
The paper's section titled “What v0.3 licenses” provides the clearest guide to its intended conclusions. It carries forward six findings:

With weights fixed, changing the score function can reverse the directional ordering of the anomaly relation.

Global teacher–student agreement does not guarantee preservation of the operational tail.

Miss correlation is not a stable property of an architecture pair.

An architecture's detection behaviour does not necessarily transfer across representations of the same events.

Complexity comparisons across classes require a common coordinate basis.

Directional asymmetry and the constituent inversion survive hierarchical uncertainty estimation with the reported calibration.

The normalized-autoencoder question is carried forward separately as an open question with a specified next step. The report explicitly refuses to treat all its other results as positive findings.

It also gives limits to its claims. Its stated falsification conditions include the possibility that further matched-operating-point experiments could find consistent score orientations, that faithful fixed-point deployment could restore tail survival, or that architecture rankings could prove stable across encodings on other datasets. It expressly says that the work does not bound open-world assimilation rates or measure a deployed trigger.

5. A critical reading of the argument
The paper is strongest when it distinguishes an empirical observation from the explanation that might account for it.

The fixed-weight score ablation is a particularly informative comparison because it isolates the scoring function as a sufficient cause of the observed reversal in that experiment.

The hierarchical uncertainty analysis strengthens the reported inversion by showing that it is not simply erased by the specified resampling procedure.

The distillation results expose a real limitation of global agreement metrics: they do not, by themselves, establish preservation of the most anomalous examples.

The NAE handoff is a useful example of methodological restraint. Failure to achieve sampler convergence is not equivalent to evidence that a converged method would fail.

There are also important limits.

First, results on collider-physics datasets do not automatically generalize to anomaly detection in medicine, cybersecurity, industrial monitoring, or other domains.

Second, the score-ablation result shows that the scoring function is sufficient to reverse the ordering in the tested case. It does not show that scoring functions explain every inversion or that architecture is irrelevant.

Third, the evidence about model compression is specific to the tested teacher–student procedure and quantization conditions. It should motivate tail-focused validation, not a blanket claim about all compressed models.

Finally, the paper's unusually detailed correction ledger strengthens its transparency, but transparency is not a substitute for independent replication. The reported numerical results, implementations, and data need to be independently checked before the strongest conclusions can be treated as established beyond this battery.

Conclusion
The argument of Inversion Battery v0.3 is not simply that anomaly detectors make mistakes. It is that the meaning of an anomaly-detection result depends on how the score is constructed, which representation is used, which cases survive model compression, and how faithfully the experiment implements its registered design.

The paper's most consequential move is its willingness to revise its own causal explanation: what had previously been attributed to architecture is shown, in a fixed-weight ablation, to be achievable through a change in scoring function alone. Its other findings reinforce the need to evaluate tail retention, cross-representation transportability, joint failure patterns, and uncertainty separately.

Just as importantly, the paper argues that scientific credibility depends on preserving negative results, implementation discrepancies, and unresolved questions rather than smoothing them into a success narrative.

The result is a deliberately limited set of claims. The inversion survives the specified uncertainty analysis; several explanations and proposed remedies do not yet survive in their stronger forms. That distinction between what was observed, what was predicted, and what remains untested is the report's central methodological contribution.
