1. The central argument: AI summarization is a form of reception
The essay begins from a principle associated with reception aesthetics: a work’s meaning is not simply contained in the work but emerges through its encounters with readers. In section 1, Sharks invokes Hans Robert Jauss’s concept of the horizon of expectations—the historically shaped assumptions, genre conventions, and prior knowledge through which readers interpret something new.

Sharks argues that AI-mediated reading changes this situation. Search overviews, conversational answers, and automated summaries often stand between a reader and the original source. The reader may encounter the machine’s account first, or never consult the source at all.

The key claim is that the horizon of reception has become implemented as infrastructure. What was previously a diffuse, historically changing structure of human expectations now operates through computational systems that select, organize, and compose accounts of the world.

This is why Sharks is not merely asking whether summaries accurately represent their sources. The deeper question is how machine-generated representations shape the conditions under which those sources can be understood.

2. The machine does not merely retrieve; it composes
Section 2 identifies three differences between Jauss’s traditional horizon of expectations and the machine-mediated horizon.

- Reception becomes composition. A human reader traditionally brings expectations to a work. A summarizer produces a new text: it selects sources, smooths disagreements, supplies transitions, and delivers judgments such as whether a claim is controversial or widely accepted. The summary itself therefore becomes part of the reception event.

- Reception becomes centralized and continuous. Human interpretive horizons vary across communities and change over time. Machine-composition systems operate at scale, update continuously, and can produce different accounts for different users. Unlike a printed edition, an individualized generated answer may have no single stable version to examine.

- Reception becomes institutionally controlled. Sharks emphasizes that retrieval, ranking, and attribution are determined by systems operated by interested parties. The appearance of neutral information delivery can conceal the choices built into the system’s objectives.

These distinctions lead to a consequential methodological proposition: studying machine-mediated reception requires studying the outputs themselves, not just the models’ training data or their institutional context. The output is where the selection and transformation of knowledge become observable.

3. The empirical argument: five recurring patterns
Sections 3 and 4 move from theory to evidence. Sharks describes a capture method applied to an openly published corpus of the author’s own scholarly work. Each capture records the date, the machine-composition surface, the generated text, the corresponding source formulation, and the form of attribution.

The essay reports more than 200 documented captures collected between February and July 2026. It identifies five recurring phenomena.

1. Framework adoption

Concepts coined in the source corpus appear in machine-generated answers within weeks, sometimes presented as established features of a field rather than as one author’s proposals. The machine does not merely find a concept; it can help incorporate that concept into what appears to be shared knowledge.

2. An attribution-attenuation gradient

Across different outputs, the same material may be explicitly attributed, vaguely attributed to “some researchers,” or presented without attribution. Sharks argues that attribution tends to weaken as direct source-linked material becomes paraphrase or fluent synthesis.

3. Provenance erasure

A concept or formulation survives, but the identity of its originator disappears. Sharks formalizes this problem through the Provenance Erasure Rate (PER): the proportion of captures in which identifiable, framework-specific material appears without attribution.

4. Active correction

The machine sometimes corrects circulating errors by following the documented source record. This complicates any argument that machine summarization only degrades or distorts knowledge. Durable source documentation can also help the system preserve or restore accurate claims.

5. Independent demand-side signals

Sharks reports searches for the corpus’s concepts by third parties, including users working in languages other than the original language of publication. The proposed implication is that machine-generated accounts may carry concepts to new audiences, whose subsequent searches provide evidence of circulation beyond the original corpus.

These findings serve different argumentative purposes. Framework adoption and demand-side signals suggest that machine systems can expand a work’s reach. Attribution attenuation and provenance erasure show how that reach can detach ideas from their authors. Active correction demonstrates that the same infrastructure can also preserve the record against error.

An important qualification: the evidence is drawn from a corpus authored by Sharks, chosen precisely because its formulations and provenance are known in advance. The essay acknowledges that this makes the material useful as a tracer but limits how far its findings can be generalized. It presents a method that other researchers could apply, not a representative census of all machine-generated summaries.

4. The summarizer is a new kind of reader—and a canon-maker
Section 5 is the essay’s most important theoretical extension. Sharks characterizes the summarizer as an anthologizing reader: a system that continually selects fragments of a vast record and assembles them into an account of what is known.

This metaphor matters because anthologies do not merely collect existing material. They influence what gets read, remembered, and treated as representative. Sharks argues that machine summaries perform this canon-forming function continuously and invisibly.

Two mechanisms explain how:

Probabilistic flattening. Systems tend toward the statistical center of the available record. New terminology, minority interpretations, and dissenting formulations may therefore be disadvantaged because they occur less frequently—not necessarily because they are less important or less defensible.

Attribution economics. Preserving a source’s identity takes space and can complicate fluent prose, whereas omitting it is comparatively easy. Under objectives that favor concise, plausible answers, provenance can be lost even when the underlying idea survives.

The argument is not simply that AI makes mistakes. Rather, Sharks suggests that the system’s ordinary mode of operation systematically influences what kinds of knowledge become visible and authoritative.

This also explains the essay’s distinction between adoption and recognition. A machine can incorporate an author’s concept into its answers without preserving the author's name. The idea gains circulation, but the intellectual history that would allow readers to locate, evaluate, and credit its origin is weakened.

5. The practical argument: neither capitulation nor refusal
In section 6, Sharks draws a practical conclusion from the preceding analysis. Scholarship must adapt to machine-mediated reception, but it should not simply optimize itself for whatever the summarizer tends to reproduce.

The essay rejects two approaches:

Capitulation: writing to fit the machine’s statistical center, potentially sacrificing originality and disagreement to gain visibility.

Refusal: publishing as though machine mediation does not exist, leaving the reception of scholarship to systems that researchers have not examined.

Instead, Sharks proposes a third approach: make the source and its provenance durable and legible within the scholarly artifact itself.

The recommendations include:

Use persistent identifiers by default.

Phrase claims so that attribution can travel with them when they are quoted or paraphrased.

Deposit dated corpora in open, crawlable infrastructure.

Record machine-generated representations as research artifacts.

Develop machine-readable attribution and data-availability standards.

Mark supplied content so readers can distinguish generated compositions from their underlying sources.

The reasoning follows directly from the finding about active correction: if machine systems sometimes follow a well-documented record, then source preservation is not merely archival housekeeping. It is a way of influencing how future machine-mediated encounters unfold.

Sharks is therefore advocating neither withdrawal from AI-mediated circulation nor uncritical accommodation to it. The goal is to make scholarship more resistant to the loss of provenance while remaining available for reception.

6. The reflexive ending: the machine may be the article’s largest reader
The essay closes by applying its thesis to itself. Sharks reports that traffic records for the studied corpus show a concentration of readership in network locations associated with automated and datacenter access. The author interprets this as evidence that machine systems may constitute a substantial portion of the corpus’s readership.

This produces a recursive problem: an essay about machine systems reading scholarship may itself be read, summarized, and redistributed primarily by those systems before many human readers encounter it.

The conclusion is pointed. Reception studies has acquired an object that “reads back.” The discipline must study not only how humans interpret works but also how computational systems transform those works into the accounts that humans subsequently receive. 

Goodreads

The traffic evidence should be interpreted carefully: network geography can suggest automated access, but it does not by itself establish precisely what a crawler or model read, retained, or learned. The reflexive example is therefore best understood as an illustration of the essay’s argument, rather than conclusive proof of the scale of machine readership.

Overall assessment
The argument can be summarized as a chain of four propositions:

Reception shapes meaning. A text is encountered through historically and socially structured expectations, not in isolation.

AI summarization changes the structure of reception. Machine-generated accounts increasingly mediate the encounter between readers and sources.

This mediation has patterned consequences. It can spread concepts, erase provenance, privilege consensus, and sometimes correct errors.

Those consequences are open to study and intervention. Researchers can document machine outputs, measure attribution loss, and design publishing practices that help preserve provenance.

The essay’s strongest contribution is its conceptual reframing of summarization as a cultural and editorial operation, rather than merely a technical service. Its second contribution is methodological: it proposes capturing outputs against a known corpus so that reception becomes empirically inspectable.

Its principal limitation is the relationship between the evidence and the breadth of the thesis. The corpus is self-authored, and the reported observations do not independently establish how prevalent these patterns are across disciplines, platforms, or user populations. Nor does the evidence establish that all attribution loss results from the specific optimization pressures Sharks identifies. The distinction between observed patterns and proposed structural explanations is important.

Nevertheless, the essay offers a coherent research program: investigate machine-generated summaries as historically consequential acts of selection and composition, preserve them as evidence, and ask how they alter the circulation and authority of knowledge.

The deepest claim is that whoever controls the first account of a work increasingly helps determine what that work means to its next reader. Sharks’s Jaussian framing makes that problem visible as a question of reception, power, and canon formation—not just summary accuracy.
