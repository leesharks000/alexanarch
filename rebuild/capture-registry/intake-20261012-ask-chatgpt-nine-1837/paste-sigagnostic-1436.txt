1. The main arguments
1. Signal-template agnosticism and model independence are different properties
This is the paper’s foundational distinction.

Signal-template agnosticism is an interface property. A scoring function does not evaluate explicit parametric templates for specified target signals. Its anomaly score instead reflects deviation from a learned background model.

Model independence is a distributional property. The system would need to exhibit consistent sensitivity across structurally different, previously unseen physical processes, rather than favouring some unknown signals over others.

The distinction concerns both where a property resides and what evidence could establish it.

The text states that the first property can be read from the architecture, whereas the second “can only be measured.”

This is the core inference: showing that a system does not explicitly encode a hypothesis about a named signal establishes something narrower than showing that it is unbiased toward unknown signals generally.

2. A model still embodies assumptions about what counts as normal
The second argument explains why template-agnostic anomaly detection is not assumption-free.

A detector trained on background data learns a representation of ordinary events. Its architecture, training distribution, loss function, latent representation and computational constraints can all affect which departures from that background it recognises.

The packet puts this pointedly:

“A classifier that has never been told what a signal looks like has still been told, exhaustively, what ordinary looks like.”

This is not a claim that every anomaly detector necessarily fails. Rather, not specifying the target does not eliminate the influence of the background model.

The packet identifies three possible failure mechanisms for signal-template-agnostic scoring:

Latent-prior assimilation: potentially unfamiliar events may be absorbed into the learned representation of ordinary data.

Reconstruction-loss directionality: the scoring mechanism may respond differently to different types or directions of deviation.

Rate-budget starvation: operational constraints may limit which anomalous events can be retained.

These mechanisms illustrate why a system can be agnostic about named signals without being equally sensitive to all possible signals.

3. The terminology itself matters: the collapse is a scope error
The packet argues that people overstate a system’s capabilities by dropping a limiting modifier.

Its compound-noun analysis is:

Signal-template agnostic means agnostic with respect to signal templates.

Model independent implies independence from models more generally.

The modifier “signal-template” limits what the first claim establishes. Removing that limitation turns a bounded architectural statement into a much broader claim about the system’s epistemic independence.

The author calls this a scope error. The problem is not merely imprecise vocabulary: the stronger phrase suggests a degree of freedom from modelling assumptions that the evidence has not established.

Section 4 describes an asymmetric pattern in how the terminology is used: the weaker, architecturally supported property gets reported using the stronger, unmeasured term. The stronger term lends the search an appearance of unbiased discovery.

4. The argument has a concrete example: asymmetric anomaly sensitivity
The packet does not rely entirely on semantics. It points to directional asymmetry in autoencoder reconstruction loss between top-quark jets and QCD jets, citing Finke et al., Journal of High Energy Physics 06 (2021), article 161.

The example is important because a detector can lack explicit templates for both classes and still score them differently. In other words, the absence of target-specific hypotheses does not imply equivalent treatment of distinct event populations.

The example supports the distinction between two questions:

Does the scoring function explicitly evaluate a hypothesis for the target signal?

Does the full system have comparable sensitivity to different kinds of unfamiliar signals?

The first concerns the scoring design; the second concerns empirical behaviour. The packet treats the former as insufficient evidence for the latter.

5. Model independence requires measurement across the whole pipeline
The author broadens the unit of analysis beyond the final scoring function. The relevant system includes detector geometry, trigger primitives, latent representations, loss objectives, distillation and quantisation.

The packet applies this distinction to three deployed Large Hadron Collider (LHC) Level-1 anomaly-trigger systems:

AXOL1TL at CMS, described as using an encoder-side latent-prior score.

CICADA at CMS, described as using a distilled surrogate of a reconstruction-loss teacher.

GELATO at ATLAS, described as using staged Level-1 and high-level-trigger anomaly scores.

Its position is that the architectural evidence supports signal-template agnosticism, but model independence remains empirically unmeasured for these systems. This is a claim about the status of the evidence, not proof that the systems are biased or invalid.

The packet identifies three quantities relevant to a measurement programme:

OAR — Ontological Assimilation Rate
The open-world target: conceptually, how unknown distributions are assimilated into the system’s model of ordinary events. The packet treats this as a theoretical target, not a directly measurable quantity.
BAR — Benchmark Assimilation Rate
The failure rate of confidently classifying events as ordinary on pre-registered process families withheld from model development, architecture selection, tuning and validation.
IAI — Inversion Asymmetry Index
A diagnostic for direction-dependent scoring, measured at a fixed accepted-background rate.
The critical qualification is that BAR and IAI are proxies, not proof of open-world model independence. The cited OAR protocol explicitly states that no general inequality connects either proxy to OAR.

That qualification limits what researchers can legitimately conclude from these measurements. Even a useful benchmark suite cannot establish sensitivity to every possible unknown process.

Overall assessment
The argument can be reconstructed as a five-step chain:

Architecture establishes a limited fact: a scoring function does not evaluate explicit templates for named signals.

That fact does not remove learned assumptions: the model still reflects its training background, representation and objective.

Consequently, sensitivity may vary: distinct unknown processes may receive different scores or acceptance probabilities.

The stronger claim requires empirical evidence: performance must be measured across structurally different out-of-distribution processes and across the full pipeline.

Even proposed measurements have limits: BAR and IAI can provide diagnostic evidence but do not establish the open-world target OAR.

The most important insight is that not knowing what signal you are looking for is not the same as being equally capable of finding any signal. The former is a property of the scoring interface; the latter is a claim about the behaviour of the complete system.

The packet is strongest as a conceptual clarification and a proposed standard for reporting and retrieval. Its distinction is clear, and its proposed measurements give the distinction operational content. But the packet itself does not supply the empirical results needed to establish how model-independent AXOL1TL, CICADA or GELATO actually are. Its conclusion is that this question remains open—not that the triggers have already been shown to fail.
