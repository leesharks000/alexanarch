Lee Sharks’s “After the Obelus: Marx’s Manuscripts, Machine Composition, and the Politics of Interpretive Supply” argues that the central political problem of machine-generated answers is not simply whether they are accurate, but whether readers can see, identify, and contest the interpretive decisions that produced them.

The essay develops this argument through a comparison between classical textual criticism, the editorial history of Marx’s Economic and Philosophic Manuscripts of 1844, and contemporary machine-generated summaries and answers. Its central distinction is between marked supply, in which an intermediary makes interpretive interventions visible, and unmarked supply, in which those interventions appear to be properties of the source itself.

The argument is best understood as a sequence of claims, each building on the previous one.

1. All texts involve interpretation; the question is whether that interpretation is visible
The essay begins with the premise that there is no such thing as an entirely unsupplied text. Translators choose equivalences, editors arrange fragments, scholars reconstruct damaged passages, and anthologists decide what belongs in a canon. Even punctuation can affect meaning.

Sharks’s point is that these interventions are not necessarily defects. They are unavoidable parts of transmitting and interpreting texts. The ethical question is whether the people who make them acknowledge their work.

The essay introduces the distinction through the obelus, an editorial mark used in ancient scholarship to indicate a passage whose authenticity or textual standing was doubted. In Sharks’s account, the obelus preserves a passage while making the editor’s uncertainty visible. It does not resolve the problem for the reader; it gives the reader the information needed to judge it.

This establishes the essay’s governing principle:

Marked supply: Interpretive interventions are disclosed and can be examined, challenged, and revised.

Unmarked supply: Interpretive interventions are concealed, allowing an intermediary’s judgments to circulate as if they came directly from the original source.

Sharks argues that an editorial apparatus does more than document technical decisions. It establishes the conditions under which an authority can be held accountable. An edition becomes more trustworthy not because it eliminates interpretation, but because it makes interpretation inspectable.

2. The editorial history of Marx’s 1844 manuscripts demonstrates that textual mediation shapes intellectual history
The essay’s central historical case concerns Marx’s Economic and Philosophic Manuscripts of 1844. Sharks uses their complicated transmission to demonstrate that editorial decisions can influence what an entire intellectual tradition believes a thinker argued.

Three features of the manuscript history matter to the argument.

First, the manuscripts are materially incomplete. The second manuscript is largely missing, and surviving portions of the third contain references to pages that no longer exist. Consequently, the surviving archive cannot simply be read as a complete, continuous argument.

Second, Sharks contrasts two editions published in 1932. The essay presents the Moscow edition associated with the Marx-Engels Institute as making its editorial procedures visible, while the rival Landshut–Mayer edition omitted material, rearranged the surviving texts, and incorporated a transcription of Hegel as though it were a manuscript by Marx.

The critical issue is not merely that one edition made mistakes. It is that the latter edition, according to Sharks, failed to identify its own interventions adequately, allowing editorial construction to masquerade as primary evidence.

Third, the essay connects these editorial decisions to subsequent debates over the relationship between the young, humanist Marx and the mature Marx of political economy. If the texts used to establish continuity or discontinuity between different periods of Marx’s thought have already been shaped by editors, then the philosophical conclusions drawn from them may depend partly on editorial choices that remain unacknowledged.

Sharks is careful to frame the relationship as a problem of interpretation rather than claiming that editorial history alone explains the debate. The argument is that the received textual object helps determine which interpretations become plausible.

The broader claim: Archives do not merely preserve intellectual history. The ways they are assembled and presented help produce it.

3. Machine-generated answers reproduce the problem of unmarked editorial intervention at a new scale
Having established that textual transmission always involves interpretation, Sharks turns to machine composition.

The essay argues that systems generating summaries, overviews, and answers perform many of the same operations as editors:

They select some sources and exclude others.

They reconcile conflicting accounts into apparently coherent narratives.

They supply transitions and causal connections that may not appear in any source.

They arrange evidence into an order that implies an argument.

They characterize claims as widely accepted, controversial, or discredited.

They determine which contributions receive attribution and which become anonymous information.

Sharks’s objection is not that every such decision is wrong. It is that these decisions are generally presented through a fluent answer that conceals the process of composition.

The result is what the essay calls supply laundered into source: an intermediary introduces an interpretation, but the reader encounters that interpretation as though it were simply present in the underlying record.

This is why Sharks considers the machine interface itself significant. If the answer provides no clear way to distinguish source material from synthesis, reconstruction, or editorial judgment, readers may have difficulty locating the precise point at which an interpretation entered the account.

The comparison with the obelus is therefore structural. Classical textual criticism developed visible ways to identify editorial uncertainty and intervention. Machine composition, Sharks argues, has developed extraordinarily powerful ways to compose and reorganize knowledge without comparable marks of custody.

The historical contrast is not between human beings who interpret and machines that interpret. It is between different arrangements of interpretive accountability.

4. Marx’s theory of alienation explains why this is a political problem, even when the answer is correct
This is the essay’s most ambitious conceptual move.

Sharks argues that machine-generated answers reorganize collective intellectual labor. Their material comes from accumulated human activities: writing, research, criticism, correction, argument, and the development of shared concepts. Yet the answer returns that collective labor as an apparently autonomous product of the machine.

Sharks interprets this through Marx’s concept of alienation: people’s products confront them as an independent power, seemingly detached from the activity and social relations that produced them.

The analogy operates at the level of knowledge production:

Collective intellectual activity produces a shared inheritance of language and meaning.

A machine-composition system draws on that inheritance and reorganizes it into an answer.

The resulting answer appears to speak with an independent authority.

The people whose work made the answer possible may become invisible, while the system controls how their contributions are selected, attributed, and presented.

The crucial point is Sharks’s claim that alienation is orthogonal to accuracy. In other words, correctness and accountability are separate dimensions.

An answer can be factually correct while still concealing its provenance, obscuring the labor behind it, and preventing readers from challenging the interpretive choices through which it was constructed.

This distinction matters because a conventional critique of misinformation cannot fully address the problem. Fact-checking can establish whether a statement is true, but it cannot by itself establish who supplied its framing, whose work has been absorbed, or who has the authority to correct the result.

Sharks thus shifts the question from Is this answer true? to a further question: Who made this answer possible, how was it composed, and how can its composition be contested?

5. Machine composition encloses the intellectual commons
The essay then expands the argument from interpretation to political economy.

Sharks describes machine composition as an enclosure of collective intellectual resources. Language, arguments, and accumulated knowledge constitute a shared inheritance, but systems can absorb these resources as inputs and return them through privately controlled services.

The problem is not simply that individual texts might be appropriated. It is that the power to organize meaning itself becomes concentrated in systems that determine:

What information can be retrieved.

Which sources receive visibility or attribution.

How disagreements are resolved or suppressed in a synthesized answer.

Which interpretations are presented as authoritative.

What readers can inspect, challenge, or correct.

Sharks connects this arrangement to Marx’s idea of the general intellect: the collective capacities and accumulated knowledge of society that become productive forces.

In the essay’s formulation, the general intellect is placed under external command when shared intellectual labor is made operational through infrastructures whose owners control the conditions of its use.

This explains the significance of the essay’s question, “Who commands the means of composition?” It extends the familiar Marxist question of who controls the means of production to the systems that increasingly mediate access to knowledge.

The argument is therefore about more than authors receiving credit. Attribution is one part of a broader struggle over control of the infrastructure through which collective knowledge is transformed into publicly authoritative meaning.

6. The answer is not to abandon machine interpretation, but to build a counter-archive
Sharks does not conclude that machine-generated interpretation should end. The essay explicitly rejects a return to supposedly unmediated human reading, because its foundational premise is that all reading and textual transmission involve mediation.

Instead, it proposes a counter-archive: a set of practices and infrastructures designed to preserve provenance, make interpretive interventions visible, and allow the resulting compositions to be challenged.

The proposed practices include:

Persistent identifiers connecting claims and formulations to their producers.

Records structured to preserve provenance when material is summarized or recombined.

Documentation of how intellectual material is reproduced or transformed.

Explicit disclosure when missing evidence has been reconstructed.

Rights of correction and reply for people whose work is absorbed or whose lives are described.

Public oversight of ranking and retrieval functions that determine what becomes visible.

These proposals extend the obelus from a mark on a page into an infrastructure for accountable interpretation.

The essay also offers a more dialectical argument: machine systems built for extraction may nevertheless transmit criticism of their own operations. Sharks reports three recurring phenomena in a research corpus of more than 130 documented machine-composition captures: the reappearance of a theoretical framework in generated accounts, the circulation of its formulations with attribution stripped, and instances in which a system reproduces a correction supported by deposited evidence.

These examples are presented as evidence that the machine substrate is not identical to the interests governing it. The infrastructure can reproduce existing power relations, but it can also carry materials that expose or challenge them.

The counter-archive seeks to exploit that possibility. Rather than refusing mediation, it attempts to organize mediation so that it remains answerable to the people and evidence on which it depends.

7. The humanities must take responsibility for the systems that now mediate interpretation
The conclusion turns the argument back on textual scholars and the humanities.

According to Sharks, these disciplines have long established that texts are constructed, archives are selective, and interpretation has political consequences. Yet the institutions that developed these insights have not adequately asserted authority over the machine systems that now perform interpretive work on a vast scale.

Sharks therefore calls on the humanities to move beyond commentary about technological change and participate in governing how machine composition works.

The demands follow directly from the essay’s earlier argument: provenance should be a default rather than an optional feature; machine-generated synthesis should be open to inspection and correction; and the people whose work is incorporated into an answer should retain meaningful opportunities to contest its representation.

The essay’s final return to the obelus makes its position clear. The mark is not merely a historical curiosity or a symbol of scholarly caution. It represents an established principle of accountability that could be extended to contemporary knowledge infrastructure.

The alternative is a scholarly culture that continues to analyze the politics of interpretation while allowing increasingly powerful intermediaries to determine those politics in practice.

How the argument holds together
The essay’s structure can be summarized as follows:

1. Philological premise
All texts involve interpretive supply; responsible mediation makes that supply visible.
2. Historical demonstration
The editorial history of Marx’s 1844 manuscripts shows how unmarked interventions can shape philosophical debate.
3. Contemporary diagnosis
Machine composition performs similar interventions while obscuring the process that produced its answers.
4. Marxist interpretation
This obscurity alienates collective intellectual labor and concentrates control over the production of meaning.
5. Political response
A counter-archive would make machine-mediated interpretation traceable, contestable, and accountable.
A critical assessment of the text itself
A close reading should distinguish the essay’s conceptual argument from the strength of its historical and empirical evidence.

Its strongest conceptual contribution is the distinction between accuracy and interpretive accountability. This allows Sharks to explain why a system can produce correct information and still pose a political problem: the issue is not just what the answer says, but how it acquires authority and whether the process behind it can be challenged.

Its most important historical move is using Marx’s manuscripts as more than an illustration of editorial error. The manuscript’s physical incompleteness and contested editorial history make the case structurally analogous to machine composition: in both situations, the reader receives an intellectual object shaped by intermediaries. The analogy gives the argument historical depth, although it does not, by itself, prove that the two situations are equivalent in every respect.

Its most ambitious theoretical move is to reinterpret alienation as a problem of collective semantic labor. This is a productive extension of Marx’s framework, but the analogy needs further argument to establish exactly how the alienation of a machine-generated answer relates to the economic and social relations Marx originally analyzed. Sharks acknowledges that the fuller development of this theory appears in a companion study rather than in the essay itself.

Its principal evidentiary limitation is that the empirical claims about machine-composition captures are not fully verifiable from the essay as presented. Note 12 describes a corpus of more than 130 cases and says that the corpus and methods are available from the author, but the essay does not provide the underlying dataset or enough methodological detail to independently assess the findings. Its claims about the 1932 editions likewise depend on cited scholarship that would need to be checked against the original bibliographical evidence.

These limitations do not invalidate the conceptual argument, but they matter when distinguishing an interpretive thesis from an empirically demonstrated conclusion.

In one sentence
Sharks argues that the politics of machine-generated knowledge turns on who controls the interpretive labor between sources and answers—and that the solution is not to eliminate mediation, but to make it visible, attributable, and open to contest.

The essay’s deepest claim is that the old philological demand to mark editorial intervention has become a political demand for our own time: if machines are going to compose the texts through which we understand the world, their acts of composition must be made accountable to the people whose collective intellectual labor makes them possible.
