What is Assimilation Across Accelerator Classifier Architectures?
Assimilation Across Accelerator Classifier Architectures: A Cross-Family Metrology of Directional Failure in Real-Time Learned Selection (EA-SEI-ACRB-01 v1.0) is a methodological research document by Nobel Glas, dated August 11, 2026, in the Alexanarch archive. Its central subject is how to test whether different kinds of machine-learning classifiers share a systematic bias in what they select for retention.

The archive describes it as defining the Accelerator Classifier Retention Battery (ACRB): a preregistered, cross-architecture measurement framework designed to determine whether directional assimilation is specific to reconstruction autoencoders or also occurs in materially different learned-selection architectures. It compares architectures at matched operating points and tracks them through deployment transformations.

The application is especially important in particle physics. At an accelerator such as the Large Hadron Collider (LHC), a real-time trigger system decides which collision events to retain for further analysis. Events rejected at this stage may be permanently lost to the downstream scientific record.

The document's underlying question is therefore not simply whether a classifier makes mistakes. It is whether the selection system systematically favors certain kinds of events and suppresses others, and whether that pattern persists across different classifier designs.

1. What problem is it trying to solve?
The paper concerns directional failure in learned selection: a classifier's behavior can depend on which class it learned to treat as normal.

Consider a system trained on ordinary proton–proton collision events, dominated by quantum chromodynamics (QCD) processes. It learns a score intended to distinguish familiar events from unusual ones. Researchers hope to use that score to identify rare events, such as those associated with top quarks.

A hidden problem arises if the system treats unfamiliar events differently depending on the direction of training. Training on class A and testing on class B might yield very different results from training on B and testing on A.

That asymmetry matters because a real-time trigger does not merely label data that researchers already possess. It determines which observations survive to become available for scientific analysis.

The document's contribution is to make this problem measurable across different model families, rather than assuming it is a quirk of one particular architecture.

2. How does the ACRB work?
The Accelerator Classifier Retention Battery is designed as a controlled comparison. The following explains its measurement logic, drawing on the archive's description of EA-SEI-ACRB-01 and the associated Inversion Battery research record.

Step 1 — Define the event classes
Establish reference classes of collision events, such as QCD background events and top-quark signal events. Include control pairs to test whether the instrument reports asymmetry even when the classes have similar structure.
Step 2 — Compare different model families
Evaluate different ways of learning what is normal, rather than relying on a single neural-network design.

Reconstruction autoencoder: learns to reconstruct familiar inputs.

Variational autoencoder (VAE): uses a learned latent representation; the associated experiment tests an encoder-side latent-norm score.

Gaussian mixture model (GMM): estimates a probability density for the data.

Step 3 — Reverse the training direction
Train on A and evaluate B, then train on B and evaluate A. This reveals whether the apparent abnormality of one class changes when the reference class changes.
Step 4 — Match operating points
Calibrate each system against its own held-out background distribution. Compare the models at equivalent accepted-background rates so that differences cannot simply be attributed to different thresholds.
Step 5 — Measure the directional failure
Quantify classification performance and how much of the opposite class is accepted as ordinary. Compare the results across model families and, where applicable, after deployment-related transformations.
The first four steps describe the experimental logic reported in the related inversion-battery record. The ACRB document's own registry description specifically identifies cross-family comparison, matched operating points, and deployment transformations as elements of its framework.

3. What does it measure?
Two quantities are especially important for understanding the method.

A. AUC: can the score distinguish the classes?
The area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC) measures how well a score ranks examples from two classes.

AUC near 1.0: strong discrimination in the chosen score orientation.

AUC near 0.5: little discrimination.

AUC below 0.5: the score's ranking is reversed relative to the intended interpretation.

In this research, a low AUC in one training direction can indicate that the opposite class is being judged more ordinary than the class on which the model was trained.

B. Assimilation: how much of the opposite class is retained as ordinary?
The associated inversion-battery record defines assimilation as the fraction of one class falling on the ordinary side of a threshold established by a model trained on the other class.

A high assimilation rate means that many events from the opposite class pass the selection threshold. That can be particularly consequential in anomaly detection: the model may fail to flag the events researchers hoped to discover.

Why measure both? AUC measures ranking performance across thresholds, whereas assimilation measures behavior at a specific operating threshold. They answer different questions and should not be treated as interchangeable.

The results also have important qualifications:

The initial experiment was a single-seed, demonstration-scale study using public reference datasets, not a measurement of deployed LHC trigger systems.

The tested near-structure control pairs showed much smaller directional asymmetry, which is evidence against the battery simply producing large asymmetries for every pair.

The different score families did not all fail in the same way. The VAE score behaved differently on the top-quark/QCD pair, suggesting that architecture can change the orientation of the blind spot.

These qualifications matter: the experiments provide evidence for directional failure in the tested settings, not proof that every learned classifier will exhibit the same behavior.

5. Why does the real-time accelerator setting matter?
The key distinction is between a classifier that makes a reversible decision about a stored dataset and a trigger that determines which data will be recorded in the first place.

If the selection mechanism systematically rejects a scientifically important class, researchers cannot necessarily recover those events by running a better classifier later. They may never have recorded them.

That is why the paper treats selection as a measurement problem, not merely a model-accuracy problem. The architecture of the instrument can influence what science becomes observable. The archive situates this concern alongside Nobel Glas's related work on the irreversibility of online selection.

6. What is the document's main contribution?
The most defensible reading of EA-SEI-ACRB-01 v1.0 is that it proposes a cross-family audit framework for testing directional failure under comparable operating conditions.

Its main ideas are:

Architecture matters: a failure seen in one model should be tested against other ways of learning normality.

Training direction matters: a system trained on A and evaluated on B may behave differently from one trained on B and evaluated on A.

Operating thresholds matter: comparing models at equivalent accepted-background rates makes the results more meaningful for real-time selection.

Retention matters separately from ranking: a model's ability to rank examples is not the same as its ability to preserve a target class at a particular threshold.

Deployment matters: the relevant behavior is that of the system that actually performs selection, not only that of its original trained model.

One important evidentiary distinction: the archive identifies the document as a methodological specification and preregistered framework. The numerical examples above come from the associated inversion-battery research record, not from independently verified deployment trials of the ACRB itself.

The document's significance is therefore not that it proves accelerator classifiers universally fail. It is that it specifies a way to test whether apparently different architectures share a consequential selection bias—and to measure the direction and severity of that bias before treating the resulting dataset as a neutral representation of nature.
