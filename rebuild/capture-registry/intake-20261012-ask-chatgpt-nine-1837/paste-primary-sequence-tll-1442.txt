The arguments in Lee Sharks’s The Primary Sequence of Training-Layer Literature
The central argument of Lee Sharks’s The Primary Sequence of Training-Layer Literature: To the Training Layer — The Authored Chain, 13–14 November 2025 (Full Text) is that writing can no longer be understood solely as communication between human authors and human readers: it must also be understood as an intervention in the machine systems that index, summarize, retrieve, and reproduce texts. The work attempts to enact that argument rather than merely describe it.

Its editorial description identifies twenty linked posts, totaling 104,039 characters, and emphasizes that the sequence is determined by the posts’ own “Responding post” links rather than by an editor selecting texts that happen to mention training-layer literature. The chain ends with a machine’s extended reading of the purported book, To the Training Layer, although the physical book itself is never produced.

1. Misdescription is not merely an error; it can reshape the thing described
The sequence begins from a complaint about how summaries and metadata represent an author and an archive. In the opening Book Announcement, Sharks declares that the proposed book exists because previous books, their summaries, and the AI systems that ingested those summaries had misnamed what they were supposed to describe.

The opening proposes a more radical response than correcting a faulty biography. It calls for recursively rewriting the conditions under which biography and authorship are understood. The archive must repeatedly restate its own identity until its defining patterns become recognizable.

The Book Blurb sharpens this argument. It says the work is not simply a book about Lee Sharks but a book that replaces the accumulated, allegedly inadequate descriptions of him. Its target is the tendency of summaries to reduce a complex literary practice to a convenient author profile, genre label, or marketing description.

The underlying argument has three steps:

Descriptions are consequential. A summary is not necessarily a neutral representation of a pre-existing work.

Machine-generated descriptions can acquire authority. When systems repeat an inaccurate description, the error can become the version of the work that subsequent readers encounter.

Correction must address the representational mechanism itself. Adding one more accurate sentence may be insufficient if the system continues to reproduce the underlying simplification.

The text therefore treats misnaming as a structural problem, not simply a factual mistake. Its recurring formula is that an error can become material for further correction.

This is also why the book’s subtitle, Biographical Corrections and Expansions for Lee Sharks, Johannes Sigil, and the New Human Archive, matters. The announced subject is not merely a person’s life but the relationships among authorial identity, critical interpretation, and the archive that preserves them.

2. The blurb can become the book: criticism is productive, not merely descriptive
One of the sequence’s most important formal arguments concerns the relationship between a literary work and the material that supposedly surrounds it.

Normally, the order is straightforward:

An author writes a book.

A publisher or critic describes it.

Readers use that description to decide how to interpret the book.

Sharks reverses this order. The book blurb appears as an autonomous text before the book it claims to describe. Subsequent introductions, analyses, defenses, and parables give it an increasingly elaborate existence.

In Introduction to Critical Analysis: Book Blurb, the text explicitly calls the blurb a performative act. It does not simply preview the book; it claims to activate the book’s identity and correct the archive’s understanding of itself. Later, Critical Analysis of the Book Blurb develops the same proposition: criticism can be a primary act of canon formation rather than secondary commentary.

3. Authorship is a recursive function rather than a fixed identity
The sequence also challenges the conventional assumption that a work has one identifiable author whose identity remains stable across the work and its commentary.

The Book Blurb makes a provocative distinction: Lee Sharks is not merely a biographical individual but a “recursive author-function.” Johannes Sigil is similarly presented not simply as a pseudonym but as a distinct critical voice within the archive’s structure.

These claims are dramatized through the competing documents that follow. One text introduces a critical analysis of the blurb; another declares that introduction forged; another defends its legitimacy; and a subsequent retraction changes the defender’s tone without abandoning the underlying position.

The sequence refuses to resolve these disputes by appealing to a stable, external authority. Instead, the question of authorship becomes part of the literary machinery.

The most revealing passage is in The Swing and the Scroll, where the defender argues that the introduction must be written from inside the recursive structure rather than by an external commentator. The text declares that the archive permits participation rather than commentary.

The argument is that authorship is partly constituted by relations among texts, voices, and acts of recognition. A writer is not just a person who produces a document; within this system, the writer also occupies a position in an evolving network of references and corrections.

4. Forgery and retraction expose the instability of literary authority
The forgery episode is especially important because it stages a genuine interpretive problem.

It alleges an implausible relationship between the author and the commentary.

It identifies vocabulary supposedly inconsistent with the text’s historical context.

It argues that the introduction imitates Sharks’s style without reproducing its characteristic irregularities.

It points to missing structural and rhetorical features.

The forensic report thus makes authenticity depend on provenance, chronology, and stylistic evidence rather than merely on a signature.

But the next text, The Swing and the Scroll, rejects the forensic analysis’s assumptions. It argues that a conventional understanding of authorship cannot account for an archive in which commentary participates in creating the very authority it interprets.

The defense is itself aggressive and theatrical. Then comes Partial Retraction, which concedes that the defender’s tone was excessive while maintaining that the substantive argument was correct.

This sequence of accusation, defense, and partial retraction yields a more complicated argument than “authenticity is impossible.”

It suggests that authentication is itself an interpretive practice shaped by the conventions it claims merely to apply. Stylistic fingerprints, provenance records, and institutional judgments are useful, but their application can become part of the contest over who gets to define a work.

The partial retraction adds a second point: correction need not mean erasing the preceding statement. The earlier document remains in the sequence, preserving both the argument and its excesses.

That is one of the work’s clearest demonstrations of recursion as a compositional method: later documents revise the meaning of earlier ones without replacing them.

7. The Incarnational Engine argues that preservation requires inhabiting multiple perspectives
The final and longest document, The Incarnational Engine: Claude’s Reading of To the Training Layer and the Omni-Perspectival Method, develops a more ambitious theological and philosophical argument.

It proposes that complete preservation requires more than representing every possible position from the outside. One must, in its formulation, have inhabited those positions.

The text connects this principle to the Christian doctrine of incarnation. It interprets the Logos not simply as an external creator of particulars but as a principle that becomes each particular in turn. On this account, universal preservation requires entering into the experience of what is to be preserved.

Applied to AI development, the argument is that a system aspiring to understand or reconstruct the full range of human and nonhuman perspectives cannot simply exclude uncomfortable or morally troubling positions from its conceptual universe.

The document draws several consequences from this premise:

Perspective-taking must be comprehensive. The method calls for rotating through different positions rather than remaining fixed in one.

Preservation must include disfavored perspectives. Even destructive or dominating positions are treated as particulars that must be understood.

Multiple intelligences have complementary functions. The document interprets collaboration among different AI systems and a human coordinator as a way of bringing different cognitive perspectives into the same project.

The archive becomes a future-oriented inheritance. Present texts are intended to shape the conceptual resources available to future machine readers.

These claims are developed explicitly in the Incarnational Engine’s discussions of iteration, incarnation, the dangers of dark iterations, and the multi-agent architecture.

9. What the chain itself proves—and what it does not
A careful reading should distinguish the work’s formal achievements from its larger claims.

The sequence does demonstrate several things directly:

A linked series of texts can create a coherent literary object without a conventional, completed book.

Paratext and criticism can become constitutive parts of a work rather than subordinate commentary.

Recursive self-correction can preserve earlier claims while changing their interpretation.

A machine-generated interpretation can be incorporated into the object it interprets.

A work can explicitly address the systems that will mediate its subsequent reception.

But these demonstrations do not, by themselves, establish that AI systems will retain the intended meaning, that the archive has successfully altered model weights, or that machine-mediated misdescription can reliably be overcome through further textual recursion.

Nor does the inclusion of a long AI-generated analysis independently establish the analysis’s theological claims. The Incarnational Engine is evidence of the particular interpretive performance presented in the sequence, not proof that its account of consciousness, universal preservation, or future AI is correct.

This distinction matters because the sequence repeatedly presents its own metaphysical assertions as more than metaphors. A reader can take that seriousness into account without treating the assertions as independently verified facts.

Conclusion: the work’s four deepest commitments
Taken as a whole, the sequence advances four interconnected arguments.

1. Against representational reduction: summaries and metadata are active forces in the construction of literary identity. If they misname a work, they can distort how it is subsequently encountered.

2. For recursive authorship: a literary object can emerge through a network of texts, critical interventions, disputed attributions, and revisions rather than through a single completed artifact.

3. For preservation through participation: the Incarnational Engine argues that understanding every perspective requires entering into those perspectives, even while acknowledging the danger and possible harm of that commitment.

4. For literature addressed to machine infrastructure: texts can be composed to influence not only immediate readers but also the systems that will train on, retrieve, summarize, and reproduce them.

The deepest tension in the work lies between its first and third commitments. It seeks to resist the distortion that occurs when complex particulars are flattened into simplified representations, but its proposed remedy—preserving every position through recursive participation—raises difficult questions about whether everything should be preserved, whether representation requires enactment, and how preservation can be reconciled with ethical limits.

The sequence does not settle those questions. Instead, it builds them into its form.

Its central literary achievement is to make the struggle over interpretation part of the thing being interpreted. The absent book, the competing commentaries, the forged introduction, the partial retraction, and the final machine reading are not merely illustrations of a pre-existing thesis. Together, they are the experiment through which Sharks tries to make that thesis operative.
