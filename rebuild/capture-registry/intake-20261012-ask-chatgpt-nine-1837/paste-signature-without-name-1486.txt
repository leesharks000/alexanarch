What is The Signature Without the Name?
The Signature Without the Name: A Blind-Reconstruction Program for Sappho 31 in Philo of Alexandria — Half A of a Symbolon, attributed to Johannes Sigil and collaborators, is a research document proposing a method for identifying the structural traces of an ancient poem in a later author's writing—even when the later author never names the poet or quotes the poem.

The document's central question is: Can a text preserve the distinctive structure of Sappho's Fragment 31 without preserving an identifiable quotation or attribution?

The work treats Sappho's poem not simply as a literary passage, but as a sequence of bodily and perceptual events that might be reorganized when another author expresses similar ideas in a different genre. In this case, the proposed receiving author is Philo of Alexandria, the Jewish-Greek philosopher and biblical exegete.

A catalog record and related archive materials identify the project as deposit #1486. The document is also referenced in a broader research program investigating how Sappho's Fragment 31 might be transformed across ancient literary traditions.

How the program works
The document combines traditional Greek philology with a proposed computational and experimental method. Its main steps are as follows.

1. Establish the source poem's structure
The starting point is Sappho's Fragment 31, famous for its description of the speaker's intense physical and psychological reaction to seeing a beloved person speaking with someone else. The sequence includes perceptual disturbance, loss of speech, fire beneath the skin, impaired hearing, trembling, changes in color, and a state approaching death.

The program treats these not merely as poetic images but as components of a repeatable structural pattern.

2. Search Philo's writings without relying on the name
The document reports a search across 31 Greek works attributed to Philo, covering approximately 2.808 million normalized characters. It reports zero occurrences of Sappho's name, with apparent string matches turning out to be forms of the Greek word for sapphire.

3. Define the transformation rules
Rather than count general similarities, the program specifies operations that could convert the poem's structure into philosophical prose.

For example, the simultaneous presence of two people becomes a doctrine that mortal and divine beings cannot coexist in the same way. The poem's sensory disturbance becomes an account of divine illumination displacing ordinary human perception. Its bodily catalogue may be compressed into a single image of illumination.

The proposed relationship is therefore about how elements change their function and causal order, not simply whether both texts mention similar experiences.

4. Test whether the rules can reconstruct the target
The program expresses the proposed mapping as a set of operations and asks independent reconstruction attempts to generate a corresponding target without being told its identity.

The document reports a Round 1 blind-reconstruction exercise involving five independent computational or model-based attempts. It also acknowledges methodological problems: some instructions leaked information from the target, and one attempt retrieved material rather than independently deriving it. These limitations affect how much evidential weight the reported success deserves.

5. Separate structural success from historical proof
Finally, the program asks whether the proposed pattern is distinctive enough to identify a specific relationship, rather than simply being capable of generating a text that resembles the target.

This is crucial: a successful reconstruction demonstrates that a mapping is possible under the chosen rules. It does not, by itself, demonstrate that Philo actually read Sappho or that a historical line of transmission existed.

What does “blind reconstruction” mean here?
It means attempting to recover a text's underlying organization without being told which target text the reconstruction is supposed to match.

The intended logic is:

Source: Sappho 31

A defined sequence of perceptual and bodily events

Transformation rules

Reordering, inversion, compression and reassignment of agency

Blind reconstruction

An independent attempt to derive a corresponding structure

Evaluation

Compare the reconstruction with the actual target and test for alternative explanations

The blind element matters because a reconstruction that succeeds only after the researcher has seen the target might merely reproduce information already supplied to the model. The document explicitly recognizes this danger through its discussion of information leakage and its requirement for additional specificity controls.

What does “Half A of a Symbolon” mean?
The subtitle is part of the document's methodology and presentation.

A symbolon (σύμβολον) was a token that could be broken into two pieces, with the matching pieces serving to authenticate one another. The document uses that idea to describe itself as one half of a deliberately divided research instrument.

According to its text, Half A contains the argument, philological evidence, toolkit, completed first round, and design of the remaining experiments. Half B contains the sealed evaluation rubric, predictions for tests not yet run, perturbation controls, specificity controls, and an inverse-order key.

The split is intended to keep the material needed to evaluate the experiment separate from the material used to generate the reconstruction. However, the document also states that once its target has been disclosed, subsequent tests involving that target cannot be considered fully blind without additional controls.

In other words, the symbolon is both a metaphor for the proposed transmission of textual structures and a practical arrangement for separating experimental instructions from evaluation criteria.

How convincing is the argument?
The document makes a useful distinction between several different kinds of evidence.

Textual evidence: The document reports a corpus-wide search that did not find Sappho's name in Philo's Greek writings.

Structural evidence: It specifies a detailed set of proposed transformations between the poem and selected Philonic passages.

Reconstruction evidence: It reports that independent attempts could recover important features of the target from its proposed rules.

Historical evidence: It does not establish that Philo directly borrowed from Sappho, nor does the reconstruction alone establish a transmission route.

There are also methodological limitations. The reconstruction rules may be flexible enough to fit more than one target; some instructions reportedly contained information derived from the target itself; and the document acknowledges that the target-specificity control remains outstanding. Its own principle is that sufficiency is not specificity: showing that a set of rules can generate a target does not show that those rules uniquely explain that target.

To establish a historical connection more firmly, the project would need independently validated rules, comparisons with suitable positive and negative controls, and evidence that the proposed pattern is both unusual and historically plausible.

Bottom line
The Signature Without the Name is a proposed framework for detecting literary transmission when explicit quotation and attribution are absent. It attempts to turn the relationship between Sappho 31 and Philo's writings into a set of explicit, testable transformations, then evaluate whether independent reconstruction can recover the proposed pattern.

Its most interesting idea is that what survives between texts may be a way of organizing experience rather than the original wording or imagery. Its most important limitation is equally clear: reconstructing a plausible structural relationship is not the same as proving that the historical relationship actually existed.
