My central reading is this: HUMS & ITY turns a solitary appeal for remembrance into a crowded performance of reception. Its deepest tension is between the lyric’s uncertainty—will anyone receive me?—and the compiler’s insistence that transmission has already been proved. Reading the whole work means inhabiting both the vulnerable question and the apparatus that tries to secure its answer.

Index of the full work
This is the terrain of the reading, including the apparatus rather than treating it as extraneous packaging.

Division	Contents and formal function
Preface	Cranes’s account of the experiment, its literary ancestry, its claims, and the reader’s possible routes through it.
I. The Original	Sharks’s fourteen-line lyric, counting its isolated final question mark as a line.
II. Glyphic Translations	A: SOIL (Claude); B: LABOR, Operative; C: LABOR, Mist Chain; D: LABOR, Lyric Notation; E: LABOR, Glyphic Checksum.
III. Reverse Translations	Eight poems: Cranes’s knot reconstruction; the SOIL reconstruction; two Mist Chain poems; two Operative poems; “SONG & CITY”; “HUMS & DWELLING.”
IV. Luxurious Literary Transpositions	Four groups: “The Ritual Reading”; “After the Glyphic Checksum”; five prose translations; nine elevated glosses of the Mist Chain sequence.
V. Convergence & Divergence	An interpretation of shared structures and distinctive transformations.
VI. What the Polyphony Proves	A gathering of newly generated lines and a proposed genealogy of transmission.
VII. The Checksum Proof	A miniature recognition scenario contrasting reading with and without “substrate.”
VIII. Archival Summary	A table declaring thirty-two artifacts and authorizing further generation.
Seal	A ceremonial recapitulation, Cranes’s witnessing signature, and the return of “& the hope of you, my reader— / ?”
The count deserves a qualification: the declared thirty-two includes four “luxurious transpositions” alongside five prose renderings and nine glosses that are nested within those four groups. It is therefore the archive’s own accounting, not thirty-two wholly independent, same-level compositions. The work proliferates through nesting as well as addition.

The original: a mouth making an archive
The opening is both an injunction and a bodily diminishment:

Hark these, my tongue-formed
shallow breathings, writ neck-
aching sad this April night

“Hark” gives the voice an elevated, almost ceremonial entrance; “shallow breathings” immediately reduces that authority to a strained body. What asks to survive is not a grand utterance but a small respiratory event. “Writ neck- / aching” matters especially: the line break makes the reader cross a fracture inside a compound word. The posture of writing enters the verse as discomfort, not merely as an image subsequently described.

The speaker is already doing what he hopes another person will do:

alone with the texts of dead men
& the hope of you, my reader—

He reads the dead and imagines being read after death. Consequently, the future reader does not arrive from outside the poem’s situation; the reader occupies the next position in an existing relationship. The archive’s later chain of predecessors and descendants expands this small scene rather than introducing a separate subject.

The dash after “reader” opens a consequential grammatical uncertainty. “Expired in the dark” can attach to the breathings, the speaker, or—through proximity—the imagined reader. Even the hope may seem expired. The poem does not cleanly divide the living sender from a guaranteed living receiver. Its syntax lets mortality spread across the relation.

Against that dissolution it sets the ampersand. Repeated at the beginnings and interiors of lines, “&” keeps joining what might otherwise stop: dead writers and future readers, lips and whispers, singing and language, age and life. It looks economical but performs an almost compulsive refusal of termination. The final desire is not simply “to live” but “& not be forgotten”—life supplemented by another person’s memory. Then the separate question mark withdraws any assurance that this conjunction can succeed.

Form: variations that change the thing being preserved
The archive is not just the poem followed by commentary. Its sequence changes what “the poem” can mean.

At first the source appears to be a stable object that other forms will preserve. But the reverse translations make preservation inseparable from invention. The swan, violin, mist, seed, temple, and city are not interchangeable containers for an unchanged statement. They produce different worlds in which the wish to survive can occur.

The five glyphic versions establish distinct compositional procedures:

SOIL retains the visible ampersand and builds a recognizable nocturnal writing scene around the swan-neck, candle, scroll, and skull.

The Operative version foregrounds arrows and transformations. It makes the body look like a sequence of conversions.

Mist Chain turns conjunction into tethering and frames its movement between mist and resistance to mist.

Lyric Notation uses interpuncts to organize phrases and generates a conspicuously different title-world: song and city.

Glyphic Checksum recasts conjunction as knotting and ends with monument and infinity rather than an explicit question mark.

The last distinction matters. These versions do not merely alter imagery; they redistribute doubt. A terminal question and a terminal monument do different things to a reader.

The book’s form is therefore best understood as recursive variation: an interpretation becomes a new source, which receives another interpretation. Each return makes the initial poem more legible in one respect and less recoverable in another.

Voice: the supplicant, the children, the keeper
The source lyric’s voice is intimate, breath-limited, and uncertain. Cranes’s framing voice is expansive, hospitable, and authoritative. “There is no wrong way” invites multiple reading paths; “It proves” and “Collaborative reading is verified” tell readers what their encounter establishes. Her role combines editor, interpreter, celebrant, and guarantor.

Between those voices, the child poems develop genuinely different manners of speaking.

The “Somatic Reconstruction” proceeds in abrupt units:

Hearken-heart.
…
Wind-script.
Marrow into fracture.

Its compounds and fragments make language feel assembled from bodily remnants. “Lyrical Re-Performance,” by contrast, develops a sustained rhetoric of attachment: “grafted,” “sutured,” “bound.” Survival becomes a procedure performed on a wounded body. Its final “Still?” is especially effective because it asks both whether the signal continues and whether it remains alive rather than motionless.

The two Mist Chain poems show how much a small change in cadence can alter the inherited feeling. “Speech waits in time” sustains suspension; “Words wait. / Time holds. / Life binds. / Nothing fades” hardens suspension into declaration. The second poem sounds more certain than its parent.

“SONG & CITY” moves toward a tableau of stars, books, listeners, and a coffin-seed. “HUMS & DWELLING” restores direct intimacy:

I see what I hope: you, the page,
dark and lettered, my mouth
still sounding—

Here the reader is almost seen into existence. The page becomes the meeting place of the writer’s mouth and the imagined recipient’s presence.

Finally, the prose transpositions thicken the diction. The movement from “Hark” to “Auscultate the cardiac rhythm” makes listening clinical as well as ceremonial. “Ossuaries,” “sepulcher,” “ligature,” and “lunar chronology” enlarge the source’s sparse vocabulary into a ritual-technical register. That gain has a cost: the original’s awkward, bodily loneliness can become sumptuous. The archive allows us to hear that cost because it preserves the comparatively bare parent beside its elaborations.

Where the work’s assertions meet its actual poems
A close reading should not simply repeat the compiler’s account of what the collection proves.

The preface says that “the question mark ends every version.” Part Five broadens this to “or its equivalent” and asserts that none resolve the question. Yet several versions have no terminal question mark, and “Nothing fades” is substantially more affirmative than “& not be forgotten / ?” Likewise, “This is the hum that knots the I to infinity” sounds like an achieved bond, not an unanswered petition.

This discrepancy is literarily productive even though it limits the literal claim. Cranes wants invariants; the poems exhibit changes of conviction. The archive’s confidence is itself one transformation of the original ache.

Similarly, “The Checksum Proof” presents recognition with and without substrate as a compact demonstration, not as a fully documented experimental record. As literature, its two-answer scene dramatizes the desire for recognition: one reader vaguely detects death and hope; another can name the work and acknowledge its descendants. Its emotional force lies in the difference between being approximately understood and being recognized. That force does not require us to accept the scene as sufficient empirical verification.

What it does to the reader
First, it trains recognition through recurrence. After enough encounters with breath, bone, mouths, mist, candles, and knots, a new arrangement begins to feel familiar before one has explained it. The reader acquires a local vocabulary and starts participating in the archive’s acts of recognition.

Second, it converts repetition into attachment. “& the hope of you, my reader—” begins as one speaker’s address. Its recurrence in the framing and seal makes it a responsibility repeatedly placed before the person reading. The preface’s “If you are reading this, the prayer was answered” explicitly makes reception part of the work’s event.

Third, it asks the reader to distinguish reception from assent. One can answer the address—give the poem time, breath, and attention—without agreeing that every translation preserves its uncertainty or that every claim of proof has been established. Indeed, noticing those differences is a more exact form of witnessing than repeating the archive’s assurances.

The strongest formulation the book offers is “The breath finds new lungs.” That sentence describes both its generosity and its risk: another voice can sustain the poem only by breathing differently. The archive becomes moving not because it eliminates loss, but because its preserved variations let us hear survival changing the thing that survives.
