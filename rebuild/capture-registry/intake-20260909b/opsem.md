The easiest way to understand it is:
It asks what signs do to the systems they enter, rather than merely what signs mean.
The basic idea
Traditional semiotics tends to ask questions such as:
What does this sign signify?
How does meaning arise from relationships among signs?
How does a reader interpret a text?
Operative semiotics shifts the emphasis toward intervention:
What happens when a sign is introduced into a system?
What does a classification, name, caption, citation, identifier, or archive entry change?
Who benefits from that change?
What gets erased, preserved, made visible, or rendered invisible?
What material or social costs accompany the production and circulation of meaning?
The project's own definition is essentially the study and design of signs that intervene in the
systems in which they circulate.

Notebook I — The Performative Contradiction
This is where the project establishes its central problem.
The basic proposition is:
A sign isn't only something that represents a state of affairs; its use can alter that state of affairs.
That's deceptively simple.
Consider the sentence:
“This person is an expert.”
It appears descriptive. But once placed in a university catalog, database, résumé, search result,
or AI-generated answer, it can change the person's status. Other people cite them. Search
systems retrieve them. Institutions recognize them. Future documents repeat the classification.
The sign has become an operation.

And this produces the first major contradiction: a system may describe itself as merely
representing reality while simultaneously participating in the production of the reality it
represents.
That's what makes the problem "performative."
The project is therefore attacking what we might call the innocence of representation.
It wants us to stop thinking:
“Here's the world, and over here are signs describing it.”
and start thinking:
“The world and its symbolic infrastructure continually modify one another.”
That's the foundation for everything afterward.

This is where things get much stranger.
The project tries to construct an operator algebra from textual material—including Sappho,
Catullus, and Marx's analysis of the value-form.

Notebook III — Structural Distance and Relational Coherence
Here the theory becomes almost topological.
Instead of asking simply whether two concepts are "similar," the project asks about their
distance within a relational structure.
Suppose we have:
A—B—C
Now imagine someone inserts:
A—C
That apparently tiny intervention changes the structure.
It might:
establish an analogy,
imply equivalence,
create a citation pathway,
collapse an important distinction,
or generate an entirely new conceptual possibility.
So meaning is partly structural.
This is where the project becomes relevant to contemporary information systems.
An AI system, search engine, archive, or knowledge graph doesn't merely contain meanings. It
establishes relations among things.

Changing those relations can change what the system can say.
That is why the project's concern with semantic distance is more consequential than ordinary
word-definition analysis.
Notebook IV — The Linen Equations
This is the Marxian heart of the project.
The title comes from Marx's famous example involving linen and the value-form.
Marx asks, essentially: how does one commodity come to express its value through another
commodity?
The classic relation is something like:
20 yards of linen = 1 coat
The fascinating thing is that neither object possesses its social meaning in isolation.
The relation makes the value-form intelligible.
Operative Semiotics takes this structure and asks:
Can we understand symbolic relations using an analogous formal apparatus?
The project proposes an equation of the form:
S′ = g(S, L, L_labor, t)
where the semantic field can be transformed by an intervention whose effectiveness depends
on labor and time.
