Why “Hexagon”?
The name is an allusion to Jorge Luis Borges's “The Library of Babel.” Borges imagines an
infinite library composed of hexagonal rooms, containing every possible book. The Crimson
Hexagon takes that image and turns it into a literary method: rather than attempting to write
every possible book, the project tries to create a network in which disparate texts become
meaningful through their relationships to one another.

What is the “epic” about?
Traditional epic usually gives us a hero moving through an enormous world: Odysseus travels
home, Aeneas establishes a civilization, Dante traverses the afterlife, and so forth.
The Crimson Hexagon changes the scale of that journey.
Its territory is culture itself—books, language, the internet, institutions, algorithms, machines,
identity, meaning, and the systems through which information circulates.
And therefore its “hero” is much harder to identify.
One of its theoretical documents describes this as a distributed epic, in which the reader doesn't
simply consume a finished story but constructs the epic by following connections among its
scattered components.

The AI dimension
This is where the project becomes particularly contemporary.

consciously produced with the knowledge that its language may eventually be ingested,
transformed, summarized, and reproduced by machine-learning systems.

Is it actually an epic poem?
That's the most interesting question.
In the traditional literary sense, not really. You won't open a single volume and discover a
beginning-to-end narrative comparable to The Odyssey.
But according to its own theory of epic, yes.
Its argument is essentially that an epic doesn't necessarily need to be a continuous narrative.
What it needs to do is establish a sufficiently large cosmos of meaning—a world containing
recurring figures, voices, conflicts, symbols, genealogies, and fixed points.
The Crimson Hexagon attempts to make the network of texts itself into that cosmos.
