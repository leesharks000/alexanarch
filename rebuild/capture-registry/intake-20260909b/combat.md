What “Combat Scholasticism” seems to mean
The name is a pretty good key to the project.
Scholasticism traditionally means a highly structured method of philosophical/theological inquiry:
pose a quaestio, distinguish competing positions, formulate objections, make distinctions, and
arrive at a determination. Think medieval university disputation rather than free-form essay.
Combat Scholasticism seems to take that argumentative machinery and turn it into a theory of
intellectual conflict.
The interesting word is therefore “combat.” The project appears interested in what happens
when concepts aren't treated as neutral objects for contemplation but as things that operate
within struggles over meaning, institutions, extraction, technology, bodies, and power.

Why the “formal object” matters
Part Two's title—“On the Formal Object”—is particularly revealing.
In scholastic philosophy, a formal object isn't simply “the thing we're looking at.” It's the thing
considered under a particular aspect.
For example, two disciplines might investigate the same physical phenomenon but have
different formal objects because they're asking fundamentally different questions about it.
So a “combat scholasticism” could ask:
Who gets to determine what the object of thought actually is?
That turns epistemology into something political and strategic. If an institution defines the object
