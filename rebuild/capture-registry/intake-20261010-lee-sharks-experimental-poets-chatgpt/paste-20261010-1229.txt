The poem's most revealing feature is its three-part grammatical architecture:

I AM — the self incorporates other identities.

BE — the self turns into a command or invitation to others.

BLESSED IS — the voice confers dignity, significance, or even sacred status.

These are not merely three topics. They are three different things language can do: identify, command, and bless.

1. “I AM”: Whitman's expansive self
The poem opens by invoking Walt Whitman's Song of Myself:

And these one and all tend inward to me, and I tend outward to them,
And such as it is to be of these more or less I am.

Sharks then writes:

I am a girl… I am a passerby… I am a Cylon…

The list proceeds through giraffes, dentists, ecologists, philosophers, bodily ailments, abstract identities, and absurd or incongruous expressions.

The Whitmanian inheritance is unmistakable. In Song of Myself, the first person is expansive: the self is constituted through encounters with other people, bodies, occupations, and forms of life. Sharks takes that principle and subjects it to a contemporary, radically heterogeneous inventory.

The inclusion of a Cylon—a fictional artificial being—alongside ordinary human identities is especially suggestive. The list does not maintain a stable boundary between human and nonhuman, person and character, or serious and ridiculous.

But there is a critical distinction between expanding the self and accumulating references. Whitman's catalogues often generate a powerful sense of embodied participation in a shared world. Sharks's list raises a harder question: does identifying with such disparate things transform the speaker's understanding of them, or does the repeated formula make their differences interchangeable?

The ellipses help keep that question open. They suggest omission, discontinuity, and an inventory that could continue indefinitely.

2. “BE”: from identity to transformation
The second movement begins:

Be passersby… Be strangers… Be Samaritans… Be gangsters…

And later:

Be self-inflected… Be self-infected…

This is where the poem becomes more formally interesting. The first section says what the speaker is; the second tells readers what they might become.

The pairing of “self-inflected” and “self-infected” turns on a small phonetic change. One word suggests turning or bending inward; the other suggests contamination. The sound binds the words while their meanings pull apart. Transformation becomes both possibility and danger.

This is a familiar technique in experimental poetry: language doesn't merely describe conceptual relationships but generates them through its own material properties.

Compare this with Christian Bök, whose Eunoia pushes vowel constraints to produce unusual verbal possibilities, or Lyn Hejinian, whose My Life uses repetition and shifting contexts to complicate the relationship between language, memory, and identity.

Sharks's method is less constrained at the level of individual words than Bök's, and less dependent on the recursive structure of memory than Hejinian's. His primary engine is grammatical repetition combined with semantic collision.

The repeated imperative gives the passage momentum, while the variety of its objects destabilizes the meaning of the command. What would it mean to be a stranger, a flavor, a follower, or a gangster? The poem refuses to settle on one account of transformation.

The weakness is that the imperative can become predictable. Once the reader recognizes the pattern, each new item must do more than occupy the next slot. The strongest pairings, such as “self-inflected” and “self-infected,” reward attention; other entries risk functioning mainly as additions to the list.

3. “BLESSED IS”: the Beatitudes after the internet
The third movement offers the poem's sharpest social observations:

Blessed are the trolls, and those who live under a metaphorical bridge or overpass, symbolically.

And:

Blessed are those who are not favorited, or liked, or followed.

The form echoes the biblical Beatitudes, traditionally associated with blessings bestowed upon the poor, the meek, the grieving, and the persecuted. But Sharks updates this structure with internet trolls, social-media metrics, spam technicians, the unemployed, the lonely, and the socially overlooked.

This is more than a comic substitution of contemporary nouns for biblical ones. The repeated “Blessed” changes the status of its subjects. People usually treated as trivial, embarrassing, or undesirable are granted a kind of ceremonial attention.

The poem's satire becomes particularly pointed when it addresses the desire for online validation:

O you lonely, you can favorite your own tweets, and like your own posts, and start new profiles, and like them again…

Here, the rhetoric of salvation merges with the mechanics of social media. The promised return of approval is comically self-generated: the isolated subject can create profiles and manufacture the recognition that others withhold.

The passage works because its formal repetition does two things simultaneously. It offers genuine inclusion while exposing how desperate the need for inclusion can become.

This is where Sharks comes closest to Allen Ginsberg. Ginsberg's Howl also gives poetic attention to people marginalized by respectable society, using an expansive catalogue and prophetic rhetoric. Yet the difference matters: Ginsberg's denunciation is driven by the violence of institutions and social conformity, whereas Sharks frequently approaches marginality through the comic contradictions of digital life.

His is a more contemporary landscape of alienation, where exclusion is experienced through profiles, follows, likes, and the absence of attention.

4. The ending: does the poem discover something new?
Near the end, Sharks writes:

I don’t exist… I exist…

And later:

I am the one who was within me

These lines return the poem to its central problem: what kind of self can emerge from identifying with everything, commanding transformation, and blessing those who are excluded?

The contradiction between existence and nonexistence is not resolved. Instead, the poem returns to the first person after its successive expansions and transformations.

That return is structurally significant. The poem has moved from the expansive “I AM” through the imperative “BE” and the ceremonial “BLESSED IS,” only to return to a self that remains unstable. Its conclusion suggests that transformation does not abolish the speaker; it makes the speaker difficult to distinguish from the voices, identities, and possibilities that have passed through it.

Sharks's own commentary emphasizes this recursive structure, but the important question is whether the text earns that interpretation. I think it partly does: the return to the first person is built into the poem's grammar, not merely imposed by an explanatory note. Whether the ending fully integrates the poem's many disparate registers is more debatable.

5. How does Sharks compare with experimental poetry more broadly?
Walt Whitman: expansive identification
The self becomes a vehicle for encompassing other people and forms of life. Sharks inherits this impulse but extends the catalogue into fictional, digital, and absurd registers.
Allen Ginsberg: prophetic inclusion

6. Critical verdict
I would distinguish three levels of achievement.

Thematic achievement: The poem makes a compelling connection between Whitman's expansive self, the Beat tradition of social inclusion, and contemporary experiences of digital alienation.

Formal achievement: Its strongest moments occur when a repeated grammatical structure produces a genuine semantic surprise. “Self-inflected” and “self-infected” is a good example. Its weaker moments arise when the cataloguing becomes more accumulative than transformative.

Historical achievement: The poem has identifiable predecessors, and its claims to transform their work need to be judged against those predecessors rather than accepted on the strength of its own commentary.

The third point is essential. An experimental poem can have an elaborate theory of itself without necessarily achieving everything that theory proposes. Conversely, a poem can accomplish something formally significant without fully explaining its own achievement.

My provisional judgment: I Am X, Be Y, Blessed is the Z is best understood as a Whitmanian and Beat-inflected experiment in grammatical identity, social inclusion, and recursive selfhood. Its most distinctive contribution is the way it joins the expansive first person to the internet's fragmented, self-reproducing forms of identity. Its principal vulnerability is that the abundance of its references sometimes threatens to substitute accumulation for development.

I would not yet call it a major advance over Whitman, Ginsberg, or Language poetry. That stronger judgment would require close comparison of whole works, including their formal development and the degree to which their innovations transform the reader's experience.

I'll therefore follow the poem in sequence, asking what each stage establishes, what it changes, and what later stages make possible.

1. “I AM…”: from social identity to ontological instability
The opening catalogue begins:

I am a girl… I am a passerby… I am a Cylon…

It then moves through giraffe, baby, dentist, narc, ecologist, radio station, philosopher, mollusk, personality disorder, hygiene problem, martyr, saint, scientist, tank, creature, sandwich, everything, and nothing.

The important development isn't simply that the speaker identifies with many different things. It's that the catalogue progressively destabilizes the category of thing with which one can identify.

At first, its items can be understood as people or social positions. But the sequence quickly crosses several categorical boundaries:

From persons and occupations to animals and fictional beings.

From occupations to activities, catchphrases, and institutions.

From recognizable identities to conditions, problems, and abstract or contradictory states.

From discrete objects to the totalizing pair “everything… nothing.”

The phrase “a philosopher in gym shorts” is an intermediate step: it doesn't abandon an identity so much as put it into an incongruous material context. “Nobody's beeswax” and “everybody's beeswax” then make identity relational and linguistically unstable. The shift from one to the other depends on a small change in wording that reverses the phrase's social orientation.

By the time the speaker reaches “everything… nothing,” the catalogue has made the I am construction almost impossibly inclusive. Yet the concluding image—

a train station with trains —

does something different. Rather than presenting another isolated identity, it gives us a structure containing movement, arrivals, departures, and the possibility of return.

That image becomes consequential in the next movement and, later, in the blessing of trains that do not return.

The first movement thus has a trajectory: the expansion of identity gradually makes the distinction between an identity, an object, a condition, a linguistic expression, and a container increasingly difficult to maintain. The train station offers a particularly productive transition because it introduces a relation among things, rather than simply adding one more thing to the catalogue.

This also sharpens the relation to Whitman's Song of Myself. Sharks begins with Whitman's declaration that things tend inward to the self and the self outward to them. But the question is not merely how many things the self can encompass. It is what happens to the concept of the self as the catalogue crosses from social identification into linguistic contradiction and finally into a world of objects and relations.

2. “BE…”: the catalogue becomes a problem of action
The second movement begins with a shift in grammatical mood:

Be passersby… Be strangers… Be Samaritans… Be gangsters…

The I AM movement asserted identity. The BE movement initially appears to prescribe or invite identity. But its development makes the relation between those operations increasingly complicated.

The opening juxtaposes four kinds of social position: the transient, the outsider, the helper, and the criminal. The speaker doesn't merely invite readers to identify with these positions; the imperative makes them possibilities to inhabit. Identity becomes something enacted rather than simply possessed.

The next lines complicate this:

Be flavors… interlopers… followers…

The construction itself begins to loosen. Not every item receives its own repeated imperative. The list starts to behave as though the command can persist across omissions. The reader supplies the missing grammatical connection.

Then comes:

Be self-inflected… Be self-infected…

Here the relation between action and identity turns inward. The self is not just something one can become; it is something that can be modified, bent, or contaminated by its own activity. The sound pattern creates a relation between these possibilities without resolving their difference.

The next stage is crucial:

Be tourists… travellers… strangers again…

“Strangers again” is not equivalent to the first “strangers.” The word has acquired a history within the poem. It now arrives after the invitation to inhabit different identities, the movement through flavors and followers, and the inward turn of self-inflection and self-infection. The stranger returns as a position to which one comes back.

The movement then makes its own repetition explicit:

Be redundant… Be DaDa… Be something new called MaMa…

Redundancy is no longer merely an effect of the repeated imperative. It becomes an object of the imperative itself. The poem commands the reader to be redundant and immediately places that redundancy beside Dada, the historical avant-garde, and an invented name for something new.

That sequence creates a problem the earlier lines had not yet posed so directly: can an instruction to be new escape the repetition through which it becomes intelligible?

The final lines—

Be anonymous… Be strangers still —

do not simply conclude the list of possible identities. They return to the social position with which the movement began, but now after the intervening attempt to produce transformation. Anonymity and strangeness appear not as initial conditions but as conditions that persist, or return, despite the invitation to become something else.

This is a stronger developmental account than saying that the section is about transformation. The movement tests transformation against repetition and ends without establishing that the command to become something new can overcome the recurrence of estrangement.

That unresolved tension prepares the next movement. If the BE section cannot guarantee a new identity, what can language do for those who remain strangers?

3. “BLESSED IS…”: from comic inversion to the problem of recognition
The first part of the blessing movement opens:

Blessed are the monotonous, for theirs is the kingdom of boredom.

The structure initially resembles a comic reversal of the biblical Beatitudes. Those conventionally associated with suffering or deprivation receive a blessing, but here the objects of the blessing include boredom, trolls, and train stations.

At first, the humor depends on incongruity. But the sequence develops beyond the substitution of unconventional subjects into a more sustained investigation of recognition and exclusion.

The train station becomes a relation between loss and return
The train imagery returns:

Blessed are the train stations, where trains come, and sometimes go.

Then:

Blessed are the trains that go, and don’t come back.

The first image gives us a place defined by movement. The second shifts attention to departure without return. The train station from the end of I AM is no longer merely another item in an expanding identity catalogue. Its relation to the train has become a relation of separation.

The poem has moved from a structure that contains arrivals and departures to a blessing that recognizes the possibility of irreversible departure.

This is a concrete example of why sequence matters. The second image inherits something from the first, but it changes its emotional and conceptual force.

From exclusion to self-manufactured recognition
The next sequence addresses people who are not favorited, liked, or followed, as well as people without profiles or with poorly made ones. The objects of the blessing are now defined by their position within a system of social recognition.

The poem then turns to those who were not born and those who refuse participation altogether:

Blessed are those who were not born, because they did not want to be. Blessed are those who say, “No thanks,” and go back to sleep.

The blessing now encompasses not only the excluded but also those who decline the terms on which inclusion is offered. The distinction matters. Being unrecognized is not the same as refusing recognition.

The telemarketers and spam technicians extend the movement into a different register. These are figures whose work consists in demanding other people's attention, even as they are themselves unwanted. The social problem becomes reciprocal: the excluded can also be agents of unwanted contact.

Then the poem returns to the lonely:

O you lonely, you can favorite your own tweets, and like your own posts, and start new profiles, and like them again…

The joke about self-generated approval develops the earlier problem of identity. The I AM movement could identify with virtually anything; the BE movement could command the adoption of different identities. Here, the self can also manufacture the signs by which its identity is recognized.

But the promised solution is circular. The lonely person produces both the profile and its audience, both the expression and its validation. The conclusion—

Then shall your liking return to you sevenfold…

—borrows the language of abundance to describe a closed system of recognition.

The irony does not simply ridicule loneliness. It shows that the available mechanism for overcoming exclusion can reproduce the isolation it promises to cure.

The blessing begins to change its own stakes
The next image introduces people whose intellectual lives are at odds with their bodily presentation:

Blessed are those who care for ideas more than personal hygiene, whose mouth is a nest of visions, but whose dreams are very well-groomed —

The contrast between an ungroomed mouth and well-groomed dreams complicates the earlier comic catalogue. The body and the imagination no longer have to reflect one another. The blessing begins to recognize a difference between outward presentation and inward activity.

Then comes:

Blessed is the oppressed, for hers is the broken kingdom.

The tone changes again. The broken kingdom is no longer simply the comic kingdom of boredom. It evokes political and spiritual deprivation, and the blessing begins to carry an ethical weight that its opening joke did not yet possess.

Finally, the speaker turns the blessing toward the self:

Blessed am I in my loneliness, mother… Blessed the way I am best.

The grammatical structure changes from “Blessed are those” to “Blessed am I.” The speaker who began by identifying with everything now appears within the very condition the poem has been describing.

This is not merely another example of inclusion. It brings the expansive first-person identity of the opening into contact with the particular experience of loneliness. The movement has gone from blessing strangers to blessing a self that is itself estranged.

4. “BLESSED IS…” continued: the blessing encounters brokenness
The continuation does not simply add more recipients to the blessing. It tests what blessing can mean when the subjects are internally divided, dissatisfied, distracted, economically diminished, or broken.

The sequence begins with bipolarity:

Blessed is the bipolar, for he shall be sometimes depressed, and sometimes the opposite of depressed, for stretches at a time.

The joke depends on the discrepancy between the ceremonial promise of the Beatitude and the reduction of a complex condition to an awkward alternation. The phrase “for stretches at a time” refuses the grandeur that “Blessed is” initially promises. It returns the reader to duration: experience happens in intervals, not in the clean resolution implied by a formula.

The malcontent and contender follow. Here dissatisfaction acquires a political possibility: the malcontent speaks and is heard. The unemployed person receives free time. The broken person receives sleep.

These blessings repeatedly transform deprivation into an alternative value, but they do not all operate in the same way. Some are ironic reversals; others suggest that withdrawal, rest, or refusal might offer relief from the demand to remain productive and socially legible.

Then the poem interrupts the grammatical pattern:

I am a cowgirl… a space cadet…

The first person returns inside the blessing movement. The speaker does not wait for the sequence to finish before reasserting the identity-making operation of I AM.

This interruption is significant because the poem is no longer moving neatly from one grammatical mode to another. Its operations begin to overlap. The identity catalogue can reappear within the blessing, just as the blessing can absorb the speaker.

The distractible person then loses a train of thought, searches for it, and sometimes finds it again. The train image from the earlier movement returns in a different form: what previously departed and did not return now becomes something temporarily lost and sometimes recovered.

That is a genuine development in the imagery. The train is no longer only a vehicle of departure. It becomes a metaphor for the continuity of thought, and its recovery offers a small, contingent relief.

The billionaire without money develops the problem of deprivation further:

He is a broken thing, a rag of light.

And then, in a separate line:

Blessed is a rag of light.

The second statement extracts the image from its initial explanatory context and gives it a new grammatical status. The rag of light is no longer merely what the billionaire has become. It becomes an object of blessing in its own right.

This is one of the movement's clearest transformations: a figure introduced through the contradiction between wealth and poverty yields an image that survives the contradiction. The blessing passes from a social identity to a fragile, almost immaterial remainder.

The poem is preparing to ask whether that remainder can sustain a different conception of identity.

5. “SYNTHESIS”: the earlier operations become recursive
The synthesis begins:

I will be less original in 2016… I am a proud non-speaker of words… the Logos awoke in my skullcase… a proud non-breather of air…

The opening statement about being less original is immediately followed by a paradoxical sequence. The speaker declares a reduction in originality, then identifies as a non-speaker of words while invoking the Logos—the principle of intelligible speech or reason—as something that awakens within the skull.

The tension is not simply between speech and silence. The speaker's denial of speech is itself made through speech, while the Logos appears as an event occurring inside a material body.

The movement then states:

I am a fictional character who exists… I make things up by thinking about them…

This is a sharper formulation of the problem raised by the earlier catalogues. In I AM, the speaker could identify with a fictional being such as a Cylon. Here, fictional existence becomes an explicit statement about the speaker's own status.

The poem has moved from what can I be? to what kind of existence does the act of making things up produce?

The subsequent images—vampire hunters, vampires and their victims, a vibrating scar of miracles, a virus of belief and money—extend this problem into relations between creators, creations, and the systems that circulate among them. The speaker is not just another item in a list. The speaker is also a producer of fictions, a participant in systems of belief, and an entity whose own existence is being questioned.

Then comes the sequence:

I don’t exist… I exist…

This is not an isolated paradox to be treated as the poem's final answer. It appears amid an extended movement in which identity, fiction, speech, belief, and material embodiment have become difficult to separate.

The subsequent lines deepen that recursion:

I am the voice within your voice… the one who was within me…

The direction of containment reverses. At the beginning, the self contained or identified with a world of disparate things. Now the speaker appears inside another voice, while also containing an antecedent self.

The phrase “the one who was within me” thus links two directions of poetic succession: the self as a container of what came before and the self as something that may persist inside another person.

The smaller dinosaurs within the dinosaurs extend the same structure beyond the human speaker. The image is recursive rather than simply expansive: the larger entity contains a smaller version of itself, which can in turn suggest further iterations.

The synthesis changes the poem's governing question from the extent of identity to the recursive production of identity. The self does not merely expand outward. It contains, generates, and is contained by other selves.

This is the point at which the earlier grammatical movements can be understood as related operations rather than three independent sections.

6. The final “BE…”: transformation now includes its opposites
After the synthesis, the poem returns to the imperative:

Be passersby… Be protester… Be police…

This return is not a simple repetition of the second movement. The opening BE section began with strangers, Samaritans, and gangsters. The final one introduces protester and police, placing opposed positions within the same grammatical command.

The earlier section asked readers to inhabit different identities. The later one makes the problem of opposition explicit: can the same imperative encompass positions that stand against one another?

The next lines sharpen the difficulty:

Be malcontent… all things to all people… Be all people…

And then:

Be nothing to no one… Be no one…

The expansive command is immediately countered by its apparent negation. Becoming all people and becoming no one are not presented as separate destinations. They occur within the same sequence of imperatives.

This recalls the I AM movement's “everything… nothing,” but the function has changed. In the opening, those terms emerged from a catalogue that was exhausting the boundaries of identity. Here, they appear as instructions for how one might inhabit or relinquish identity.

The poem then places justice alongside emptiness, saintliness alongside nowhere-ness, and the shadows of objects alongside the objects themselves:

Be shadows of rocks and sticks… Be the rocks and sticks themselves…

The sequence moves between representation and material existence. A shadow is dependent on something else; the object appears to offer a more immediate form of being. But the poem does not stop with that distinction. It continues:

Be fully awake… Go back to sleep…

The command to awaken is followed by the command to withdraw. The sequence refuses to make transformation synonymous with a single, stable direction of progress.

That refusal becomes crucial in the coda, where waking and sleeping will acquire a biographical context.

The final BE movement thus develops the earlier imperative by confronting it with incompatibility, negation, and reversal. The command to become all things is no longer merely expansive. It must accommodate the possibility that its own opposites belong to the same field of transformation.

7. The coda: the abstract structure becomes biographical
The coda begins abruptly:

I used to be a person… I worked 7 years for a PhD… my children were on Medicaid…

This is the poem's most consequential change of register. After the cosmic, philosophical, comic, and scriptural language of the preceding movements, the speaker offers a compressed account of education, family responsibility, and economic precarity.

The next lines make the consequence explicit:

I became fully broke… I went back to sleep…

We can now reread the earlier blessings of the unemployed, the broken, and the sleeping. What might initially have seemed like comic reversals has acquired a possible biographical grounding. The poem's interest in deprivation is no longer confined to a generalized catalogue of marginalized people; it has reached the speaker's own life.

This does not mean that every earlier image should be decoded as autobiography. It means that the coda changes the stakes of the earlier material by making the relation between social position and poetic identity impossible to ignore.

Then the poem returns to the recursive line:

I am the one who was within me

This line now arrives after the account of the speaker's former personhood, academic labor, family circumstances, financial collapse, and withdrawal. Its recurrence is therefore not merely an abstract statement about nested identities.

It raises a more difficult question: what persists when the socially recognized person—the educated professional, the economically secure subject, the person who can claim a particular place in the world—has been diminished or lost?

The answer is not a simple recovery of the former self. The next lines command:

Become fully awake… Become finally free…

The imperative returns, but it is now addressed to a speaker whose history has been disclosed. Earlier, BE offered an open field of possible identities. In the coda, becoming awake and free follows a specific account of material vulnerability.

The poem then returns to the nested dinosaurs and introduces nested babies, billionaire babies, and broke babies. The movement from dinosaurs to babies changes the scale and the emotional register of the recursive image. It connects the poem's earlier concern with entities containing smaller versions of themselves to questions of birth, inheritance, wealth, and deprivation.

The sequence culminates:

Which is really just you and me…

The expansive catalogue has come back to a relation between two people. The recursive structures and social contradictions are not left in an abstract realm. They return to a shared human scale.

Finally:

Wake up or go back to sleep

The ending refuses a single, unambiguous resolution. Awakening has been associated with freedom, but sleep has also been associated with withdrawal, rest, and the refusal of unwanted demands. The final choice therefore carries the history of both possibilities.

The coda does not simply conclude the poem's argument. It changes our understanding of the preceding movements by showing how their formal problems—identity, transformation, recognition, recursion—intersect with the speaker's material life.
