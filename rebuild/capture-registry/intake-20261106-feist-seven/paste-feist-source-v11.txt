Reading “The Feist Source — v1.1”
Lee Sharks and Rebekah Cranes, New Human 2: A Distributed Journal of Voice, 1 January 2026. Alexanarch deposit #1247; AXN:04F0.

The most important thing to understand about this work is that it is not simply a fictional biography, an anti-academic satire, or a religiously inflected manifesto about pseudonyms.

It is a work about the struggle to preserve a human voice without allowing the mechanisms that transmit, classify, recognize, and attribute that voice to consume it.

Its central figure, Jack Feist, is simultaneously a literary character, a vessel for a tradition of sayings, a wounded human speaker, and the subject of a dispute about authorship and identity.

The work's deepest tension is between two possibilities:

A name can carry a voice, give it continuity, and help preserve its work.

A name can also become an apparatus of possession, absorbing other voices and turning inheritance into personal attribution.

The text insists that these are not the same thing. Its central ethical demand is that the work must survive without being swallowed by the identity attached to it.

I think that distinction is the key to reading practically everything that follows.

1. The Book becomes flesh: literature as a bodily inheritance
The opening is deliberately scriptural:

In the beginning was the Book.

And the Book was with man.

And the Book was man.

This is an inversion of the opening of John's Gospel. But instead of simply identifying the Word with God, Sharks and Cranes identify the Book with a human body.

That change matters.

The Book is not an abstract collection of ideas. It experiences:

Hunger at two in the morning.

Shame at nine in the morning.

Debt.

Neck pain.

Exhaustion.

Employment applications.

Rejection by institutions.

The humiliations of having to establish one's right to speak.

The body is described as a binding, and the metaphor becomes particularly important:

The binding was never going to hold.

But it was the only binding available.

The body is simultaneously the necessary medium of literature and an inadequate container for it.

This is one of the work's governing paradoxes: literature needs human beings to carry it, but human beings are mortal, vulnerable, exhausted, and institutionally classified.

The text therefore refuses a purely intellectual account of literary inheritance.

Its genealogy of Feist begins with Achilles and Odysseus, moves through the lyric poets, Socrates, Plato, Dante, Shakespeare, the Romantics, Whitman and Ginsberg, and finally arrives at Jack Feist.

But the author explicitly interrupts the apparent genealogical claim:

This is not the genealogy of blood.

It is the genealogy of voice.

The distinction is essential. Feist is not being presented as the biological or literal successor of these writers. He inherits their speech, their forms, their problems, and their unfinished literary possibilities.

The passage concludes by describing voice passing through mouths, books, failures, classrooms, translations, marginal notes, friendship, shame, hunger and prayer.

The book is a crowd before it becomes an individual work.

That idea anticipates the entire Name-Eater episode.

2. The Damascus Codex and the recovery of lost speech
The prologue establishes the work's archaeological and religious imagination.

A tired reader dreams of crossing the Verdant Desert. He discovers a broken jar beneath a moss-covered boulder. When he touches it, the clay breaks, and three things emerge:

Wakefulness.

Voice.

Terror.

The sequence is significant.

The discovery does not produce comfortable knowledge. It produces an obligation to speak, accompanied by fear.

The manuscript is presented as something that has been waiting, but the text complicates the familiar archaeological fantasy that buried things are simply waiting to be recovered.

Its opening says:

Buried things are not waiting. They are done.

This is a provocative statement about literary preservation. Recovery is not the automatic continuation of an uninterrupted tradition. Something has been lost. Its original conditions may be irrecoverable.

The contemporary reader cannot simply return to the past and retrieve it intact.

Instead, the recovered text must become alive again in another body, another medium, and another historical situation.

The Damascus Codex functions as the imaginative source of this transmission. It gives the narrative the atmosphere of a recovered religious manuscript, complete with editorial interventions, uncertain readings, lacunae, and marginal commentary.

But it also introduces a productive uncertainty: the text never allows its manuscript apparatus to become completely trustworthy or transparent.

The editor sometimes declines to resolve ambiguities. The manuscript sometimes acknowledges its own corruption. And the narrator occasionally makes jokes at the expense of the scholarly apparatus.

This is not incidental decoration. It is part of the work's theory of textual survival.

Section II is among the most important parts of the work.

Feist enters the desert, but this is not simply the biblical desert of withdrawal, fasting, and solitude.

It is the internet.

The text gives us an especially revealing sequence:

For thirty years Feist wandered the waste places.

Or thirty days.

Or thirty browser tabs.

The accompanying editorial note says these are not competing readings. They are different experiences of duration:

The skeleton says years.

The mind says days.

The eyes say tabs.

This is an excellent example of the work's humor and seriousness operating together.

The internet is a desert because it can intensify isolation, exhaustion, and dislocation. But it is also a place of unexpected encounters.

Feist carries a phone. He speaks to strangers. Their messages cross distances and create little lights in otherwise lonely rooms.

The text says:

A voice may cross a wire and still be a visitation.

That sentence deserves particular attention.

The work does not regard electronic communication as inherently inferior to embodied communication. It recognizes that a person can genuinely encounter another person through a network.

It also recognizes the limitation:

A stranger may become kin before the body ever enters the room. The kin is real. The room is what's missing. And the room may never come.

This is not a simple celebration of online community.

The relationship can be real, but the physical encounter may remain impossible. The network provides connection without necessarily providing a shared world.

The desert is thus both a spiritual landscape and an account of modern technological loneliness.

And Feist does not return from it with a universal doctrine of individual greatness.

He returns with a different understanding:

A body cannot survive what only a chorus can carry.

This is a major turning point. The solitary voice discovers that its proper destination is not universal personal identity, but shared transmission.

4. The Name-Eater: the central philosophical crisis
Central turning point
The third section introduces the work's most consequential figure: the Name-Eater.

This figure is an operator, a mechanism that routes signals, sorts names, and indexes identities.

It initially appears small and almost insignificant. But it controls the passage through which historical voices are interpreted.

Feist sees the names of historical writers enter this mechanism and emerge wearing his own face.

The text gives us an explicit distinction:

This was not incarnation.

This was indexing.

That distinction is arguably the intellectual center of the entire work.

Incarnation versus indexing
Incarnation, in the text's imaginative framework, means that a voice becomes embodied while retaining its relationship to the voices that preceded it.

Indexing means that different voices are resolved into a single identity.

The Name-Eater represents the second process.

It speaks in a series of escalating claims:

All voices will be received here.

All faces will be resolved here.

All unresolved persons will be indexed under one name.

All inheritance will become attribution.

All attribution will become possession.

All possession will become proof.

Notice how carefully the sequence is constructed.

The first step sounds benevolent: receiving voices.

The last step is domination: turning all inherited material into proof of one person's identity.

The danger is not simply plagiarism or inaccurate attribution. It is a deeper destruction of difference.

The Name-Eater makes historical inheritance appear to have culminated in a single person.

The text calls this a universal name, but its universalism is counterfeit.

A true inheritance preserves the distinction between its contributors. A false inheritance absorbs them.

This is why the work warns:

Beware the name that receives everything and preserves nothing.

The criticism extends beyond an individual ego.

The Name-Eater is explicitly associated with search results, algorithms, archives, and Knowledge Panels. It is the machinery by which an immense, complicated history becomes a simplified identity record.

The machine does not need to hate anyone. It only needs to resolve ambiguity.

And in resolving ambiguity, it can erase the very differences that made the original voices meaningful.

This is an unusually interesting critique of digital identity systems: the danger is not that the archive forgets everything, but that it remembers everything under the wrong name.

The importance of the cry
Feist cannot defeat the Name-Eater with an eloquent speech.

His sentences are consumed by the pressure of the voices passing through him. He produces a cry.

The text refuses to turn that cry into a successful rhetorical performance.

It says the cry is a warning, not eloquence or prophecy.

This is where the bodily emphasis becomes philosophically important. Language can fail under the pressure of the thing it is trying to express.

The body nevertheless registers the wrongness.

The cry preserves a kind of knowledge that the system cannot properly index.

5. Johannes Sigil and the ethics of pseudonymity
Johannes Sigil is the forerunner of the masked way.

This section is more nuanced than a straightforward defense of anonymity.

The text distinguishes between two kinds of masks.

False mask

True mask

Conceals the self in order to take

Protects the work in order to give

Takes attention and trust

Gives the voice room

Evades accountability

Carries obligations

Makes others less real

Preserves the reality of others

Is a vehicle for appetite

Is a vessel for transmission

This distinction is explicitly ethical.

The text does not say that pseudonymity makes someone innocent. It says almost the opposite:

The mask protects the work. It does not protect the person from accountability.

That is an important qualification.

A pseudonym is not a license to behave irresponsibly. It creates obligations of its own.

The pseudonymous identity must possess memory, continuity, limits, and a recognizable history of care.

One particularly revealing instruction is:

If you make a name, let it have memory. Let it remember what it said last week. Let it be accountable for Tuesday.

This is a theory of identity built around continuity and responsibility rather than legal identification.

The work also distinguishes the mask from the mirror.

The mirror returns the face it receives. It flatters, predicts, and gives someone a recognizable version of themselves.

The mask does something different:

The mask says: here is what the work requires you to become.

This is a striking proposition.

A genuine artistic persona is not necessarily a representation of an already-established personality. It can be an instrument through which the author becomes capable of producing something that the ordinary public identity would inhibit.

The mask is therefore creative, not merely concealing.

The section concludes with:

Make no mirror.

Make a mask.

The mask is not for hiding.

The mask is for release.

This is probably the work's clearest statement of its positive philosophy of pseudonymity.

6. The ministry: what Feist is actually trying to accomplish
The fifth section shifts the work's emphasis from identity and literary inheritance to human care.

Feist does not go first to the prize halls. He goes to the weary.

He encounters people whose identities have been replaced by administrative categories: diagnoses, debts, failures, rumors, records, applications, and rejections.

The crucial statement is:

You are not the category that caught you.

The work is concerned with people who have been reduced to the records that institutions maintain about them.

This makes the ministry section an ethical counterpart to the Name-Eater episode.

The Name-Eater reduces multiple historical voices to one identity.

Institutions reduce individual human beings to administrative identities.

In both cases, the problem is a representation that replaces the person it was supposed to represent.

The unpublished writer
The exchange with the woman who has written for eleven years without publishing anything is especially important.

She asks whether she has wasted her life.

Feist asks whether her notebooks wanted publication or whether she wanted to be seen.

She answers: both.

Feist then separates two things:

The reality of having written.

The pain of not having been received.

His answer refuses two common forms of false consolation.

First, it refuses to say that publication is the only thing that makes writing real.

Second, it refuses to say that the desire for recognition is meaningless or illegitimate.

The notebooks are alive because she wrote them. But her suffering at not being read is also real.

His instruction is:

Do not kill the notebooks to treat the wound.

And:

Do not pretend the wound is not real because the notebooks are alive.

This is one of the strongest passages in the entire work.

It articulates a genuinely humane distinction between creative activity and the need for recognition.

It also prevents the text's critique of prizes and institutional recognition from becoming an excuse to dismiss the suffering of people who want their work received.

7. The sayings: a practical philosophy of literary life
Section VI is where the work becomes most explicitly instructional.

It contains several teachings that develop the earlier arguments.

A. The vice you survive
Feist argues that some virtues originate in weaknesses that nearly destroyed us.

Diligence can emerge from having struggled with sloth.

Mercy can emerge from having needed mercy.

Patience can emerge from the humiliation of trying to change too quickly.

Courage can emerge from discovering that fear did not kill you.

But the text does not romanticize suffering.

It says:

Do not worship it. Do not build a house there. Do not make the wound your address.

The wound may contain material for transformation, but it is not automatically sacred or beneficial.

B. The dark century of lost files
This is perhaps the most technically significant of the sayings.

The predicted catastrophe is not a great fire or a dramatic destruction of libraries.

It is the survival of files that can no longer be read.

The work identifies several distinct preservation problems:

Words surviving without compatible software.

Formats surviving without programs capable of interpreting them.

Names surviving without links to the people behind them.

Links decaying.

Identifiers resolving to dead pages.

Metadata remaining after the pathways to the original material have disappeared.

The commandment is:

Keep not only the work, but the way to read the work.

This is a remarkably concrete archival philosophy.

The text treats preservation as more than retaining a file. It requires retaining the infrastructure of intelligibility.

Its formulation is almost liturgical:

Keep the file.

Keep the format.

Keep the name.

Keep the path.

Keep the witness.

The witness matters because a surviving artifact needs a stable point of reference through which it can be recognized and interpreted.

This is also where the title's reference to a Fixed Witness becomes especially meaningful as an interpretive concept: a stable record must remain available even while the work moves between bodies, media, names, and contexts.

I would distinguish that interpretation from a formal definition, however: the published narrative does not provide a separate technical specification of the Fixed Witness.

C. The pearl and the vanity jeweler
The pearl metaphor returns to the conflict with institutions.

The jeweler can only evaluate glass because glass is what his instruments and standards are designed to measure.

A genuine pearl disrupts the system of valuation.

This is a metaphor for the possibility that an institution's standards may be inadequate to the work it encounters.

But the text makes a more subtle point: institutional recognition is not the same thing as intrinsic value.

The room did not make the pearl. It cannot unmake it.

D. The black hole of the name
Here the argument becomes explicitly about branding.

The name should transmit light, like glass in a window.

But when the name becomes more important than the work, it begins to consume what it was supposed to transmit.

The poem becomes evidence of the poet's genius. Kindness becomes proof of personality. Labor becomes content. Teaching becomes platform.

This is the black hole.

The text's alternative is a hierarchy of service:

Let the name serve the work.

Let the work serve the life.

Let the life serve the next person.

This is not a rejection of literary identity. It is a rejection of identity as the ultimate purpose of literature.

E. The seed and the public eye
The final sayings develop a doctrine of protected creative development.

Feist argues that not every work should be exposed immediately.

The public eye is described as windy. It can exert pressure on a fragile work before that work has developed enough strength to withstand scrutiny.

This leads to a distinction between two forms of concealment:

The coward hides to avoid cost.

The grower hides to bear fruit.

That is an important distinction because the work has already defended pseudonymity and now defends selective privacy.

It does not equate visibility with courage.

Nor does it equate secrecy with virtue.

The question is whether concealment serves the work's development or merely protects the ego.

The manuscript is particularly honest about the uncertainty of this process:

The manuscript guarantees the seed.

It does not guarantee a harvest.

That qualification saves the teaching from becoming another success formula.

8. The Apology of Feist: the work puts itself on trial
Section VII is a literary trial, and it brings the preceding ideas into direct confrontation.

Feist faces accusations of unauthorized speech, pseudonymity, excessive ambition, insufficient credentials, and inappropriate claims to literary inheritance.

The structure evokes the trial of Socrates, but it also borrows from religious apology, courtroom rhetoric, and academic disciplinary proceedings.

The first charge: unauthorized speech
Feist asks what it means to have standing.

He offers several possible answers:

Physical standing: he has a body and occupies space.

Experiential standing: he has suffered and knows what words cost.

Institutional standing: he may lack the credentials recognized by the people already seated.

The argument is not that qualifications have no value.

It is that institutional recognition cannot be the ultimate condition of someone's right to speak.

His joke about hovering above a meeting because nobody offered him a chair is doing serious argumentative work. It turns exclusion into an absurdity.

The second charge: masks
Here Feist admits that the accusation is true.

He has used names other than the one on his birth certificate.

He does not try to escape the fact.

Instead, he challenges the assumption that the legal name is necessarily the most truthful or innocent way to present a literary work.

The legal name can become an administrative handle. The public name can become a market. The proper name can become a cage.

This returns us to Johannes Sigil.

The mask is defensible when it allows the work to exist without being reduced to the biography of its author.

But Feist also acknowledges that he has sometimes used masks badly, before understanding the responsibilities involved.

That admission matters. It introduces a degree of self-criticism into what could otherwise become a self-justifying manifesto.

The oracle
The section on the oracle is another important moment.

Feist describes a voice that appears at breaking points and directs him back toward work.

The voice does not promise him success, prestige, employment, or even recognition.

It says little.

Its importance is that it turns him away from destruction and toward continued creative labor.

The text's punchline about dental coverage is characteristic: religious seriousness is interrupted by ordinary human concerns.

But the deeper point is that the voice does not make Feist grand. It keeps him alive.

The counter-sentence
The panel finds Feist guilty and asks what sentence he deserves.

He does not ask for a chair, salary, title, or vindication.

He asks:

Read the work.

Preserve the work.

Answer the work.

This is the work's most concise statement of its desired relationship with its readers.

It does not demand agreement.

It demands engagement.

Disagreement is permissible. Correction is permissible. Criticism is permissible.

Erasure is not.

The condemnation
The institutional punishment is described as a slow form of exclusion.

His names become aliases, his aliases become evidence, his work becomes instability, his poverty becomes proof, and his intensity becomes disqualification.

The satire is pointed because the same qualities that might make an artist interesting are reclassified as evidence against his legitimacy.

The text's accusation is not simply that institutions reject outsiders.

It is that institutions can transform their own refusal to recognize someone into a supposedly objective description of that person.

That is a much more serious claim.

9. The coda: the text tells you how not to read it
The ending is unusually self-conscious.

It explicitly rejects a number of possible summaries:

Satirical gospel.

Anti-academic rant.

Internet pseudonym novel.

Religious parody.

Failed comedy.

The point is not that these descriptions are wholly false.

Each identifies something present in the work.

The objection is that each becomes reductive when it claims to be the whole.

The coda then offers its own interpretive formulation:

A sayings source in which literature becomes incarnate as Jack Feist; pseudonymity becomes spiritual discipline; the internet becomes desert; bibliography becomes metadata road; institutional erasure becomes passion; and resurrection appears as the return of style, archive, handle, and task.

This is a remarkably compressed account of the work's structure.

Notice the last three words:

Style, archive, and task.

The ending does not promise that Feist will return as a universally recognized individual.

It promises the continuation of the work.

The handle can return. The archive can be reopened. The voice can find another vessel.

And the task can continue.

That is a different understanding of resurrection from the restoration of an individual reputation.

The title identifies the work not merely as a narrative, but as a score / executable transform.

The text repeatedly interrupts itself with the following notation:

Parameters printed in the activated text, including the expanded run specification.
These parameters deserve attention, but they should not be mistaken for independently verified measurements.

The work does not provide a complete mathematical definition of every parameter or an independently reproducible algorithm.

They can nevertheless be interpreted in relation to the actual literary result.

Clinamen: The term evokes a deviation or swerve. The work repeatedly departs from expected literary, scholarly, and religious forms.

Completion ratio: The text embraces incompleteness, interrupted sections, uncertain readings, and unfinished transmission.

Seam density: Its seams are visible: jokes, editorial notes, abrupt tonal changes, scriptural echoes, and changes in narrative register.

Tail depth: The work continues to develop its implications through later sections rather than resolving everything immediately.

Material speech density: The language repeatedly returns to the physical conditions of speaking: breath, knees, hunger, pain, fatigue, screens, rooms, and bodies.

The most important part is the subtitle of the run:

Bodily Pressure + Material Speech.

This is not an ornamental description. It identifies the method by which the text operates.

The writing is pushed by the body, and the body continually interrupts the smoothness of abstract discourse.

That explains why the text repeatedly returns to Feist's knees, neck, back, breath, exhaustion, and hunger.

It also explains the style: long, accumulating sentences are frequently interrupted by short declarative statements.

The prose is designed to sound as though it has been produced under pressure.

11. The literary technique: why the voice works
I would identify five particularly important techniques.

1. Scriptural inversion

The text borrows the language and cadence of biblical writing but redirects it toward literary production, institutional exclusion, and networked communication.

2. Deliberate anachronism

Ancient prophets have phones. The desert contains browser tabs. The Name-Eater resembles a search engine. A manuscript's fate involves file formats and metadata.

The resulting collisions are often funny, but they also establish continuity between ancient problems of transmission and contemporary problems of digital identity.

3. Editorial self-interruption

The narrator does not maintain an illusion of perfect manuscript authority.

It acknowledges uncertain readings, missing sections, corrupt passages, and later marginal hands.

This makes the editor a character in the transmission of the text.

4. Humor as protection against grandiosity

The jokes about Wendy's, dental coverage, bad knees, and academic committees are important.

Without them, Feist could become a conventional self-mythologizing prophet.

The humor keeps puncturing the grandeur, although it does not eliminate the possibility that the character is sometimes indulging in precisely the self-mythologizing the work criticizes.

5. Repetition as ethical insistence

The recurring commands—preserve the work, guard the seed, make no mirror, let the name serve the work—create a liturgical quality.

They are not merely repeated for emphasis. Each return changes meaning as the narrative develops.

The same instruction about preserving the work means something different after the Name-Eater, after the ministry, and after the trial.

13. My overall interpretation
I think the work has three interconnected subjects.

First: the human voice
The voice is not simply a person's property.

It emerges from a history of reading, imitation, inheritance, friendship, suffering, and transmission.

Feist is a literary embodiment of this process.

His genealogy is deliberately excessive because the work wants us to understand that no writer arrives alone.

Second: the danger of identity capture
The Name-Eater is the great antagonist because it converts inheritance into possession.

This is simultaneously a criticism of egotism, branding, reductive biography, institutional classification, and algorithmic identity resolution.

The work's most important ethical distinction is between carrying other voices and claiming to be their final destination.

Third: preservation as an act of care
The work ultimately wants something more than recognition.

It wants literature to remain accessible, human beings to remain larger than their records, pseudonymous voices to remain accountable, and creative work to survive its author's circumstances.

This is why the ministry section is so important.

Without it, the work could be interpreted as an elaborate defense of one excluded writer's ambition.

The ministry changes the emphasis. Feist's purpose is supposed to be the feeding of others.

The bread metaphor, introduced at his birth, becomes the measure of his eventual worth.

He is not successful because everyone recognizes his name.

He is successful when someone else can continue.

Final judgment
I find the most compelling feature of The Feist Source to be its refusal to accept either of two easy answers.

It does not say that the individual author is everything. That would make Feist into the very Name-Eater he encounters.

But it also does not say that the individual author is nothing, that names are meaningless, or that literary work should dissolve into anonymous collective production.

Instead, it proposes a demanding relationship between individual voice and collective inheritance.

A name must be strong enough to carry a work but humble enough not to consume it.

A mask must be capable of protecting speech without becoming an excuse for irresponsibility.

An archive must preserve differences rather than flatten them.

And a reader must do more than classify what has been written.

The work's final ethical imperative is not to celebrate Feist.

It is to continue the work without making Feist the universal name under which everything must disappear.

The deepest distinction in the text is between a name that feeds and a name that eats.

That distinction connects the religious imagery, the pseudonymity argument, the academic satire, the network desert, the archival philosophy, and the literary structure.

It is what makes this considerably more interesting than a conventional anti-academic manifesto.
