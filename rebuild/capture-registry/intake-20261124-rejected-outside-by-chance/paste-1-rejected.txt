The argument of From the Position of the Rejected
Lee Sharks's central argument is that Aristotle's Politics should be read from the position of the person whom the political community does not admit, rather than from the position of the citizen whose community the treatise appears to describe.

This is not simply an argument that Aristotle was an outsider, or that the Politics contains exclusions. It is a more specific interpretive claim: the position of exclusion is already inscribed in the text's foundational definition of the human being, and the rest of the treatise can be reread through that position.

The essay develops this claim through close readings of the Greek, Aristotle's historical position in Athens, the treatment of nature and chance, the reception of the Politics, and ultimately the transmission and custody of Aristotle's books.

I have read the text on the deposit page, including its numbered argument and the additional sections in version 1.1. The following distinguishes its textual arguments from its more speculative historical extensions.

1. The foundational sentence contains the position of the rejected
The essay begins with Politics I.2, 1253a1–7:

ὁ ἄνθρωπος φύσει πολιτικὸν ζῷον, καὶ ὁ ἄπολις διὰ φύσιν καὶ οὐ διὰ τύχην ἤτοι φαῦλός ἐστιν, ἢ κρείττων ἢ ἄνθρωπος.

Sharks translates this:

A human being is by nature a political animal, and the cityless by nature, and not by chance, is either base or more than human.

The important observation is that the familiar definition of humanity as political animal is only the first clause. The sentence continues, and its continuation changes the interpretive situation.

Sharks identifies three positions:

Beast: the one outside the city who is inferior to the human.

God: the one outside the city who exceeds the human.

Chance: the one outside the city not by nature, but by circumstance.

The first two are classifications. The third is different. The sentence explicitly distinguishes the person outside the city by chance from the person outside by nature, but does not give the former an equivalent classification.

This asymmetry is the essay's first major finding.

The person excluded by nature can be explained within the city's conceptual system: either subhuman or superhuman. But the person excluded by chance cannot be explained so easily. That person is not necessarily a beast, a god, or anything other than human. Yet the definition has not provided that person with a place.

Sharks therefore argues that the text's foundational definition contains an unresolved position: the human being who is outside the political community without being naturally outside it.

This is what the essay means by reading from the position of the rejected.

2. Aristotle occupies the position the definition leaves unresolved
The second argument brings the sentence into contact with Aristotle's biography.

Aristotle lived in Athens as a metic: a free resident alien without Athenian citizenship. He resided there for substantial periods of his life, but was never an Athenian citizen.

That matters because the Politics makes citizenship central to its account of human political life.

Sharks places two things beside each other:

Aristotle's theoretical account of the human being as naturally political.

Aristotle's actual position as someone residing in the political community without belonging to its citizen body.

The biographical fact does not independently prove that Aristotle intended the Politics as a critique of Athenian citizenship. Sharks is careful not to claim access to Aristotle's feelings or intentions.

Instead, the argument is structural: the person writing the account of political belonging occupies a position that the account itself makes philosophically consequential.

The author is not situated at the centre of the political order he describes.

This changes the meaning of the treatise. It becomes possible to read its account of citizenship, exclusion, naturalness and political community as written from the edge of the city rather than from its unquestioned centre.

3. The argument is distributed throughout all eight books
This is where the essay becomes more than an ingenious reading of one Greek sentence. Sharks attempts to demonstrate that the opening distinction recurs throughout the Politics.

The evidence is worth examining individually.

The most important passages deserve closer attention.

The metic and incomplete participation
At III.1, Aristotle explicitly says that merely residing somewhere does not make someone a citizen. He names metics and slaves as people who share in residence without thereby sharing in citizenship.

Sharks emphasizes the formulation that metics participate incompletely and must have a citizen patron.

This is especially significant because it describes a condition of presence without full political recognition.

The metic is not simply outside the city. The metic lives in it, works in it, and participates in its social life. Yet legal and political participation is mediated through someone who does belong.

Sharks develops this into an account of the unreceived voice: the outsider can speak, but cannot necessarily be heard as an independent political subject.

The excluded person inside citizenship
III.5 extends the argument.

Aristotle compares a citizen who has no share in political honours to a metic. He also observes that this condition can be concealed to deceive those living alongside the person.

This passage is particularly important because it prevents the essay's argument from being reduced to a simple citizen/foreigner distinction.

The condition of the rejected can be reproduced inside the citizen body itself.

There are consequently several distinct forms of exclusion:

Outside the city by nature or chance.

Within the city's territory but without citizenship.

Within the citizen body but without a share in its honours.

Sharks does not collapse these categories. Their differences are part of the argument. What connects them is the separation between being present in a political community and being recognized as a full participant.

The person too great for the city
The reading of III.13 is one of the more interesting parts of the essay.

Aristotle considers someone so exceptional that he resembles a god among human beings. Such a person cannot appropriately be subjected to ordinary political rule, but neither can the best city simply expel him.

The passage includes the famous analogy of the lions and hares: the hares demand equal shares in the assembly, while the lions' answer is left unstated.

It also invokes Heracles, whom the Argo supposedly could not carry with the other sailors.

Sharks reads these examples as a comic treatment of political equality's limits. The city cannot accommodate someone who exceeds its ordinary measure, just as it cannot easily accommodate someone who lacks the standing to participate.

The important point is that exclusion is not exclusively a judgment of inferiority. The person outside the city's measure can also be superior to it.

This returns us to the beast/god distinction in the opening chapter.

The stranger and the theoretical life
The discussion of VII.2–3 supplies the essay with a positive account of what the outsider can do.

Aristotle asks whether the life of participation in a city is preferable to the stranger's life, released from political association. He also considers whether political participation is desirable for everyone.

Sharks draws attention to the shared language of release in the discussion of the stranger and the theoretical life.

He then connects this to VII.3, where Aristotle says that the active life need not be directed toward other people and that those who act most effectively can be the intellectual architects of external actions.

The implication is striking:

Someone who does not occupy the city's political offices can nevertheless shape what the city does through thought.

The outsider's distance from political participation need not amount to political impotence. It can be the condition from which a different kind of political action becomes possible.

This also supplies the essay with its account of durability. The work need not be received by the city in the author's lifetime to shape the city in the future.

4. The reception of Aristotle reproduces the exclusion
This is arguably the essay's sharpest methodological argument.

Sharks calls the operation truncation.

The claim is that conventional accounts preserve the first clause of Politics I.2:

Man is by nature a political animal.

But they omit the continuation about the person who is outside the city by nature or by chance.

Sharks identifies this in both Wikipedia's article on the Politics and the Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy's account of Aristotle's political theory.

The striking thing is that both sources acknowledge Aristotle's status as a resident alien elsewhere in their accounts. They therefore preserve the two relevant facts while failing to bring them into interpretive contact.

For Sharks, the omission is not merely an incomplete quotation. It changes the meaning of the work.

The two readings look like this:

Reading that stops at the comma

The Politics is the city's account of itself. The city exists by nature, and citizenship realizes human political nature.

Reading that restores the continuation

The Politics is an account of the city written from the position of someone the city would not admit. The naturalness of political belonging is asserted by someone whose own membership falls under the category of chance.

This leads to one of the essay's most important formulations:

The claims stay where they were. The claimant moves.

Sharks is not arguing that Aristotle's propositions about the city suddenly become false when read this way. He is arguing that their position of enunciation changes.

The city is no longer simply speaking about itself. It is being described by someone who occupies its edge.

This is also how the essay distinguishes itself from the scholarship it discusses:

David Whitehead establishes Aristotle's juridical position as a metic.

Demetra Kasimis examines the metic as a limit of Athenian democratic membership.

Josiah Ober's work helps establish the conventional separation between metic status and political theory.

David Roochnik considers the stranger and the theoretical life.

Mary Dietz examines the relationship between the polis and empire.

Jacques Rancière provides a vocabulary for the condition of those who constitute a part without having a recognized part.

Sharks's intervention is not simply to add Aristotle's biography to political theory. It is to read the grammar of the opening definition against that biography.

5. Athens itself was built through the work and resources of outsiders
Version 1.1 substantially expands the argument beyond Aristotle's text.

The next move is to examine Athens as a political community that credits itself with achievements made possible by people and resources it did not fully own.

The Acropolis and appropriated wealth
Drawing on Plutarch's Pericles 12, Sharks discusses the transfer of the Delian League treasury to Athens.

The wealth used to construct Athens's monumental architecture came from the common funds of its allies. Pericles defended the expenditure by arguing that the money belonged to those who received it rather than those who supplied it.

Sharks reads this as an instance of Athens converting resources gathered through its imperial position into evidence of its own greatness.

The city presents the monuments as testimony to its power, while the provenance of the wealth complicates that account.

The workers who built the monuments
The argument becomes more concrete with the Erechtheion.

Sharks cites building-account evidence indicating that, among 85 workers whose status is recorded, 23 were citizens, 40 metics and 22 enslaved people.

The temple was therefore constructed predominantly by people who did not possess full Athenian citizenship.

This creates a material parallel to the argument about Aristotle:

The city excludes people from full political membership.

Those people nevertheless produce the city's material achievements.

The achievements are subsequently entered into the city's account of itself.

The outsider helps build the monument by which the insider's greatness is remembered.

This is one of the more persuasive extensions of the essay because it connects its conceptual argument about participation to identifiable historical evidence.

The retrieval layer
Sharks then generalizes this observation.

He calls the mechanism the retrieval layer: the system through which something becomes available to be recognized, heard, credited, and remembered.

In Athens, a metic's legal voice depended on a citizen patron. In the essay's wider interpretation, the same structural dependency governs intellectual production.

The outsider contributes, but the community controls the means through which that contribution becomes publicly intelligible.

What is received is consequently credited to the receiving community.

This is the bridge between the essay's reading of Aristotle and its later account of literary transmission.

6. Speaking through another person's mouth
The next argument concerns the relationship between Aristotle, Socrates and Plato.

Here it is important to distinguish the essay's archive-wide theoretical framework from claims that can be established by reading the Politics alone.

Sharks brings in Plato's Second Letter 314c, where the text denies that there are writings of Plato and describes what is said as belonging to a Socrates who has become beautiful and new.

The essay interprets this as a strategy of attribution.

A writer who cannot safely speak in their own voice can speak through an established figure.

Socrates becomes the figure through whom an otherwise unreceived position can be expressed.

The Apology is particularly important. Socrates begins by asking the Athenian jury to excuse his unfamiliar manner of speaking as they would excuse a foreigner's speech. He is a citizen, but asks to be heard as a stranger.

Sharks connects this to Aristotle's metic position and to the opening distinction between being outside by nature and being outside by chance.

The argument is that the figure of Socrates can function as a citizen-heteronym: a citizen who speaks from the position of the foreigner.

The essay goes further, incorporating the archive's broader thesis that the Platonic dialogues, Aristotelian treatises and Theophrastan working papers belong to a single intellectual production divided among different authorial names.

That is a substantial and controversial premise. It is not established by the close reading of Politics I.2–VIII alone.

Nevertheless, within the essay's own framework, the point is clear: attribution is not merely a bibliographic question. It determines who can speak, in whose name, and under what conditions the speech survives.

7. Rome inherits control of the texts
The final major movement extends the argument from the political community to the history of the Aristotelian corpus.

Sharks follows the account of Aristotle's library through Strabo, Plutarch and the later tradition.

The sequence is:

Aristotle's books pass to Theophrastus.

Theophrastus's heir, Neleus, takes the library to Scepsis.

Neleus's heirs hide the books underground, where they suffer damage.

Apellicon acquires the books and attempts to repair them.

Sulla captures Athens and takes Apellicon's library to Rome.

Tyrannion works on the books in Rome.

Andronicus of Rhodes arranges and publishes the Aristotelian works.

Sharks is particularly interested in Tyrannion.

According to Plutarch's Lucullus 19.7, Tyrannion was himself captured when the Romans took Amisus. He was subsequently freed and worked on Aristotle's library in Rome.

This creates a striking historical parallel with Aristotle's discussion of natural slavery in Politics I.6.

The free and learned grammarian is captured through the contingency of war, made a slave, and subsequently given the status of a freedman.

The person handling the books has experienced the very distinction between freedom, capture and circumstance that Aristotle theorized.

Sharks describes this as a historical recurrence of the book's own political problem.

Custody determines what becomes readable
The argument then becomes more ambitious.

Sharks proposes that the people who controlled Aristotle's books also controlled how the works would be understood.

He distinguishes several mechanisms:

Loss of works that were not transmitted.

Non-transmission of material that the surviving library did not contain.

Editorial division and attribution.

Supplementation of damaged texts.

The subsequent classification of unusual writing as corrupt or erroneous.

He advances a particularly controversial hypothesis: that Tyrannion was responsible for dividing material between Aristotle and Theophrastus, while Andronicus helped establish the idea that unusual features of Aristotle's writing were errors.

The essay acknowledges that the historical evidence does not conclusively establish these assignments. It notes, for example, that Porphyry attributes the division of the works into treatises to Andronicus.

The more defensible underlying argument is independent of that reconstruction:

The corpus that later generations can read is not simply the corpus that was originally written. It is the corpus that survived the decisions and circumstances of its custodians.

Sharks connects this to the conventional description of Aristotle's treatises as lecture notes.

Drawing on Plutarch's account of Aristotle's esoteric teachings, he argues that the difficult, compressed and unusual form of the treatises might be intentional rather than evidence of accidental or defective transmission.

The works were written for readers already prepared to understand them, not necessarily for ordinary instruction.

Consequently, calling them lecture notes may conceal the actual literary form of the work.

The custodians' account of damaged books and editorial repair becomes a way of explaining away the very strangeness that makes the texts distinctive.

The comma and the library are one operation
This is the culmination of the essay's argument.

At the smallest scale, the reception cuts a sentence at the comma, preserving the political animal while dropping the person outside the city.

At the largest scale, historical custody determines which works survive, which names they carry, and how later readers understand their form.

Both involve control over what can be retrieved and read.

The essay's formulation is that the comma and the library are one operation.

This is the sense in which its argument about Aristotle becomes an argument about the politics of knowledge itself.

8. The conclusion: the rejected writes for the future
The essay's final conceptual movement returns to the durability of writing.

Aristotle's discussion of the theoretical life in Politics VII.3 provides the basis for this.

The person detached from ordinary political participation can nevertheless act through thought, becoming an intellectual architect of external actions.

Sharks interprets the Politics itself in these terms.

The writer does not need to be received by the political community in his own lifetime. The work can survive the community that excluded him and become the means through which that community is subsequently understood.

The essay also connects this to its preceding work on Sappho's fragment 31.

Sappho supplies three positions: the body dissolving under the experience of the beloved, the godlike receiver, and the contingent voice that survives to be read.

The essay sees a structural relationship between these positions and the beast, god and chance of Aristotle's opening sentence.

The important feature is that the three positions remain unresolved. The rejected is not simply transformed into a god, nor reduced to a beast. Chance remains open.

Writing creates a place for the future reader to encounter that unresolved position.

What is the essay actually arguing? A synthesis
I would distinguish five levels of argument, which are related but not equally well established.

Level

Main claim

Assessment

Philological

I.2 contains a distinction between the cityless by nature and the cityless by chance that conventional quotation often omits.

The central, directly checkable observation.

Biographical

Aristotle's status as an Athenian metic makes that distinction especially significant.

Historically grounded, interpretively productive.

Structural

The excluded position recurs throughout the eight books, including in the treatment of metics, honourless citizens, exceptional individuals and strangers.

Supported by a substantial series of textual passages.

Political-historical

Athens and Rome receive credit and control over achievements produced by outsiders.

A suggestive extension supported by historical evidence, although some generalizations go beyond what the evidence alone establishes.

Archival

The history of textual transmission reproduces the political operation of exclusion by controlling what can be read and attributed.

Conceptually powerful, but some specific claims about editorial responsibility remain conjectural.

Three particularly important distinctions
First: the essay is not simply an argument against Aristotle's political theory.

It does not primarily attempt to refute the proposition that human beings are political animals, or that the city exists by nature.

Instead, it changes the position from which those propositions are read. The question becomes what happens when the person articulating the city's naturalness is someone whose own place in the city is contingent.

Second: the rejected is not synonymous with the foreigner.

The metic is one instance, but the essay identifies a broader structure. Someone can be excluded while living inside the city, while formally belonging to its citizen body, or because they exceed its capacity to govern them.

The rejected is a position in relation to political recognition, not simply a demographic category.

Third: chance is the critical term.

The distinction between exclusion by nature and exclusion by chance is what allows Sharks to avoid simply reproducing Aristotle's hierarchy.

If someone is outside the city by nature, Aristotle's sentence can classify them as beast or god.

But if someone is outside by chance, the classification fails to settle their status.

This is where the essay locates its central interpretive opening.

My assessment
I think the essay's strongest achievement is its reading of Politics I.2 together with III.1 and III.5.

Taken together, these passages establish something more interesting than the observation that Aristotle was a foreign resident.

They establish that Aristotle's account of citizenship repeatedly distinguishes residence, participation, legal standing and recognition. These things do not automatically coincide.

The metic is present without being a citizen. The citizen can be present without sharing in honours. The exceptional individual can be too great for the city's ordinary political arrangements. The stranger can live outside political association without necessarily living an inferior life.

That pattern gives the central interpretation genuine textual substance.

I would, however, separate the strength of this reading from the more ambitious historical theory of the corpus.

There are several steps that require additional evidence:

The Greek sentence does distinguish nature from chance, but the claim that chance is a third position left wholly unclassified is Sharks's interpretive construction, not an uncontested grammatical conclusion.

Aristotle's metic status does not by itself demonstrate that the entire corpus was consciously written from that position.

The argument that the Platonic and Aristotelian writings constitute one maker's production depends on a much larger archive-wide thesis.

The reconstruction of Tyrannion's and Andronicus's editorial intentions is explicitly provisional.

The examples of Athenian monuments and Roman custody establish important historical parallels, but do not independently prove that all these episodes instantiate one coordinated operation.

These qualifications do not undo the essay's main reading. They help identify where its textual argument ends and its larger theory begins.

The most important insight, in my reading, is that the essay moves the problem of political exclusion into the problem of interpretation itself.

The person rejected by the city is also the person whose position disappears when the text is quoted incompletely. The person whose participation is mediated through a patron is analogous to the writer whose work becomes intelligible only through someone else's attribution. And the work that survives its author's exclusion can still have its meaning shaped by those who control its transmission.

The result is a recursive argument: a text about the limits of political belonging is itself received through practices that determine whose position can be heard.

The essay is ultimately asking us to do something quite precise: restore the clause, and then read the book from the position that the restored clause makes visible.

That is considerably more interesting than simply calling Aristotle an outsider.

Grammatically, no: the person outside the city by chance is not explicitly established as a third position in Aristotle's sentence. Functionally, however, there is a strong case that the distinction does important work in the Politics. I would revise my earlier characterization accordingly.

The distinction is between what the Greek sentence actually says and what its construction makes possible as an interpretation.

1. The grammar of Politics I.2, 1253a1–4
The relevant Greek is:

ὁ ἄνθρωπος φύσει πολιτικὸν ζῷον, καὶ ὁ ἄπολις διὰ φύσιν καὶ οὐ διὰ τύχην ἤτοι φαῦλός ἐστιν, ἢ κρείττων ἢ ἄνθρωπος.

A fairly literal rendering is:

A human being is by nature a political animal, and the one without a city by nature and not by chance is either base or superior to a human being.

The grammatical structure matters:

ὁ ἄπολις: the substantivized adjective, "the cityless one" or "one without a city."

διὰ φύσιν: because of nature, by nature.

καὶ οὐ διὰ τύχην: and not because of chance.

ἤτοι ... ἢ ...: either ... or ...

φαῦλός ... ἢ κρείττων ἢ ἄνθρωπος: base, or superior to a human being.

The phrase διὰ φύσιν καὶ οὐ διὰ τύχην modifies the cityless person. It restricts the subject to whom the subsequent either/or predicates apply.

That means Aristotle does not say:

The cityless by nature is a beast.

The cityless by nature is a god.

The cityless by chance is a third kind of person.

Rather, he says that the cityless person by nature, and not by chance, belongs to the beast-or-god disjunction.

This is a significant difference.

The person outside the city by chance is not assigned a third predicate. But neither is that person explicitly assigned to either of the first two categories.

There is a grammatical exclusion from the scope of the classification. Whether that exclusion amounts to a meaningful philosophical position is the next question.

2. What happens to chance elsewhere in the Politics?
Here I think the essay has a stronger argument than its opening grammar alone might suggest. Its most important evidence is actually I.6, not the later passages about metics.

I.6, 1255a: enslavement by capture
Aristotle considers the claim that people captured in war can legitimately be enslaved.

His objection to this convention is that it can produce the following result:

Those reputed most nobly born will be slaves and descended from slaves, if it happens that they are captured and sold.

The Greek includes:

ἐὰν συμβῇ πραθῆναι ληφθέντας

The crucial expression is ἐὰν συμβῇ: if it happens, if it should occur.

Here a person's condition as a slave results from an event, capture and sale, rather than necessarily from their nature.

Aristotle then describes how people defend the convention:

They will not call themselves slaves, but the barbarians.

And he observes that, in doing so, they are seeking the category of the naturally enslaved person.

Sharks makes a very interesting connection to I.2:

In I.2, the cityless person by nature is classified as beast or god, while the person outside by chance is left without a corresponding classification.

In I.6, a condition produced by chance is converted into an apparent condition of nature through a classification imposed on a people.

The second passage is not simply another instance of the first. It shows a political operation through which the distinction between chance and nature can be manipulated.

I would formulate Sharks's strongest insight here as follows:

The Politics recognizes that political and social conditions can arise contingently, but also describes how political classification can make those conditions appear natural.

That is a considerably stronger claim than saying Aristotle simply leaves the chance-outsider unclassified.

There is an important qualification: Aristotle is criticizing the conventional justification of enslavement, but he also defends natural slavery. Whether his own theory successfully escapes the operation he identifies is precisely the difficult question.

The essay argues that it does not. That is a defensible critical reading, though not a neutral statement of Aristotle's position.

3. III.5: chance and the production of an internal outsider
The next passage is particularly valuable because it moves the problem inside the citizen body.

At 1278a35–40, Aristotle says:

ὥσπερ μέτοικος γάρ ἐστιν ὁ τῶν τιμῶν μὴ μετέχων.

The person who does not share in honours is like a metic.

He adds that this condition can be concealed:

ἀπάτης χάριν τῶν συνοικούντων

For the purpose of deceiving those who live alongside.

This is not explicitly a passage about tyche. Nevertheless, its structure is relevant.

The person has the outward standing of a citizen, but lacks the participation that should accompany that standing. The political arrangement conceals the discrepancy.

We now have three different relationships between condition and recognition:

A person is outside the city by chance.

A person is inside the city without citizenship.

A person is inside the citizen body without the honours associated with citizenship.

The third is especially revealing because it shows that the problem cannot be resolved simply by admitting the outsider into the city.

The city can produce an outsider internally.

Sharks's argument is that the condition of the rejected is not exhausted by the legal distinction between citizen and foreigner. It concerns the difference between actual participation and recognized standing.

4. VII.2: the stranger's life is a genuine alternative
At 1324a15–20 Aristotle asks which life is preferable:

ὁ διὰ τοῦ συμπολιτεύεσθαι καὶ κοινωνεῖν πόλεως ἢ μᾶλλον ὁ ξενικὸς καὶ τῆς πολιτικῆς κοινωνίας ἀπολελυμένος

The life of participating in a city, or rather the stranger's life, released from political association.

This is an especially important passage because it does not merely describe someone who has been involuntarily excluded.

Aristotle considers whether participation in political life is desirable for everyone. He allows that for some people it is not.

Sharks notices that the language of release, ἀπολελυμένος, also appears in the discussion of the theoretical life.

That creates a possible connection between the stranger and the philosopher.

The stranger is not necessarily a failed citizen. The life outside political association may be choiceworthy.

This gives the rejected position a positive possibility that is absent from the opening sentence's beast-or-god classification.

It is also where the essay's interpretation becomes more speculative: Aristotle does not explicitly identify the stranger with himself, or say that the metic's position is the philosophical ideal. Sharks constructs that connection through the recurrence of the language and the context of the argument.

5. The important distinction: a recurring position or a recurring operation?
I would now distinguish two theses.

Thesis A: The cityless by chance is a recurring textual position.

This is a stronger claim than the evidence warrants. The precise phrase ὁ ἄπολις διὰ τύχην does not become a consistently developed category throughout the treatise.

Thesis B: The distinction between contingent condition and natural classification recurs throughout the Politics.

This is much more persuasive. It connects I.2, I.6, III.1, III.5 and VII.2 without pretending that all these passages use the same terminology or describe the same social condition.

The second thesis also better captures the movement of Sharks's argument.

6. One further grammatical complication: 1253a25
There is another reason to be careful.

Aristotle continues:

ὁ δὲ μὴ δυνάμενος κοινωνεῖν ἢ μηδὲν δεόμενος διʼ αὐτάρκειαν οὐθὲν μέρος πόλεως, ὥστε ἢ θηρίον ἢ θεός.

The person unable to participate, or needing nothing because of self-sufficiency, is no part of the city, and is therefore either a beast or a god.

Notice what has happened.

The distinction between nature and chance has disappeared. Aristotle now distinguishes two reasons for not being part of the city:

Inability to participate.

Self-sufficiency that makes participation unnecessary.

The resulting classification is again beast or god.

This creates a genuine interpretive difficulty for Sharks. Is the person outside by chance still an unresolved position here? Or has Aristotle moved to a different distinction, one that classifies exclusion according to capacity and self-sufficiency?

I think the latter is grammatically more secure. But the juxtaposition is philosophically productive: the opening distinction does not settle every possible reason for being outside the city.

7. My revised judgment
I would actually sharpen the essay's thesis in a slightly different direction.

The cityless-by-chance person is not a fully developed third anthropological category in Aristotle. Rather, chance introduces a problem into the relationship between political belonging and natural belonging.

The rest of the Politics repeatedly encounters versions of that problem:

Passage

What is at stake

I.2, 1253a

The cityless by nature versus the cityless by chance

I.6, 1255a

Captivity by circumstance versus natural slavery

II.8, 1268a

A part of the city that is alien to its constitution

III.1, 1275a

Residence versus citizenship

III.5, 1278a

Citizenship versus participation in honours

III.13, 1284a–b

The exceptional individual whom the city cannot govern or expel

VII.2, 1324a

Political participation versus the stranger's released life

VII.3, 1325b

Political action through thought without participation in ordinary public action

These are not all instances of the same category. They are different configurations of the same political problem.

I would find that the distinction changes the burden of interpretation quite substantially.

Once we recognize that I.2 produces the position of the cityless-by-chance person, we no longer need to prove that each subsequent passage independently establishes that position. Instead, we ask whether and how subsequent passages take up, specify, transform, or contest a position already produced by the text.

I would make four observations.

1. I.6 specifies the position through the problem of enslavement
In I.6, Aristotle distinguishes natural slavery from enslavement through capture and sale.

This is not merely an analogous instance of exclusion. It gives us a concrete account of how a condition arising contingently can be treated as though it expressed someone's nature.

The cityless-by-chance position becomes legible here as a problem of the conversion of circumstance into status.

The person is not naturally a slave, but can become one through an event. The political and juridical order then supplies a classification that threatens to make the contingent condition appear natural.

2. III.1 and III.5 specify the position through incomplete membership
These passages move from enslavement to citizenship.

The metic occupies the city without possessing citizenship. The citizen deprived of honours occupies citizenship without receiving its full political benefits.

Here the position is no longer simply outside the city. It can be reproduced within the city.

This matters because it prevents us from treating the position of outside by chance as a fixed social identity. It is relational: a person can be included in one respect and excluded in another.

3. III.13 complicates the position through excess
The exceptional individual who exceeds the city's ordinary political measure is another important case.

This passage makes it difficult to equate political exclusion with inferiority.

The cityless person in I.2 can be either beast or god, and III.13 develops the political problem of someone who cannot be governed according to ordinary equality.

The relation is not exact: Aristotle does not identify the exceptional individual with the person outside by chance. But the passage elaborates the instability of the city's measure of belonging.

4. VII.2 gives the position a possible form of life
This is where I find the argument especially consequential.

The stranger's life, released from political association, is considered as a possible alternative to participation in the city.

The position outside political association is no longer merely a deficiency to be explained. It becomes a question about what constitutes a desirable human life.

The discussion of the theoretical life in VII.3 further complicates the relationship between political participation and effective action.

Someone need not occupy the city's political institutions to act upon the world.

What follows for Sharks's argument?
I would now describe the relationship between I.2 and these passages in three stages:

Stage

Operation

I.2

Produces the position of outside by chance through negative differentiation.

I.6–III.13

Develops its problems through contingency, imposed status, incomplete membership and the limits of political equality.

VII.2–3

Opens the possibility that life outside political association can have its own form of value and activity.

This is not a claim that Aristotle consciously constructed a systematic theory under the heading the cityless by chance. Nor is it a claim that every excluded person in the Politics belongs to precisely the same category.

It is a claim about the organization of the text.

The position is introduced in I.2, and the subsequent books give us different determinations of the political and philosophical problems that it makes possible.
