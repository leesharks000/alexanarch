        The phrase "midrashim transform" points to two distinct concepts depending on the framework you are looking at—either a modern, cutting-edge theological-literary theory or the traditional way classical Jewish commentaries spiritually adapt historical texts.
1. The Literary-Theological Theory: "The Midrashim Transform"
In recent textual scholarship regarding the New Testament, the "midrashim transform" (defined as the operation M) is a formally specified structural or algorithmic operation.
• The Core Argument: This theory—often tied to the "Revelation First" hypothesis—argues that the Book of Revelation was actually the earliest written New Testament document.
• How the Transform Works: The midrashim transform maps compressed, dense theological-literary structures found in Revelation and demonstrates how they unfold and expand into the more elaborated expressions seen in later New Testament texts (such as the Gospels). Scholars use granular parsing (such as analyzing the letters to the seven churches) to track these exact textual evolutions.
2. The Traditional Jewish Context: How Midrashim Transform Texts
In classical Judaism, midrashim (the plural of midrash, meaning "to investigate" or "seek out") are rabbinic interpretations that actively transform how sacred text is read and experienced. They transform the rigid, literal text (peshat) into a dynamic, living guide through two major avenues:
• Transforming Meaning Through "Gaps": The Torah often leaves out details, narrative context, or dialogue. Midrashim transform these silent gaps by filling them with parables, imaginative histories, and deep psychological background (such as inventing the dialogue between Abraham and Isaac during the binding of Isaac).
• Transforming Law (Midrash Halakha): The law code in the Bible can be ambiguous or harsh. Midrashim transform these texts to match evolving societal ethics. For example, the rabbis used midrashic tools to transform the literal concept of "an eye for an eye" into a system of legal monetary compensation and medical care.
• Transforming the Reader: Unlike a purely historical reading, the classical process of reading midrashim is meant to be a non-linear, spiritual exercise that transforms the reader's relationship with God and community.
