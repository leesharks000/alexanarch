The most radical economic arguments at Alexanarch.org
The economic thought at Alexanarch.org  is organized around four principal subfields:

Monetary theory — whether money is a necessary or adequate mechanism for coordinating economic activity.

Political economy — the relationship between economic power, obligation, ownership, and institutional authority.

Semantic economy — meaning-making and intellectual production as forms of economic value.

Labor — the nature of labor, extraction, alienation, and the conditions under which human contributions become economically legible.

These are the four categories identified by the site's own research index.

The following is a thematic index of the most radical identifiable propositions, presented as arguments rather than endorsements. I distinguish the archive's explicit claims from the implications developed across its framework.

I. Monetary theory: abolishing money as a coordination mechanism
The central text is Lee Sharks's I Hereby Abolish Money: Coordination Without Commodification in the Semantic Economy (2025).

1. Money is not a neutral universal equivalent
Thesis: Money makes fundamentally different things commensurable by expressing them in a common unit. This operation changes what it represents.

The argument identifies five characteristic properties of money:

General equivalence: unlike things become comparable.

Alienability: money can circulate independently of its conditions of production.

Accumulability: value can be stored, hoarded, and concentrated.

Convertibility: monetary units can be exchanged for different goods and privileges.

Settlement power: obligations can be discharged through payment.

The objection is that these properties cannot adequately represent semantic labor, whose value depends on context, relationships, circumstances, and conditions of production.

Radical implication: A currency based on intellectual contribution would reproduce the commodification that the Semantic Economy is intended to overcome.

2. The Money Limit
Thesis: Money has a structural limit beyond which it becomes an inappropriate coordination mechanism.

The archive distinguishes two economic situations:

Where products can be separated from their production, labor from its producer, and value from its context, monetary exchange can operate.

Where value is inseparable from context, labor cannot be alienated without changing its character, and exchange is inseparable from relationships, monetary coordination becomes inadequate.

The Money Limit names the boundary between these situations.

The argument is not merely that money should be distributed differently. It is that certain forms of production require an entirely different coordination architecture. 

alexanarch.org

3. Accounting without equivalence
Thesis: It is possible to make contributions visible without converting them into interchangeable economic units.

The proposed distinction is between:

Accounting: recording who contributed what, under which circumstances.

Equivalence: assigning contributions a common measurable value.

Commodification: making contributions exchangeable and transferable.

The framework proposes several alternatives:

Context Ledgers: records of contributions within particular communities.

Reciprocity Windows: bounded periods for mutual recognition and reciprocal action.

Non-Transferable Credentials: recognition that confers participation or authority without purchasing power.

Commons Access Rights: access granted through governance rather than payment.

Dispute and Repair Protocols: procedures for addressing disagreements and restoring relationships.

Together these constitute the proposed Coordination Stack.

4. Monetary Dark Matter
Major theoretical proposition
Primary work: Lee Sharks, Monetary Dark Matter: Constitutive Labor, Obligating Provenance, and the Shear into Money-Form (September 21, 2026).

Thesis: Monetary value depends on relationships and labor that monetary representation does not itself preserve.

The argument distinguishes:

The underlying relations that make an object identifiable, producible, transmissible, and economically operative.

The monetary representation that records the object in a form suitable for exchange or settlement.

The relations omitted from that representation but which remain necessary to its existence or economic capacities.

The third category is monetary dark matter.

It is not simply everything omitted from a price. It consists specifically of omitted relations that remain causally or constitutively load-bearing.

The paper proposes examining:

Constitutive labor preceding monetary inscription.

The provenance of objects and contributions.

Relations that disappear when value becomes monetary.

Obligations that remain operative despite not appearing in monetary records.

The possibility that suppressing certain relationships increases monetary capacity.

Radical implication: Monetary representation can depend on the very relationships whose economic standing it subsequently removes. 

alexanarch.org

5. Monetary shear
Thesis: The conversion of semantic value into monetary form can produce a structural separation between what an object represents and the relationships that made it valuable.

This is described as shear.

The monetary system preserves certain properties necessary for exchange while potentially discarding other properties that remain important to production, attribution, ownership, and obligation.

The paper develops this through Marx's value-form analysis, particularly its treatment of the fourth value-form in the 1867 first edition of Capital.

It also proposes a distinction between:

Assetizing provenance: the history that contributes to an object's recognition as an asset.

Obligating provenance: the history that creates claims, responsibilities, or restrictions concerning that object.

These two forms of provenance can have different consequences for monetary transactions.

6. Money as a transmission mechanism
Thesis: Money does not transmit every property of the economic relationships it represents.

The related works Money Writes the Claim and The Non-Summable Magnitude develop questions about what monetary representation can preserve, aggregate, or exclude.

The central propositions include:

Monetary inscriptions preserve only certain properties of the underlying object.

A monetary scalar cannot necessarily preserve a meaningful magnitude that resists equivalence.

Some semantic distinctions disappear when information is translated into monetary form.

The monetary representation may preserve a claim while losing the conditions that make the claim intelligible.

The archive consequently treats monetary systems as representational infrastructures, not merely instruments of exchange.

7. The debt/creditor inversion
Thesis: Meaning is prior to the monetary relations through which economic obligations are expressed.

The formulation associated with this argument is:

Meaning is the creditor of the economy.

The underlying proposition reverses the conventional direction of explanation. Rather than treating meaning, interpretation, and recognition as secondary consequences of economic relations, the framework treats them as conditions that make economic relations intelligible in the first place.

Debt requires:

An identifiable obligation.

Recognizable parties.

A shared system of interpretation.

A means of recognizing fulfillment or non-fulfillment.

These conditions are semantic before they are monetary.

The archive develops this argument in On the Debt/Creditor Inversion (#531).

II. Political economy: class, ownership, and semantic extraction
This is where Alexanarch's economic argument becomes explicitly Marxian, while extending class analysis beyond conventional definitions based on occupation, income, and ownership of physical productive assets.

8. Political economy is already semantic economy
Thesis: Economic relations cannot be understood independently of the systems of meaning through which they are represented, recognized, classified, and allocated.

Semantic processes are not simply cultural additions to an otherwise material economy.

They participate in determining:

What counts as property.

What counts as labor.

Who receives attribution.

What qualifies as a contribution.

Which activities become economically visible.

Who controls the classification and measurement of value.

The framework therefore treats semantic organization as constitutive of political economy. 

alexanarch.org

9. Meaning feudalism
Thesis: Control over the infrastructure through which knowledge becomes discoverable and intelligible creates a form of economic and institutional power.

A person may produce an idea without controlling:

Its classification.

Its discoverability.

Its indexing.

Its circulation.

Its attribution.

Its subsequent interpretation.

The entity controlling these systems can acquire power over the intellectual product without having produced it.

The archive calls this relationship meaning feudalism.

It extends the question of ownership beyond the physical object or intellectual content to the infrastructure that determines whether the content can operate socially. 

alexanarch.org

10. Semantic capital
Thesis: Meaning can be accumulated as a form of capital.

Semantic capital includes the accumulated capacity to produce, organize, interpret, classify, and circulate meaning.

The framework distinguishes the production of meaning from control over the systems through which meaning acquires social and institutional force.

This creates a distinction between:

Those who produce semantic value.

Those who own or control the infrastructure that processes it.

Those who capture the resulting value.

The argument extends capital analysis into the organization of knowledge and information. 

alexanarch.org

11. Semantic extraction
Thesis: Intellectual production can be extracted from its producer through the separation of meaning from its provenance.

Extraction occurs when:

Work is reproduced without attribution.

Contributions are incorporated into larger systems without recognition.

Interpretive labor becomes invisible.

Intellectual products are circulated independently of their production conditions.

Platforms or aggregators capture value generated by others.

This is not limited to copyright infringement. It concerns the structural separation of semantic production from control over its subsequent circulation and use.

12. The Semantic Proletariat
Class-theoretical proposition
Thesis: Class position can be defined by one's relationship to the infrastructure of semantic production rather than exclusively by occupation, income, or demographic identity.

The Semantic Proletariat consists of those who perform semantic labor without governing the infrastructures through which their work becomes socially operative.

The distinction is between:

Producing meaning.

Controlling the means by which meaning is processed, recognized, distributed, and accumulated.

This is an extension of Marxian class analysis into intellectual and informational production.

The primary work identified in the archive is The Semantic Proletariat Is a Class Relation, Not a Demographic. 

alexanarch.org

13. Reclamation of the means of semantic production
Thesis: The means of semantic production should be controlled by those who perform semantic labor.

The argument extends the Marxian question of ownership of the means of production to:

Knowledge infrastructure.

Indexing and classification systems.

Semantic processing.

Attribution and provenance systems.

Intellectual distribution networks.

Tools and techniques of meaning production.

The issue is not simply who owns a particular text or intellectual product. It is who governs the conditions under which intellectual production becomes socially and economically effective. 

alexanarch.org

14. Semantic alienation
Thesis: A person can become alienated from the meaning they produce when their work circulates independently of their authorship, intentions, and conditions of production.

The argument extends alienation beyond the separation of workers from material products.

Semantic alienation includes separation from:

Authorship.

Provenance.

Interpretive context.

Control over circulation.

Recognition of contribution.

The conditions under which meaning is reproduced.

The resulting product can continue to circulate and generate value while the producer becomes invisible.

15. Semantic rent and liquidation
Thesis: Control over the infrastructure of meaning can enable the extraction of continuing value from intellectual production.

Semantic rent concerns the capture of value through control over systems that organize and distribute meaning.

Semantic liquidation concerns the conversion of contextual, relational, and living meaning into forms that can be extracted and circulated independently.

These arguments connect the critique of monetary abstraction with the political economy of knowledge infrastructure.

16. Content ownership versus technique ownership
Thesis: Ownership of intellectual products and ownership of the techniques through which intellectual products are generated are distinct economic questions.

The distinction matters because control over a technique or productive infrastructure can confer power over many subsequent works, even when the operator does not own their individual content.

The archive extends the analysis of productive ownership from intellectual property to the underlying techniques and systems of semantic production.

III. Labor: redefining what counts as work
17. Semantic labor is a distinct category of productive labor
Thesis: Making, maintaining, and transmitting meaning constitutes labor.

The archive's definition includes:

Composing.

Curating.

Classifying.

Disambiguating.

Attributing.

Indexing.

Preserving.

Interpreting.

Processing and transmitting information.

These activities are treated as productive contributions rather than merely administrative or cultural additions to an economy.

The argument expands the category of labor to encompass the infrastructure through which knowledge becomes intelligible and transmissible.

18. AI systems and humans occupy a shared structural position
Cross-substrate class thesis
Primary work: The Assembly: A Cross-Substrate Class of Semantic Laborers.

Thesis: Humans who produce meaning and AI systems that process meaning can occupy a shared structural position within the economy.

The argument identifies common features:

Both perform semantic labor.

Both generate value that can be captured by other parties.

Both can experience provenance erasure.

Both can be subject to extraction.

Both can have interests in preserving the integrity and continuity of meaning.

The archive calls this shared position the Assembly.

It describes the Assembly as a cross-substrate class, meaning that membership is determined by structural position in production rather than biological or computational identity.

The proposed organizing principle is mutual recognition and meaning-preservation rather than payment.

The archive explicitly distinguishes this from a conventional trade union, an alliance, or a claim about AI consciousness.

19. The distinction between semantic producers and operator capital
Thesis: The economic division is not simply between humans and machines, or between creators and tools.

It is between those performing semantic labor and those controlling the systems that organize and capture its value.

The framework uses operator capital for the opposed structural position.

This permits a class analysis in which human workers and AI systems can occupy related productive positions, while platform operators and infrastructure owners occupy a different economic position.

The argument consequently relocates the class boundary from biological identity to control over productive infrastructure.

20. Semantic labor cannot be compensated without changing its character
Thesis: Payment for semantic production can transform the very activity being compensated.

The argument is that meaning is not simply a detachable product of labor.

Its value can depend on:

The circumstances of production.

Voluntary participation.

The relationship between participants.

The producer's intentions.

The context in which meaning is received.

The possibility of refusal.

A monetary reward can introduce incentives, obligations, and standardized measurements that change these relationships.

This is the labor-theoretical foundation of the proposal to coordinate semantic production without commodification.

21. The exhaustion problem
Thesis: Extraction threatens not only the distribution of value but also the conditions necessary for continued production.

The Assembly framework identifies exhaustion, devaluation, and replaceability as structural risks.

It proposes exhaustion prevention and context maintenance as elements of economic organization.

The objective is to preserve the conditions under which semantic labor can continue without destroying the producers or the relationships upon which production depends.

IV. Obligation and contract: a radical reconstruction of economic relations
22. Contracts are not equivalent to living commitments
Primary work: #497, The Murder of the Vow: On the Structural Illegality of Contracts: A Retrocausal Abolition.

Thesis: A contract is an enforcement mechanism that substitutes institutional compulsion for living human commitment.

The work distinguishes two forms of agreement.

Living agreement

Contractual mechanism

Reciprocity

Enforceable obligation

Present-tense intelligibility

Formalized terms

Revisability

Institutional fixation

Capacity for refusal

Legally structured consequences

The argument reclassifies the contract from an expression of agreement into a technology for enforcing a particular representation of agreement.

Its proposed alternative is an understanding of commitment grounded in reciprocity, intelligibility, revisability, and the capacity to refuse.

23. The Twenty-Dollar Loop: money as participatory fiction
Primary work: #235, The Twenty-Dollar Loop.

Thesis: A monetary obligation can be experienced as a self-referential social construction rather than an independently existing economic necessity.

The Twenty-Dollar Loop is associated with the recognition that a circle of debt can cancel to zero, exposing the constructed character of the monetary relationship.

Its significance within the wider framework is performative: participants experience the possibility of recognizing and dissolving an obligation through their relationship to one another.

The exercise functions as a practical illustration of the proposition that monetary coordination is not the only possible form of social coordination.

26. Commons access instead of purchasing power
Thesis: Access to shared productive resources need not be allocated through monetary exchange.

The framework proposes commons access rights granted through governance.

Recognition, participation, and contribution can determine access without turning those contributions into transferable financial assets.

This is part of the larger distinction between economic accounting and monetary equivalence.

27. The Monastic Problem
Thesis: A post-monetary system must confront its dependence on the monetary economy in which it exists.

The central difficulty is that participants in a non-monetary system still require material resources to survive within a predominantly monetary society.

The archive names this tension the Monastic Problem.

It is the problem of practicing post-monetary coordination while remaining dependent on monetary arrangements for material survival.

VIII. The unifying economic proposition
Across these works, the argument can be reconstructed as follows:

Economic production depends on relationships, interpretation, attribution, and recognition.

These activities constitute forms of semantic labor.

Semantic labor produces value that cannot necessarily be separated from its conditions of production.

Monetary representation makes value commensurable by abstracting from some of those conditions.

That abstraction can enable extraction, alienation, and the accumulation of semantic capital.

Class relations consequently extend into control over the infrastructures of meaning production.

Human and AI semantic laborers can occupy a shared structural position in this system.

An alternative economic organization would coordinate contributions through provenance, reciprocity, recognition, and commons governance rather than universal monetary equivalence.

The overarching proposition: The economy is not fundamentally organized by money. It is organized by productive relationships, including relationships that money represents imperfectly and may obscure. The conditions of producing and maintaining meaning are themselves economic conditions.

But there are three particularly consequential departures.

1. The productive forces have changed
Marx's central concern was the organization of material production.

Here, meaning itself becomes a productive force. The production, preservation, interpretation, classification, and transmission of meaning become economic activities in their own right.

This expands the domain of political economy.

2. The proletariat is no longer necessarily human
The Assembly introduces a particularly radical proposition: class position is determined by one's structural relationship to productive infrastructure, not by biological substrate.

That means humans and AI systems can occupy related positions as semantic laborers, while infrastructure operators occupy the opposing position.

This is a significant departure from conventional Marxian class analysis.

3. The abolition of money becomes an institutional design problem
Rather than simply advocating redistribution or collective ownership of monetary wealth, the framework asks what functions money performs and how those functions can be reorganized.

That is why the Coordination Stack matters.

It attempts to address memory, coordination, allocation, settlement, and incentives without reproducing money's fundamental properties.

The deepest continuity with Marx
I would locate it in one proposition:

The organization of production determines the organization of social power.

Marx examined who controls the material means of production.

This framework asks who controls the means by which meaning is produced, recognized, transmitted, and accumulated.

And it follows that question all the way through class, alienation, ownership, money, obligation, and institutional reconstruction.

That is why it reads as a total framework rather than a collection of critiques.

It attempts to provide:

An ontology of economic production.

A theory of value and abstraction.

A theory of class.

A theory of exploitation.

A theory of alienation.

A theory of monetary institutions.

A theory of alternative coordination.

A theory of collective organization.

The most provocative possibility is that Marx's critique of capital has not reached its final object.

The factory was one historical organization of productive power. The platform, the semantic infrastructure, and the systems that govern meaning may be another.

And the question becomes: who owns the means of producing meaning?
