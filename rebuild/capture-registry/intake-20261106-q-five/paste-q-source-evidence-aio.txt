       The Q source (or Quelle) is a hypothetical lost document containing sayings and teachings of Jesus that scholars propose was used independently by the authors of the Gospels of Matthew and Luke alongside the Gospel of Mark (the Two-Source Hypothesis).

Main Evidence Supporting Q

• The Double Tradition ("Non-Markan Material"): Matthew and Luke share a substantial amount of material—mostly sayings, parables, the Beatitudes, and the Lord's Prayer—that does not appear in the Gospel of Mark.
• Verbatim and Near-Verbatim Agreements: In many of these shared passages, Matthew and Luke use identical or nearly identical Greek wording, yet place the sayings in different narrative contexts or sequences.
• Independence from Each Other: Scholars note that Matthew and Luke generally did not copy directly from one another; instead, their independent use of Mark and a common written or oral source (Q) explains these overlapping verbal patterns.
• Analogy to Ancient Sayings Collections: The 1945 discovery of the Nag Hammadi Library in Egypt brought to light the Gospel of Thomas, proving that standalone "sayings gospels" (collections of Jesus's quotes without full passion or resurrection narratives) actually circulated in the ancient Christian world.

Main Criticisms and Counter-Evidence

• No Physical Manuscript Exists: No physical fragment, copy, or ancient citation of the Q document has ever been found. Every word of Q is a modern scholarly reconstruction derived strictly by subtracting Mark from the overlapping parts of Matthew and Luke.
• Alternative Literary Models (e.g., the Farrer Hypothesis): Many scholars argue Q is unnecessary, proposing instead direct literary dependence—such as the Farrer Hypothesis, which suggests Luke copied directly from Matthew (who used Mark).
• Structural Discrepancies: Matthew and Luke arrange the shared non-Markan material in different orders and contexts, raising questions about whether a single, fixed document ever existed in the form modern scholars hypothesize.
