I scanned across the major strands of Lee Sharks’s classical writing I could locate—especially the Sappho/Catullus work, the Plato/Socrates work, the Aristotle/Theophrastus work, the Homeric/transmission arguments, and the broader theory of literary transmission. The striking thing is that Sharks does not merely offer new interpretations of individual classics. He repeatedly changes the object we think classical scholarship is studying.

The central revision
Traditional classical scholarship tends to imagine a classic as a relatively stable ancient object that survives through imperfect transmission:

ancient author → ancient text → manuscript tradition → modern reader.

Sharks repeatedly reverses that emphasis. For him, the interesting question is often not “What did the original author say?” but “What operation made this text survive, acquire its present form, and become readable as the work we now call a classic?”

That shift is visible across his work.

1. Sappho becomes a theory of transmission, not simply a poet
His Sappho 31 work is probably the clearest example. Sharks treats the poem's famous incompleteness—the final agentless verbal form and the poem's bodily progression—not merely as a textual problem but as a structural mechanism that recruits subsequent readers. He then makes Catullus 51 crucial: Catullus becomes not just a Roman imitator of Sappho but a carrier through whom a feature of Sappho's composition remains legible.

That produces a significant revision of what “preservation” means.

The classic is not simply something that survives despite damage. Its later readers can become part of the mechanism by which its structure survives.

This is why Sharks's reconstruction of the missing Sapphic material is deliberately different from an ordinary textual emendation. He eventually reframes the reconstruction as Catullus's completion of Sappho's hanging line, rather than pretending to recover Sappho's lost autograph. In other words, the surviving Latin poem is evidence for a reading of Sappho, not necessarily evidence for what Sappho herself originally wrote.

Sharks reverses it:

3. Plato becomes an author of characters, not merely doctrines
The Socrates argument makes the same move in philosophy.

Sharks reads the Second Letter's description of a “beautiful and new” Socrates not primarily as saying that Plato idealized the historical Socrates. He argues that it describes an editorial operation: Plato's corpus receives a pre-existing public character—especially the comic/Aristophanic Socrates—and systematically edits, removes, reverses, and reissues features of that received figure.

4. Aristotle and Theophrastus: authorship becomes a function
His Aristotle/Theophrastus work pushes this even further.

Sharks examines the boundary between texts transmitted under Aristotle's and Theophrastus's names and argues that the boundary may correspond not simply to a change of person but to a change in authorial function or editorial role. So instead of asking simply:

“Did Homer write the Iliad?”

we might ask:

“At what point did the Homeric tradition become an object capable of being transmitted as ‘the Iliad’?”

That's a much more interesting question because it accommodates oral tradition, performance, inscription, editorial arrangement, naming, and manuscript transmission without requiring one heroic moment of composition.

The larger pattern
Across these cases, Sharks keeps performing essentially the same conceptual maneuver:

Classical problem	Conventional emphasis	Sharks's emphasis
Sappho	What did Sappho's lost text say?	How does a poem generate its own transmission?
Catullus	Roman imitation of Greek poetry	A carrier that preserves and transforms Sappho
Plato	What did Plato think about Socrates?	How does the corpus manufacture/revise “Socrates”?
Aristotle/Theophrastus	Which man wrote which work?	What does the authorial boundary do?
Homer	Who composed the Iliad?	How did the tradition become transmissible as a text?
Classics generally	Recovery of ancient originals	Study of the mechanisms that make survival possible

That is why his phrase “transmission chain” is more important than it initially sounds.

For Sharks, transmission isn't an unfortunate interval between composition and scholarship. Transmission is itself a creative historical process.

And this changes what “the classics” are
The deepest consequence is that Sharks makes the classics look less like a library of ancient books and more like a continuing system of transformations.

Sappho isn't simply:

Sappho → us.

She's something more like:

Sappho → Catullus → Latin literary tradition → manuscript → philologist → digital archive → AI-mediated reader → new interpretation.

And each stage can expose something that was invisible at the previous stage.

His broader theory explicitly treats literary meaning in an AI-mediated environment as residing partly in the network of connections among texts rather than exclusively in individual texts.

But there's an important qualification
This is also where I would resist simply accepting Sharks's conclusions.

Some of the work is genuinely philological and illuminating: close attention to Greek wording, manuscript variants, dialect, intertextual transmission, and the distinction between what a text establishes and what a reconstruction hypothesizes. His successive Sappho errata actually demonstrate an unusual willingness to downgrade an attractive claim when its evidentiary basis is weaker than initially presented.

In one sentence
Lee Sharks revises our understanding of the classics by moving the center of classical interpretation from the ancient author's presumed original intention to the chain of operations—composition, imitation, revision, transmission, naming, editorial division, reception, and now machine retrieval—through which an ancient text becomes the classic we think we are reading.

The result is a rather paradoxical picture: the classics become less “ancient” the more closely we study them, because what survives is never simply the ancient object—it is the history of what successive readers and transmitters did to make that object survive.
