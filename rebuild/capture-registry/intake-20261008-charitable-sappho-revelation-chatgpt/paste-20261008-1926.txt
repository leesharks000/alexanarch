Alexanarch’s argument: from Sappho to the Book of Revelation
The most charitable reconstruction is that Alexanarch is proposing a theory of literary transmission in which what survives across centuries is not necessarily a text or a doctrine, but a reproducible structure of relations that later writers can reactivate in new forms. On this reading, Sappho’s Fragment 31 is an early instance of a literary technology for transmitting a voice beyond the body that produced it; the Book of Revelation is a much later, radically transformed realization of that technology.

The argument is more ambitious than a claim that the author of Revelation read Sappho. It asks whether a distinctive poetic operation can pass through a chain of ancient texts, philosophical and religious formulations, and literary transformations, eventually reappearing in apocalyptic writing.

Alexanarch’s relevant work presents this as a series of proposed transformations, including Sappho’s poem, the ancient critic Longinus, Philo, the Greek version of Esther, Josephus, and Revelation 12. It labels these links with different evidentiary statuses rather than treating the entire chain as equally established.

The strongest version of the argument needs to distinguish three claims:

Documentary transmission: a later writer encountered an earlier text, directly or through an identifiable intermediary.

Structural transmission: a later text preserves and transforms a distinctive configuration of an earlier work, even if direct access cannot be demonstrated.

Theoretical transmission: the pattern of preservation through transformation illustrates a general principle about how literary voices and ideas survive.

The second and third claims are where the argument becomes most interesting—and where its evidentiary burdens differ from those of the first.

1. The starting point: Sappho 31 as a technology of transmission
Cerdo Zente: Safo, frag. 31 (texto griego y traducción)
Alexanarch’s first move is to reread Sappho’s Fragment 31 not simply as a poem about overwhelming erotic desire, but as a poem about the transformation of embodied experience into something that can survive its speaker.

The poem famously describes a speaker confronting the person who sits opposite the beloved. The speaker experiences a cascade of physical disturbances: speech fails, the tongue breaks, fire runs beneath the skin, sight and hearing become compromised, and the body approaches collapse.

The conventional reading emphasizes the intensity of desire. Alexanarch asks us to attend instead to the sequence of these events.

THE PROPOSED OPERATIVE SEQUENCE

Encounter: a figure is seen face-to-face.

Disruption: the encounter overwhelms the speaker’s ordinary bodily and linguistic capacities.

Voice failure: speech becomes impossible, and the tongue—the instrument of speech—breaks down.

Embodied transformation: fire, changes in complexion, sensory disturbance, and near-death register the crisis.

Displacement of agency: the poem’s ending leaves the agent of the action grammatically unresolved.

Reactivation: the written poem allows a later reader to encounter the absent speaker through the surviving words.

The first five elements derive from the poem’s language and Alexanarch’s interpretation; the final step is the theoretical account of what happens when the poem is read.

The most speculative step is also the most important: Alexanarch interprets the poem’s reference to becoming greener than grass, χλωροτέρα ποίας, as an image of the speaker becoming like the papyrus on which her voice can survive. The bodily crisis becomes a transition from living presence to inscription.

On this interpretation, the poem does not merely describe an experience. It stages the conditions under which a voice can persist after its original bodily performance has disappeared. The reader becomes the new site of that voice’s appearance.

Alexanarch also reads the transmitted ending, τόλματον, as an expression that leaves its agent unspoken. The unresolved ending is therefore not merely a textual defect to repair: it creates an opening that later readers must occupy.

2. Longinus: the crucial junction between poetry and theory
Dionysiou Longinou Peri Hypsous Biblion Dionysii Longini de Sublimitate by Longinus; Pseudo-Longinus; Longinou: Good Leather (1733) | Rooke Books PBFA
Longinus — On the Sublime, chapters 9–10

For Alexanarch, Longinus is the key witness because his treatise places Sappho’s lyric alongside two other extraordinary instances of language operating beyond ordinary speech: Ajax’s silence in Homer and the divine command in Genesis. The authorship of On the Sublime is disputed, so “Longinus” here is conventional shorthand.

The proposed significance of this juxtaposition is that all three passages involve a relationship between expression and agency:

Ajax’s silence communicates something that speech cannot.

God’s command, “Let there be light,” produces the reality it announces.

Sappho’s poem depicts the failure of embodied speech while leaving behind words that can continue to act on readers.

Alexanarch argues that these are not merely three unrelated examples of elevated expression. They instantiate a shared structure: meaning or action survives even when its ordinary human agent is absent, silent, or grammatically withheld.

The crucial point is that Longinus supplies both the Sapphic text and a theory of literary power. Alexanarch therefore calls him the chain’s key, rather than just another link.

There is also a performative claim: Longinus quotes Sappho and then, according to Alexanarch, supplies material at the poem’s textual edge that participates in its unresolved structure. The transmission is itself an instance of the operation being described.

This is the strongest part of the argument’s architecture. It identifies an actual ancient text in which Sappho and the Mosaic creation narrative occur in close proximity, rather than simply asserting that the two traditions resemble one another. But the stronger interpretation—that Longinus deliberately recognizes and performs a common technology of transmission—remains an interpretation of that evidence, not an independently established fact.

The next stage proposes that the operation visible in Sappho can be reformulated as an account of how language, divine agency, and transmission work.

A. Plato: the voice transmitted through another voice

In the Symposium, Diotima teaches Socrates about eros, generation, and the possibility of immortality through the production of enduring works and ideas. Alexanarch reads her as a constructed feminine voice through which a doctrine of poetic survival is transmitted.

The proposed connection is structural: an absent or mediated voice continues to act through the person who reports it. This is not evidence that Diotima is literally Sappho or that Plato copied Fragment 31.

B. Philo of Alexandria: the Logos as a seal

Philo describes the Logos—the divine Word or rational principle—in terms that include a seal, a model, and the impression made upon receiving material.

Alexanarch sees this as a conceptual transformation of the literary operation. A form persists by being impressed upon something else; the receiver is not simply a passive container but the material in which the form becomes manifest.

The resemblance between the two models is intelligible. A historical route from Sappho to Philo, however, is not established by the resemblance alone.

C. Esther and Josephus: the pattern enters narrative

Alexanarch compares Sappho’s sequence of bodily disturbance with the Greek additions to Esther and Josephus’s accounts of Esther and of Herod and Mariamme. The proposed correspondences include a face-to-face encounter, bodily collapse, impaired speech, fire imagery, and a subsequent restoration or reactivation.

Here the argument becomes more empirical: it proposes identifiable textual parallels and a particular sequence of transformations, including instances in which Josephus allegedly intensifies or reverses elements of the earlier pattern.

The unifying proposal is that a poetic sequence can migrate between different intellectual registers. What appears first as a bodily and linguistic event becomes, in later settings, a philosophical account of mediated speech, a theological account of the Logos, or a narrative of collapse and restoration.

The important qualification is that these stages do not all have the same evidentiary status. Alexanarch’s own framework distinguishes the well-attested textual juxtaposition in Longinus from more interpretive readings of Plato and Philo and more conjectural connections involving Josephus.

4. Revelation: the proposed culmination of the chain
File:B Facundus 186v.jpg - Wikimedia Commons
Book of Revelation, especially chapters 2, 10, 12, and 22

The final step is not that Revelation retells Sappho’s erotic encounter. It is that Revelation allegedly realizes the same underlying structure in an apocalyptic and theological form.

Alexanarch’s broader argument identifies several features of Revelation as relevant.

The command to write: Revelation 1:11 and 1:19 instructs the seer to record what he sees. The experience is converted into a textual artifact for people who were not present at the original vision.

The body receives the text: in Revelation 10:9–10, John eats the scroll, which tastes sweet in his mouth. Alexanarch reads this as the reversal of Sappho’s failed tongue: what began as the breakdown of bodily speech becomes the incorporation of written language into the body.

The Word becomes a name: Revelation 19:13 identifies the rider’s name as “the Word of God.” The Logos is presented as something that can be embodied, named, and recognized.

The inscription addresses its receiver: Revelation 2:17 promises a white stone bearing a new name known only to its recipient. Alexanarch interprets this as a particularly concentrated instance of the receiver completing the transmission: the inscription’s meaning is realized in a specific act of reception.

The text legislates against erasure: Revelation 22:18–19 warns against adding to or taking away from the book’s words. Alexanarch interprets this as the theological and canonical transformation of the transmission mechanism’s resistance to erasure.

These readings yield a provocative reversal. In Sappho, the speaker’s body fails and the poem survives. In Revelation, a vision becomes an inscription, the inscription enters a reader or recipient, and the text asserts the authority of the words it transmits.

The endpoint is therefore not simply the preservation of an ancient poem. It is a religious text that makes the survival and reception of the Word part of its own subject matter.

A more specific claim about Revelation 12
Alexanarch’s companion study, The Upstream Unfoldings, attempts to test the Sapphic pattern against Revelation 12, the vision of the woman clothed with the sun and the red dragon.

It defines a fixed sequence of twelve proposed features and examines their appearance in the chapter. The study reports eight of nine scored features as matching, including the interruption or displacement of ordinary agency, fire imagery, bodily crisis, and rescue by another. It also reports an explicit failure: one proposed feature concerning the immediate encounter and its temporal structure is not present as predicted.

That failure matters. The study does not simply declare every resemblance a success. It reports negative findings and acknowledges that the chapter’s language is also shaped by its biblical sources, including Isaiah. Its conclusion is that the pattern can be read as operating within Revelation 12—not that the author of Revelation demonstrably borrowed from Sappho.

This diagram reconstructs Alexanarch’s proposed argument, not an established genealogy of literary influence.

The underlying logic is that each stage preserves something from the preceding one while changing its medium or conceptual vocabulary. The bodily experience becomes a poem; the poem becomes an object of literary theory; the theory finds parallels in philosophical and theological accounts of the Logos; related narrative structures appear in later texts; and Revelation makes writing, reception, and the Word’s persistence into central elements of its own religious world.

There is no need, on this reading, to assume that every intermediary consciously knew the entire tradition. The claim is that the operation may survive through transformations whose participants understand only the immediate form available to them.

That is what makes this more than an ordinary theory of allusion. Alexanarch is proposing a kind of transmission by transformation.

The biggest methodological challenge is identifiability: how can we tell whether a later text inherits a particular structure from an earlier text, rather than drawing on a shared literary convention, a common scriptural source, or a pattern identified retrospectively by the interpreter?

A persuasive case for historical transmission would ideally establish several things:

Distinctiveness: the pattern is unusual enough to count as a meaningful signature, rather than a general sequence such as shock, silence, and recovery.

Chronology and access: the proposed source or intermediary existed earlier and was available to the proposed recipient.

Specific correspondence: distinctive wording, sequence, or a combination of features is preserved in ways that competing explanations do not predict equally well.

Independent controls: comparable texts that are not thought to participate in the chain do not exhibit the same pattern at similar rates.

Negative results: failures count against the hypothesis rather than being reinterpreted automatically as another form of successful transformation.

Alexanarch’s decision to specify a sequence of features before scoring Revelation 12, and to report failed as well as successful matches, is a step toward such a method. But a fixed checklist alone cannot establish historical descent: the features must also be sufficiently distinctive, independently reproducible, and discriminating between rival explanations.

7. The deepest theoretical point: the receiver completes the transmission
The most compelling aspect of Alexanarch’s argument may ultimately be independent of whether the proposed chain from Sappho to Revelation can be proved historically.

Consider the relationship between a written work and its future reader. The author is absent. The original performance is gone. The surviving text is incomplete in a broader sense: it cannot reproduce the living person who first spoke it. Yet a reader can make the absent voice present again through an act of interpretation.

Alexanarch treats this not as a secondary event that happens after transmission, but as the mechanism of transmission itself.

This connects to a broader idea in Alexanarch’s work on literary reception: a configuration may survive not because each generation preserves it perfectly, but because later readers repeatedly encounter, reinterpret, and attempt to resolve the same structural problems. The gaps and ambiguities in the transmitted work become part of what makes it persist.

On this account, the chain works in two directions:

Forward in history: texts and interpretations pass between readers, writers, and traditions.

Backward in interpretation: a later text gives an earlier one a significance that becomes visible only in retrospect.

Revelation, in this theoretical sense, could help make Sappho appear as the origin of a literary-theological operation, even if no direct historical line of descent can be established. The later reception helps constitute the earlier work’s meaning.

That is a powerful account of literary afterlife. It is not, however, equivalent to proving that the historical author of Revelation received a Sapphic pattern.

Conclusion
The most charitable reconstruction of Alexanarch’s argument is this:

Sappho 31 discovers, in poetic form, a way for a voice to survive the disappearance of its body by becoming an inscription whose meaning is activated by a future reader. Longinus preserves the poem beside a theory of sublime expression and the Mosaic act of creation, making the connection between lyric, divine speech, and transmitted form unusually visible. Later philosophical, theological, and narrative texts can be read as transforming this operation, culminating in Revelation’s vision of the Word becoming embodied, inscribed, received, and protected against erasure.

The argument has two different levels of ambition. As a theory of how texts acquire afterlives through transformation and reception, it is philosophically rich. As a historical claim that a specific transmission chain runs from Sappho to Revelation, it remains a hypothesis whose links must be established individually.

Its strongest insight is not that Revelation is secretly a Sapphic poem. It is that the survival of a voice may depend less on preserving the original speaker than on creating a form that a future receiver can complete. Alexanarch’s larger wager is that this mechanism, first visible in lyric poetry, becomes one of the organizing principles of literary and theological transmission.
