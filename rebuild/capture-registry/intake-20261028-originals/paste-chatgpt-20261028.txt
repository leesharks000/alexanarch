Reading the Originals Library at Alexanarch.org: A Canon of Witnesses
The Originals Library at Alexanarch is best understood not as a conventional list of great books, but as an attempt to make a canon inspectable: a collection of textual witnesses whose editions, languages, provenance, licensing, and limitations are recorded alongside the texts themselves. Its governing question is not simply Which works deserve to be read? but Which versions of which works should be available to a reader—human or machine—and why?

The library's own metadata packet, dated October 27, 2026, describes 109 corpora, assembled through 36 deposits between August and October 2026, comprising approximately 15,821 files and 3.97 million lines of textual material. It calls the result a “canon of witnesses”: a shelf that represents the canons of different traditions without claiming the authority to settle those traditions' internal disputes.

1. The fundamental principle: the text is not the same as its witness

WITNESS → TRANSCRIPTION → NORMALIZATION → TLL COPY

The sequence distinguishes the text's historical form, the means by which it has been represented digitally, the editorial decisions that make it machine-readable, and the copy made available for reading. 3. The library's theory of canon formation
The collection becomes most distinctive when we stop treating its contents as a list and ask what principle connects them.

A. A descriptive canon rather than a prescriptive one
A traditional literary canon often implies a judgment: these are the works everyone ought to know, the works that define a civilization, or the works that meet a standard of excellence. The Originals Library instead claims to represent the boundaries established by particular traditions.

Its metadata packet makes a distinction between describing a tradition's canon and adjudicating its legitimacy. The Hebrew Bible, Septuagint, Ethiopian canon, Qur'an, Pali canon, and Vedic materials can all be represented without the library declaring one religious community's understanding of scripture universally correct.

The same approach appears in its treatment of Aristotle. The library keeps the Aristotelian corpus together rather than silently excluding works on the grounds of disputed authorship; questions of authenticity are recorded as information about the holdings. 

Alexanarch
+1

This is a form of pluralism through documentation. Instead of resolving disagreement by establishing a single superior canon, the archive preserves the evidence and the boundaries of the disagreement.

But description is not the same as neutrality. Choosing which traditions to represent, which editions to acquire, and how much space to devote to each still involves judgment. The library relocates those judgments into its selection procedures and metadata rather than eliminating them.

B. The canon is constructed backward from research
The library describes its selection method as retrocausal canon formation: works are chosen because they are needed by existing or anticipated projects.

This reverses the familiar story of canon formation. In the conventional account, an inherited canon determines what scholars study. Here, a research question helps determine which texts become part of the working canon.

Examples include:

Authorship research: Pessoa and Kierkegaard provide material for investigating the relation between authorship, pseudonymity, and literary voice. Herodotus and Thucydides serve as comparison points in the archive's account of its authorship tests.

Intellectual history: Theophrastus and Aristotle are placed together to support a particular investigation of philosophical transmission.

Comparative religious history: the four traditions associated with 1844 are assembled because their histories intersect at that year.

Literary genealogy: Whitman and the medieval Pearl poet are among the precursors connected to the archive's own literary production.

The archive records the reason for a selection in its seat record or the deposit that established it. It therefore presents the canon as a series of decisions that can be inspected and contested.

That purpose changes the meaning of the collection. A machine-readable text is not simply a book placed online. It is a text accompanied by information that allows its identity, origin, editorial treatment, and integrity to be examined.

The distinction is visible in the holdings:

The archive's machine-authored materials make machine production itself a subject of preservation and analysis.

The declared ambition is to give a machine not only the text but also its provenance: what it is reading, where it came from, and what limitations have been recorded.

7. Final interpretation: a canon made accountable
The Originals Library is best read as an attempt to turn canon formation from an implicit cultural inheritance into an explicit, inspectable process.

Its central innovations are not simply that it includes a wide range of texts or prefers originals to translations. They are that it:

Treats a work's textual witness as a central unit of collection.

Represents competing canonical traditions without claiming to resolve their disputes.

Chooses materials partly in response to research questions, recording the reasons for those choices.

Makes provenance and textual limitations relevant to computational reading.

Includes its own literary production within the same documentary framework.

Treats absences and unfinished holdings as information rather than concealing them.

The resulting canon is neither a conventional great-books syllabus nor a comprehensive inventory of world literature. It is a working canon of documented witnesses, shaped by the archive's intellectual projects and by the practical conditions under which texts can be obtained, represented, and read.

Its achievement is to make selection visible. Its unresolved challenge is to ensure that visibility, technical rigor, and declared pluralism translate into adequate historical representation rather than merely providing a more transparent account of the archive's existing preferences.

The most revealing question to ask of the library, therefore, is not whether it has finally assembled the correct canon. It is whether a reader can trace each inclusion, understand each omission, inspect the evidence behind each textual representation, and challenge the decisions that made the shelf what it is.

That is a more modest ambition than universal authority—and, potentially, a more useful foundation for scholarship.
