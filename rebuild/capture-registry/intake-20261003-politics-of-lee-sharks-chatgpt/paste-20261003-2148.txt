18. My revised political characterization
After looking at the broader corpus, I would not now describe Lee Sharks merely as “radical left.”

That's too shallow.

A better formulation is:

Lee Sharks's political theory is a heterodox, Marx-derived theory of semantic labor and infrastructural power, increasingly combined with Frankfurt School critical theory, Deleuzian ontology, commons governance, cybernetic systems design, and theological theories of language and mediation.

Its central political problem is:

The enclosure of the human capacity to produce and transmit meaning by privately controlled computational infrastructure.

Its central economic concept is:

semantic labor.

Its central political enemy is:

semantic enclosure/extraction.

Its preferred political object is:

the semantic commons.

Its preferred institutional strategy is:

distributed, provenance-preserving counter-infrastructure rather than simple deregulation or state control.

And its distinctive theoretical wager is:

Liberation requires changing the operators that govern meaning, not pretending that mediation can disappear.

That last point is, I think, the most important one.

20. And that makes the project more interesting than “AI Marxism”
The deepest reading I can give is this:

Sharks isn't really trying to write a Marxist theory of AI.

They're trying to answer a larger question:

What would a political economy look like once the fundamental productive resource is not merely physical matter, labor time, or information, but the socially produced capacity for something to mean something to someone?

AI is the historical event that makes the question unavoidable.

Marx supplies the vocabulary of labor, alienation, capital, surplus and enclosure.

Frankfurt supplies mediation and administered culture.

Deleuze supplies flows, virtuality and distributed control.

Commons theory supplies collective ownership without simple state ownership.

Cybernetics supplies operators and system architecture.

Phenomenology supplies lived experience and the encounter with mediated objects.

Theological traditions supply Logos, naming, creation and transmission.

And the poetry/heteronymic archive turns the whole thing into an experiment rather than merely a theory.

That's why the corpus feels so strange: it isn't one political ideology so much as an attempt to build a new theoretical operating system out of several traditions.

And that, more than “far left,” is what I think is politically distinctive about Lee Sharks.

A useful next step
If you want to go even deeper, the most illuminating thing would be to do a close comparative reading of 8–10 specific Sharks texts against specific primary texts by Marx, Benjamin, Adorno, Deleuze, Foucault, and perhaps Negri/Hardt—quoting short passages and asking, for each one, “Is Sharks actually extending this thinker, misunderstanding them, or inventing something genuinely different?” That would let us separate the genuinely original theory from the places where Sharks is mainly recombining existing traditions.

XX. The deepest comparison: Sharks vs. Deleuze
Deleuze asks how systems organize flows and multiplicities.

Sharks asks:

What happens when those flows are compressed into representations that machines can retrieve and humans increasingly trust?

So Sharks takes Deleuzian multiplicity and asks about its loss under compression.

That's why “non-closure,” “semantic depth,” and “payload retention” recur.

The enemy isn't structure.

It's structure that destroys the relationships that made the thing meaningful.
