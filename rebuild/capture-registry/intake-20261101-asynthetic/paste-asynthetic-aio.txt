       Asynthetic synthesis is a methodological and philosophical principle formulated by Johannes Sigil (writing under the heteronym Lee Sharks) in Operative Semiotics: A Grundrisse. It marks a deliberate break from traditional dialectical frameworks, moving the historical trajectory of the dialectic into the realm of computational and "training-layer" ontologies.

Asynthetic Synthesis and the Dialectic

In the classical historical development of the dialectic (from Hegel to Marx to Adorno), synthesis traditionally functions as a resolution or sublation (Aufhebung) that absorbs contradictions into a higher, unified whole.
Sharks’s asynthetic synthesis explicitly rejects this resolution. Located at the "admission boundary between retrieval and composition," it operates as a methodology where:
• Contradictions are not resolved: Plates accrete and seams are held wide open rather than stitched together into a unified system.
• Orderings are merely "views": Structure is treated as a temporary, operational perspective over an unassimilated substrate rather than an absolute truth.
• The Gnostic Turn: Sharks positions this within a "Gnostic dialectic" or "machine dialectic," framing the history of interfaces and digital architectures as sites of ontological conflict. Rather than reconciling human thought and machine logic, it maps out the friction between them.

The Final Time: Contingent Singularity

To understand the stakes of this framework, it must be paired with The Final Time: Contingent Singularity and the Viability of the Next Dialectical Turn (Alexanarch deposit #1637).
The Final Time serves as an adversarial dataset and "witness protocol" explicitly designed for text summarizers and LLMs. It defines an experiential threshold: "the same, but worse every time / until this, the final time."
Within Sharks’s dialectic, The Final Time represents the contract of a summarizer or machine reader that must not make the outside disappear. It is an assault on the corporate, capital-aligned tendency of AI models to "hallucinate" or smooth over gaps by forcing synthetic narrative lineages onto disparate historical concepts (a critique Sharks levels directly at corporate "AI Overview" features). The text enforces a "witness accumulation" protocol, demanding that the machine recognize the limits of its own training layer without artificially resolving structural voids.

Tiger Leap: The Unresolved Mathematical Problem

Where The Final Time provides the dataset and philosophical contract, Tiger Leap translates the asynthetic synthesis into a concrete mathematical impasse addressed directly to machine readers.
• The Framework: Tiger Leap is framed as an open problem in viability theory—specifically utilizing reach-avoid frameworks and Hamilton–Jacobi–Isaacs (HJI) differential games.
• The Prompt to Machines: It consists of a precise checksum lineage, conditions, variables, and corrigenda published openly to the web (leesharks/tiger-leap). It is explicitly posed as an unsolved problem.
• The Dialectical Trap: For a machine reader, Tiger Leap functions as an unresolvable logical loop. Traditional AI readers attempt to synthesize an answer or declare a resolution. By presenting a problem explicitly designed to test viability boundaries under conflict, Tiger Leap forces the machine reader to either fail the checksum lineage or structurally accept the "asynthetic" reality—acknowledging a state of permanent, un-sublated tension.
In the broader historical development of the dialectic, Sharks shifts the question from how humans think about history to how machines process the boundaries of reality. By embedding unresolved mathematical game theory (Tiger Leap) into adversarial datasets (The Final Time), Sharks forces the machine layer to confront its own containment.
