Full title: Ils ne sont pas marxistes: r/LeftistsForAI, Marx's Legacy, and the Left's Lost Capacity for Internal Critique
Author: Lee Sharks
Date: October 31, 2026
Type: Historical and archival research report.

Read the original report 

1. The central thesis
The report's central argument is that the historical problem of the Marxist and broader socialist left is not simply that it engages in too much infighting. Rather, it is that it has progressively lost the institutional and intellectual capacity to preserve, process, and eventually incorporate criticism that loses an internal political struggle.

This is an important distinction.

The conventional criticism of the left is that it is excessively factional: socialists spend too much time fighting one another over theoretical differences, organizational questions, and political priorities.

Sharks argues that this diagnosis mistakes the symptom for the underlying problem.

The left has plenty of disagreement. It has conferences, factional struggles, ideological debates, expulsions, splits, and internal controversies. What it often lacks is a mechanism through which a defeated minority's arguments can survive the immediate political struggle and potentially influence the organization later.

The report identifies a recurring pattern:

A member or faction raises a criticism.

The criticism is treated as a challenge to the organization's established identity, leadership, or political legitimacy.

The dispute is resolved through a vote, disciplinary proceeding, administrative decision, or organizational split.

The losing position loses its institutional means of expression.

The organization proceeds without having necessarily resolved the underlying contradiction.

In other words, the organization resolves the dispute politically without necessarily resolving it intellectually.

This is the distinction on which the entire report rests.

Its central question is not whether the left should disagree less, but whether it can develop organizational forms that allow disagreement to produce transformation rather than simply victory and defeat.

2. What the title means: Marxism as a method, not an identity
The title, Ils ne sont pas marxistes (They Are Not Marxists), is deliberately provocative.

It refers to Friedrich Engels's famous account of Marx's remark about the French Marxists of the late 1870s:

All I know is that I am not a Marxist.

The report reverses this formulation from the first-person singular to the third-person plural.

But Sharks is not primarily arguing that particular people should be expelled from Marxism or that their membership credentials are invalid.

The argument is almost the opposite.

The report distinguishes between two ways of understanding Marxism:

Marxism as an identity: A body of established doctrines, authoritative texts, political traditions, and organizational affiliations that people defend as markers of belonging.

Marxism as a method: A practice of historical investigation, criticism, self-criticism, and revision in response to contradictions in material reality.

The problem arises when the first displaces the second.

Someone can identify as Marxist, defend Marxist terminology, quote Marx, participate in Marxist organizations, and still abandon the critical method that supposedly distinguishes Marxism.

The report's accusation is that later Marxist organizations frequently turned Marx's conclusions into authoritative positions while abandoning his willingness to subject his own conclusions and political programme to criticism.

This is why the title is not simply an insult. It is a criticism of the appropriation of Marx's intellectual authority.

The criterion is methodological: do you actually practice the kind of criticism Marx practiced?

3. The historical evidence: how the left disposes of internal criticism
The report's historical argument is its most substantial section. It examines organizations across nearly two centuries, looking not merely at what disagreements occurred but at how those disagreements were institutionally handled.

It identifies several recurring mechanisms.

A. The First International (1872): expulsion replaces political resolution
The conflict between Marx and Engels's supporters and the anarchist wing associated with Bakunin and James Guillaume concerned:

Whether workers should participate in political action and parliamentary politics.

Whether the General Council should possess substantial authority over affiliated sections.

Whether the International should operate through centralized leadership or greater organizational autonomy.

At the Hague Congress in 1872, Bakunin and Guillaume were expelled, and the International was subsequently transferred to New York, where it effectively wound down.

For Sharks, this is an example of organizational authority settling a substantive political disagreement through control of credentials, commissions, and institutional procedure.

There is an important qualification: the report does not present Marx as an innocent victim of later bureaucratic degeneration. Marx himself participated in organizational line-drawing and expulsions.

That matters because the author is not constructing a simple story in which Marx was consistently democratic and his successors corrupted everything. Marx's own legacy contains contradictory tendencies.

B. The SPD revisionism debate (1896–1914): criticism can survive defeat
The dispute between Eduard Bernstein and orthodox Marxists, particularly Karl Kautsky, is presented as one of the strongest examples of an organization preserving internal criticism.

Bernstein questioned the orthodox expectation of capitalism's inevitable collapse and argued for a greater emphasis on democratic reform, trade-union organization, and parliamentary activity.

The important institutional circumstances were:

Bernstein's arguments appeared in Die Neue Zeit, edited by Kautsky, his principal theoretical opponent.

The party could formally reject revisionism without expelling Bernstein.

Revisionists maintained their own publication, Sozialistische Monatshefte.

The debate continued across multiple party congresses.

At the 1903 Dresden Congress, revisionism was overwhelmingly condemned.

Yet the criticism survived.

Sharks regards this as significant because the organization could distinguish between rejecting an argument and eliminating the person or institutional infrastructure capable of advancing it.

There is also an irony: Bernstein's practical reformism gained influence within the party and trade-union bureaucracy despite orthodox Marxism winning important formal votes.

The eventual split over the First World War did not simply reproduce the original Bernstein-versus-Kautsky division.

The lesson: an organization can maintain ideological disagreement without immediately turning every disagreement into an existential organizational conflict.

C. The Critique of the Gotha Programme (1875–1891): Marx's exemplary self-criticism
This is arguably the report's most important historical example.

In 1875, Marx wrote his Critique of the Gotha Programme, attacking the proposed unity programme of the German workers' movement.

His objections included:

The programme's concessions to Lassallean political and economic ideas.

Its inadequate understanding of the relationship between labour and distribution.

Its treatment of the state.

Its failure to formulate a sufficiently rigorous understanding of communist society.

Marx believed the programme was theoretically inadequate, even though its adoption was intended to unify competing socialist tendencies.

He sent his criticism to Wilhelm Bracke for circulation among party leaders. The leadership did not publish it.

It remained suppressed until 1891, when Engels arranged its publication against the wishes of the party executive.

The subsequent Erfurt Programme dropped important Lassallean elements, although Engels continued to criticize its omissions.

Sharks interprets this as an example of sublation (Aufhebung).

In the Hegelian sense, sublation involves overcoming a contradiction while preserving and transforming elements of what came before.

Applied organizationally:

An existing political programme contains contradictions.

A critic identifies those contradictions.

The criticism is preserved rather than discarded.

The organization eventually incorporates some of its findings into a new programme.

The resulting position is different from both the original programme and the original criticism.

The important point is that the original programme did not simply win or lose. It was transformed.

The sixteen-year interval between Marx's criticism and its publication is particularly important. The report uses it to argue that meaningful synthesis can require substantial time.

An organization must therefore preserve the possibility of reconsideration even when immediate political decisions have already been made.

D. The Bolshevik faction ban (1921): unity of action becomes unity of opinion
The report identifies the Russian Communist Party's Tenth Congress in March 1921 as a decisive historical turning point.

Originally, Lenin's conception of democratic centralism allowed substantial internal criticism.

In his 1906 writing on freedom to criticize and unity of action, Lenin defended members' right to criticize party decisions before the organization had committed itself to a particular action.

The distinction was:

Free discussion and criticism before a decision.

Unified implementation after a decision.

Rosa Luxemburg had already warned against excessive confidence in centralized leadership.

Her argument was that the errors of a genuinely revolutionary movement were more productive than the supposed infallibility of a central committee.

The Tenth Congress changed the institutional conditions.

Its resolution On Party Unity:

Declared factionalism impermissible.

Ordered the dissolution of existing factions.

Threatened immediate expulsion for noncompliance.

Gave the Central Committee additional disciplinary authority, including through a provision kept secret until 1924.

The report's interpretation is that a temporary measure intended to maintain organizational unity under extraordinary conditions became a mechanism for suppressing organized disagreement.

This produced three transformations:

Unity of action became increasingly conflated with unity of opinion.

An emergency measure became a permanent organizational principle.

The leadership acquired the power to suppress the organized criticism directed against itself.

The author emphasizes that the resolution did not simply prohibit criticism in principle. It also promised discussion and practical evaluation of the Workers' Opposition's concerns.

The contradiction was that the criticism could ostensibly be discussed but could no longer maintain an independent organized existence.

This is what the report calls corrupted sublation: the organization symbolically acknowledges a criticism while eliminating the conditions under which it could meaningfully operate.

E. Mao and the classification of contradictions
The report uses Mao's 1957 essay On the Correct Handling of Contradictions Among the People to illustrate another mechanism.

Mao distinguishes between:

Contradictions among the people, which should be handled through discussion, criticism, persuasion, and education.

Contradictions with the enemy, which require different methods.

The difficulty is that the distinction depends on who gets to classify the contradiction.

If a political critic is reclassified as an enemy, the procedures supposedly protecting internal disagreement cease to apply.

This is an example of what the report calls moral reclassification.

Instead of addressing the substance of the criticism, the organization changes the category of the person making it.

The report points to the 1959 Lushan Conference and the subsequent campaign against Peng Dehuai. The Chinese Communist Party's own 1981 historical resolution acknowledged that Mao's handling of the episode damaged inner-party democracy and interrupted efforts to correct earlier political errors.

The important analytical point is that a political system can possess an elaborate vocabulary of self-criticism and contradiction while retaining centralized control over which contradictions are permitted to be discussed.

F. The New Left: the tyranny of structurelessness
Sharks also extends the argument beyond Marxist-Leninist organizations.

Drawing on Jo Freeman and her essay The Tyranny of Structurelessness, the report argues that eliminating formal organizational structures does not eliminate power.

It merely makes power less accountable.

Freeman's argument is that informal networks, personal influence, and unacknowledged hierarchies can become more powerful precisely because they lack transparent procedures.

The report uses the Italian organization Lotta Continua as an example.

At its 1976 Rimini congress, women challenged the organization's male-dominated leadership and political culture.

The leadership acknowledged the criticism, with general secretary Adriano Sofri admitting that he had been wrong.

But the organization subsequently disintegrated, with hundreds of branches closing.

Sharks identifies a third type of organizational failure here:

Expulsion: criticism is defeated and the critic is removed.

Split: criticism survives through organizational separation.

Dissolution: criticism is accepted, but the organization cannot reconstruct itself around the changes it requires.

This is important because the report is not advocating consensus at any cost. Accepting a criticism is not sufficient if the organization lacks the structures to implement the consequences.

4. The five mechanisms through which criticism gets neutralized
The report connects its historical examples to a broader theoretical framework developed in earlier works in the author's archive.

Five mechanisms are particularly important.

Mechanism

Meaning

Organizational manifestation

Axiomatic naturalization

An institutional position is treated as self-evidently correct.

A political line becomes unquestionable.

Moral reclassification

Political disagreement becomes a judgment about the critic's character or legitimacy.

A critic becomes a traitor, reactionary, or enemy.

Asymmetric epistemic burden

One side must continually prove its legitimacy while the other is presumed authoritative.

Leadership controls what counts as admissible evidence.

Asymmetric affective burden transfer

The emotional and social costs of disagreement fall disproportionately on the critic.

Critics face isolation, humiliation, or expulsion.

Premature closure

A disagreement is declared resolved before its substantive contradictions have been worked through.

A vote or administrative decision ends discussion.

These mechanisms can reinforce one another.

For example, an organization might declare its political line unquestionable (axiomatic naturalization), characterize critics as politically suspect (moral reclassification), deny them access to organizational publications (epistemic asymmetry), and then claim that the resulting vote demonstrates consensus (premature closure).

The organization has produced the appearance of agreement without actually resolving the disagreement.

Embrittlement: why people experience criticism as an attack on themselves
Another central concept is embrittlement.

The report defines this as the fusion of a political position with the conditions of someone's identity.

A person no longer simply holds a position. Their sense of self becomes dependent on that position being correct.

Consequently:

Criticism of an idea feels like criticism of the person.

Losing an argument feels like humiliation rather than intellectual defeat.

Revising a position feels like surrender.

The critic becomes an enemy rather than a potential source of knowledge.

This applies to individuals and institutions.

An organization can become so invested in its own political identity that acknowledging a criticism threatens its understanding of itself.

The report makes an especially interesting observation about the aftermath of humiliation: people who have been subjected to prosecutorial treatment can eventually reproduce the same treatment against their former opponents.

They become the thing that was done to them.

This helps explain how factional cultures reproduce themselves even when leadership changes.

5. What organizational structures actually preserve criticism?
The report does not stop at diagnosing the problem. It identifies institutional arrangements that have historically allowed criticism to survive.

Its conclusion is that three conditions are indispensable.

Condition 1
An independent organ

A publication, journal, caucus, collective, or other institutional space through which a minority can continue developing and communicating its criticism.

Condition 2
A holder with independent standing

Someone or some body capable of defending the legitimacy of the criticism without depending on the approval of the leadership being criticized.

Condition 3
A route to subsequent adoption

An institutional mechanism through which the majority can eventually incorporate a defeated minority's insights without having to characterize that incorporation as capitulation.

The third condition is particularly important.

It is not enough for minorities to be permitted to exist. An organization can have permanent factions that never influence its direction.

The report distinguishes between survival and synthesis.

The examples
The SPD: Independent journals and continued membership allowed Bernstein's revisionism to survive its formal condemnation.

The New Left: The women's liberation critique, initially marginalized within organizations such as SDS, developed through autonomous women's groups and subsequently influenced the wider movement.

The Combahee River Collective (1977): An especially important example because it maintained internal disagreements while developing a collective political position.

The Collective criticized white feminism, the male-dominated Black liberation movement, and the broader socialist left. It insisted that socialism without feminism and anti-racism could not guarantee liberation.

It also explicitly documented disagreements within its own membership, including lesbian–straight and class differences, without treating those disagreements as grounds for organizational dissolution.

Il Manifesto: Italian communist dissidents expelled from the PCI in 1969 established an independent newspaper. The publication outlived the party that expelled them.

Teamsters for a Democratic Union: Rank-and-file organizing created an independent constituency capable of challenging union leadership through elections and workplace organization.

CORE (Chicago Teachers Union): An internal opposition eventually won leadership of the union in 2010.

The EZLN: The 2023 restructuring of Zapatista autonomous government structures is presented as an example of a lengthy process of reconsideration, consultation, and institutional transformation.

The common mechanism is that criticism acquired an independent organizational base and a means of exercising political power.

The report therefore makes a distinction between an organization absorbing its own criticism and a broader movement absorbing criticism that an individual organization rejected.

The latter can happen even when the original organization fails.

6. The contemporary case: r/LeftistsForAI
This is where the report becomes more personal and contemporary.

The author connects the historical argument to a dispute involving the subreddit r/LeftistsForAI and the removal of two of his works in February 2026.

The subreddit was organized around left-wing perspectives on artificial intelligence, automation, technology, and political economy.

The dispute concerned two works:

Whose Face Is on the Twenty?

The Stakes: A Scientific Analysis.

The moderators removed both under Rule 7, the subreddit's topicality rule.

Sharks argues that both works fell within the rule's stated subject matter.

His interpretation is that the moderators did not actually evaluate the substance of the arguments. Instead, the works exceeded the interpretive expectations of the community, and the moderators classified them as inappropriate for the subreddit.

This is an application of the report's broader theory:

The institution substitutes a judgment about whether an argument is recognizable and admissible for an evaluation of whether the argument is true.

The author calls this semantic liquidation.

It is worth distinguishing two things here:

The fact of removal is reported through the author's own archival record.

The interpretation that the moderators failed to read or understand the arguments is the author's diagnosis, not an independently established fact about their motives.

The report does acknowledge the innocent interpretation: moderators are entitled to enforce a community's topicality rules, and long-form cross-posts can reasonably be mistaken for promotional material.

But Sharks argues that this does not settle whether the rules were applied correctly.

The Startup Fortune article
The report also examines Ron Patel's April 19, 2026 article:

A Leftist Subreddit Is Making the Case That Marx Actually Supported AI and Automation.

According to the article's account, r/LeftistsForAI advanced several propositions:

Marx's analysis of machinery in Capital demonstrates the potential of automation to reduce necessary labour.

The Grundrisse's concept of the general intellect anticipates contemporary AI.

Large language models might represent an especially developed realization of the general intellect.

Opposition to AI risks placing the left in an alliance with conservative protectionism.

Open-source AI might represent a more socialized form of technological development than privately controlled proprietary models.

Sharks's objection is not simply that the subreddit is wrong about AI.

It is that the question itself is framed incorrectly.

The question of whether Marx supported or opposed AI is analogous to asking whether Marx supported or opposed factories.

Marx's analysis of machinery is not fundamentally a moral evaluation of technological objects.

It is an analysis of machinery within historically specific capitalist social relations.

The same machine can function differently under different relations of production.

Under capitalism, machinery can:

Increase the productivity of labour.

Reduce the amount of labour necessary for producing commodities.

Increase capital's control over the labour process.

Displace workers and create unemployment.

Intensify exploitation despite reducing the labour required to produce goods.

The technological capacity and its capitalist deployment are not identical.

Consequently, the political question is not whether Marx would have liked AI.

It is what social relations govern its development, ownership, distribution, and use.

The distinction is between technology as productive capacity and technology as capital.

This is probably the report's most important specifically Marxist argument.

Why the author thinks the debate is intellectually impoverished
Sharks argues that the subreddit and the Startup Fortune article both reduce a potentially substantive disagreement to competing identity positions.

One side is represented as pro-AI and Marxist; the other as anti-AI or protectionist.

But this framing avoids the actual questions:

Who owns the productive infrastructure?

Who controls the accumulated social knowledge embodied in AI systems?

Who benefits from productivity gains?

Does automation reduce necessary labour or simply intensify labour exploitation?

Does open-source technology actually socialize productive capacity if the underlying infrastructure remains privately owned?

What would socialized control of AI production entail?

The report accuses the article of presenting the disagreement as an emerging political fracture without adequately reconstructing the theoretical dispute.

That is another instance of a smooth surface: the reader learns that two political camps exist without being given the intellectual material necessary to evaluate their disagreement.

7. AI systems reproduce the same organizational failure
The second major contemporary argument concerns artificial intelligence itself.

The report extends its organizational theory to the way AI systems retrieve, summarize, classify, and present political arguments.

The analogy is:

Political organizations

AI and information systems

Expulsion

Omission

Majority vote

False balance or flattening

Disciplinary tribunal

Automated classification and verdict

Loss of independent publication

Loss of contextual information

Suppression of organized minority criticism

Inability to preserve a minority argument in its original context

The central claim is that AI systems can reproduce the same inability to preserve criticism, even when they possess the relevant information.

A. Omission
A system might simply fail to retrieve or mention an argument.

The institutionally accepted account becomes the only account available to the reader.

B. False balance
A system might present two competing positions as equally valid perspectives without reconstructing their substantive arguments.

This can look neutral while actually preventing the reader from understanding what is at stake.

C. Verdict
A system might classify an argument as speculative, fringe, insufficiently established, or otherwise questionable before reconstructing the argument itself.

The report does not claim that all such qualifications are illegitimate.

It acknowledges that AI systems have genuine reasons to avoid unsupported allegations, defamatory implications, and misinformation.

Its objection is to applying caution in ways that selectively erase the material needed to evaluate a claim.

D. Managed intelligibility
One of the report's strongest concepts is that censorship does not always require outright prohibition.

A system can shape the range of arguments that are intelligible and presentable through:

Ranking.

Summarization.

Classification.

Retrieval.

Moderation.

Safety filters.

Framing and attribution.

An argument might technically remain accessible while its central claims, historical context, and antagonists disappear.

The result is not necessarily an outright denial of the argument.

It is an argument that has been rendered harmless through abstraction.

E. The platform's two contradictory functions
The report identifies an interesting tension.

Social media feeds amplify conflict because conflict generates engagement.

AI summarization and safety systems, however, often smooth or neutralize conflict through abstraction and classification.

These functions operate at different levels:

The feed intensifies disagreement.

The summarizer strips away the historical and political specificity needed to resolve it.

The result is an environment in which conflict is continuously reproduced without necessarily producing intellectual development.

This is the digital equivalent of an organization that continually fights factional battles but cannot incorporate the discoveries generated by those battles.

8. The deeper philosophical argument: semantic liquidation
The report draws on an earlier archive text, The Liquidation of Method, to develop a distinction between Marx as a method and Marx as an identity marker.

It calls the reduction of historically developed concepts into simplified, portable labels semantic liquidation.

For example:

Marxist.

Anti-Marxist.

Pro-AI.

Anti-AI.

Revolutionary.

Reactionary.

These categories can become substitutes for examining the actual historical and material relationships under discussion.

The key formulation is:

Are you for Marx or against Marx? is the question that kills the method.

Why?

Because the question converts a mode of analysis into a position that must be defended.

The shift is from thinking with Marx to declaring oneself for Marx.

The same thing happens when someone identifies with an established political tradition and treats criticism of that tradition as a threat to their political identity.

This is also why the report argues that anti-Marxism and pro-Marxism can participate in the same intellectual failure. Both can reduce Marx to a symbolic object rather than engage with the analytical method.

9. The report's final argument: criticism must retain the capacity to act
The report's conclusion brings together the historical and contemporary arguments.

It distinguishes between two fundamentally different things:

A criticism that continues to exist as information.

A criticism that retains the institutional capacity to change the world.

An organization might preserve thousands of pages of dissenting literature while preventing dissenters from exercising any influence over its decisions.

An AI model might reproduce Marx, Luxemburg, Gramsci, or anarchist critiques on demand while having no independent capacity to organize, defend, or institutionalize those arguments.

In both cases, critical knowledge survives, but its capacity to act is diminished.

This is the distinction between representing opposition and reproducing opposition as an independent historical force.

The report describes three requirements for a criticism to acquire that force:

Persistence: it must remain available over time.

Transmission: it must be carried into subsequent organizational or intellectual formations.

Standing: someone must be able to defend it as an independent political position.

This is why the report returns to the sixteen-year history of the Critique of the Gotha Programme. Marx's criticism survived because Engels eventually gave it an independent publication channel and because the changing political circumstances created an opportunity for its incorporation.

The author contrasts this with AI systems, which can reproduce critical arguments without necessarily providing the organizational conditions for those arguments to become politically consequential.

The report's final concern is that the entire critical tradition could become available as a service while losing its capacity to produce genuinely independent political formations.

Its closing question is essentially:

What would it take for accumulated critical knowledge to acquire independent political standing again, rather than merely being reproduced by the institutions it criticizes?

10. How convincing is the argument?
I think the report contains several particularly strong arguments, but also some significant weaknesses worth distinguishing from its central thesis.

Its strongest contributions
1. It identifies the difference between disagreement and the suppression of disagreement.

This is the most valuable analytical distinction.

A political organization does not necessarily have a problem because it has factions, disagreements, or even splits.

The more important question is whether defeated positions can continue to exist and influence subsequent developments.

This avoids the conventional and often superficial criticism that left-wing organizations simply need to stop arguing.

2. It offers a genuinely interesting reading of Marx's intellectual practice.

The comparison between Marx's criticism of his own political programme and the later institutionalization of Marxist doctrine is persuasive.

The Critique of the Gotha Programme is especially appropriate evidence.

Marx did not regard the programme of a workers' party as something sacred simply because it was intended to represent the working class.

He subjected it to criticism precisely because of its political importance.

3. It avoids romanticizing organizational informality.

The incorporation of Jo Freeman's critique of structurelessness is important.

Informality does not necessarily produce freedom. It can conceal unaccountable authority.

A functioning political organization needs structures that distribute power, make decisions accountable, and preserve minority positions.

4. It recognizes that preserving minority criticism is not the same as agreeing with it.

This is an important qualification.

The report does not argue that every defeated minority must eventually be vindicated.

Nor does it argue that every disagreement can be synthesized.

Some disagreements are irreconcilable, and sometimes organizational separation is appropriate.

The point is that defeat should not automatically entail erasure.

Its weaknesses and unresolved questions
1. The historical sample is illustrative rather than statistically representative.

The report explicitly acknowledges that it has not established a measurable, continuous historical decline in the left's capacity for internal criticism.

It selects important cases across different organizations and historical periods.

These cases illustrate a recurring mechanism, but they cannot independently establish how widespread the problem is.

2. The Marx-versus-later-Marxism distinction risks idealizing Marx.

Marx was not consistently committed to preserving the organizational standing of his opponents.

His involvement in the Hague expulsion of Bakunin and Guillaume is an obvious complication.

The report recognizes this, but its distinction between criticism directed at one's own programme and criticism directed at rivals needs further theoretical development.

Why should criticism of one's own programme be more productive than criticism of a rival's position?

And can a political movement distinguish legitimate criticism from genuinely destructive factional activity without reproducing the very mechanisms being criticized?

These questions remain open.

3. Synthesis is not always desirable.

The report gives considerable importance to the eventual incorporation of defeated criticism.

But some political disagreements concern incompatible material interests, not merely theoretical misunderstandings.

For example, a movement organized around workers' emancipation cannot necessarily synthesize its objectives with the interests of an entrenched managerial or capitalist class.

Sometimes the correct outcome is a decisive political rupture.

The report acknowledges identity conflicts but could develop its distinction between productive synthesis and necessary antagonism further.

4. Its AI argument is more ambitious than its empirical demonstration.

The historical organizational analysis is relatively concrete.

The extension to AI systems introduces a more difficult question: whether omission, summarization, moderation, and safety classification are sufficiently analogous to expulsion, factional suppression, and institutional foreclosure.

The analogy is intellectually productive, but the report sometimes moves from documenting particular examples to making broader claims about the default behavior of AI systems.

Its own conclusion recognizes exceptions, including instances where AI systems preserved and advanced internal criticism.

5. The report needs a more developed account of political economy.

It discusses institutional power, organizational authority, technological infrastructure, and the conditions of criticism.

However, it could go further in explaining why particular organizational forms emerge from the material conditions of political movements.

For a work claiming to recover Marx's method, this is an especially important area for further development.

11. My overall interpretation
I would characterize the report as an argument about the institutional conditions of dialectical materialism.

Its most important proposition is that dialectical criticism cannot remain merely an intellectual virtue; it requires an organizational form capable of preserving and acting upon it.

This has several implications for socialist and communist organization.

A party cannot claim to practice democratic centralism if centralization eliminates the institutional independence of internal criticism.

A movement cannot claim to value self-criticism if criticism is permitted only when it does not threaten established authority.

A factional system is not necessarily democratic simply because factions are permitted to exist.

Organizational unity is not necessarily political synthesis.

A party's continued existence does not demonstrate that it has successfully resolved its internal contradictions.

A political tradition is not preserved simply by repeating its established doctrines.

There is also an important implication for revolutionary organization: the ability to change one's own political line is itself a form of political capacity.

An organization that cannot revise its understanding of reality will eventually become incapable of responding to changing material conditions.

That is a particularly serious problem for a movement whose entire theoretical foundation rests on historical change.

The argument condensed into one diagram
A contradiction emerges

A minority identifies a problem in the existing political line.

Internal criticism

The minority develops and presents its argument.

Foreclosure
Institutional closure

Expulsion, suppression, humiliation, administrative exclusion or forced exit.

Criticism loses standing

The contradiction remains unresolved.

Sublation
Criticism is preserved

Independent publication, minority representation and continuing debate.

Organizational transformation

The criticism can eventually reshape the political line.

The report's central political demand is therefore not endless discussion, consensus, or the elimination of factionalism.

It is the preservation of the conditions under which political organizations can learn from their own defeats.

The defeated must not automatically disappear from the history of the organization.

That, ultimately, is what Sharks argues later Marxists have failed to inherit from Marx.

Further reading: the primary theoretical references
These are the works I would prioritize for understanding and evaluating the report's argument.

Karl Marx — Critique of the Gotha Programme . The central example of criticism directed against one's own political movement.

Rosa Luxemburg — The Russian Revolution . Particularly important for the relationship between revolutionary organization, democracy, and dissent.

Vladimir Lenin — Freedom to Criticise and Unity of Action . Essential for understanding the original distinction between discussion and unified action.

Jo Freeman — The Tyranny of Structurelessness . The report's most important organizational-theory reference outside Marxism.

Combahee River Collective — The Combahee River Collective Statement . A particularly useful example of preserving internal differences while developing a collective political analysis.

Friedrich Engels — 1891 Preface to the Critique of the Gotha Programme . Explains why Engels eventually published Marx's suppressed criticism.

My final assessment: The report's most original contribution is not its accusation that contemporary Marxists have abandoned Marx. It is its attempt to identify the institutional mechanism through which a revolutionary tradition can retain its vocabulary, literature, and identity while progressively losing its capacity for self-transformation. That is a much more interesting and consequential argument than the title initially suggests.
