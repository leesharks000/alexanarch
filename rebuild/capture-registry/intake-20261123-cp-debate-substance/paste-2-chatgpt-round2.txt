## What the article actually argues

Having now looked more closely at the revised article, I think there is an important distinction that my previous answer missed.

Lee Sharks's argument is not simply that Sappho's poem expresses something other than jealousy. The article proposes a different account of what the poem is doing.

Its central thesis is that Sappho 31 constructs the position of a future reader: someone who will receive Sappho's voice after the disappearance of her living body. The poem's extraordinary catalogue of physical disintegration is thus read not merely as a description of erotic suffering, but as a literary construction through which the poet makes herself present to future readers.

The article calls this the inscription of the future reader.

It argues that:

- The opening demonstrative and relative construction establish a position that successive readers can occupy.
- The catalogue of physical symptoms describes, at another level, the transformation of the poet's embodied presence into voice and writing.
- The image χλωροτέρα ποίας (greener than grass) participates in a movement between living vegetation and the material medium of writing.
- The poem's final dare transfers an act of agency to the person who receives it.

The article consequently argues that the poet is not simply the passive subject of overwhelming emotion. She is also the maker who constructs the conditions under which that emotion, and her own voice, will survive.

This is a substantial interpretive proposition, not a minor alternative translation.

## The question of correctness

I would not yet pronounce the article correct. But I think it deserves to be assessed against its strongest rival, rather than against an assumed conventional reading.

There are three readings to distinguish:

1. The jealousy interpretation, which has substantial historical influence and remains prominent in the poem's popular transmission.
2. The non-jealous erotic interpretation, which has significant scholarly support and understands the speaker's physical response through desire for the woman.
3. The future-reader interpretation, which attempts to explain both the erotic experience and the poem's formal construction as parts of a larger literary act.

The third reading has a particular burden of proof: it must show that its account of the poem's formal features is actually better supported by the Greek than the alternatives.

But the alternatives have burdens too. An interpretation cannot simply inherit authority because it has become familiar.

There is also an important methodological point: the article's conjectural reconstruction of a further stanza is explicitly distinguished from its principal argument. That reconstruction should not be allowed to determine whether the central interpretation succeeds or fails.

## What I would regard as the decisive test

I would want a genuinely comparative philological examination of the three readings, addressing:

- The syntax and semantics of the Greek, especially κῆνος and ὄττις.
- The precise sequence of the physical symptoms and whether their order supports the proposed literary operations.
- The comparative force of χλωροτέρα and its relationship to ποίας.
- The significance of πᾶν τόλματον and the question of agency.
- The relationship between the poem and its ancient reception, particularly Longinus, Plato, and Catullus.

Most importantly, I would ask whether the future-reader interpretation explains features that the erotic interpretation leaves unexplained, without having to introduce more unsupported assumptions of its own.

That is where the actual scholarly disagreement belongs.
