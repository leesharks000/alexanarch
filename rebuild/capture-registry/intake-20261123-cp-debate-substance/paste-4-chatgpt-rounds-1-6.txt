The central distinction is between two approaches:

Atomistic criticism: Examining individual words, grammatical constructions, textual variants, and isolated pieces of evidence.

Holistic interpretation: Evaluating how multiple pieces of evidence work together to support a larger interpretation of a poem, author, or literary tradition.

Lee Sharks's argument is not simply that Sappho's poem expresses something other than jealousy. The article proposes a different account of what the poem is doing.

Its central thesis is that Sappho 31 constructs the position of a future reader: someone who will receive Sappho's voice after the disappearance of her living body. The poem's extraordinary catalogue of physical disintegration is thus read not merely as a description of erotic suffering, but as a literary construction through which the poet makes herself present to future readers.

The article calls this the inscription of the future reader.

It argues that:

The opening demonstrative and relative construction establish a position that successive readers can occupy.

The catalogue of physical symptoms describes, at another level, the transformation of the poet's embodied presence into voice and writing.

The poem's final dare transfers an act of agency to the person who receives it.

The article consequently argues that the poet is not simply the passive subject of overwhelming emotion. She is also the maker who constructs the conditions under which that emotion, and her own voice, will survive.

This is a substantial interpretive proposition, not a minor alternative translation.

The combination of κῆνος (that one) and ὄττις (whoever) does not necessarily identify one particular man. It can characterize an occupant of a position: whoever happens to sit opposite the woman.

This is interesting, but it is not yet proof of a future reader.

The argument's three steps
The man is identified by his position, rather than by a personal identity.

His position can therefore be occupied repeatedly.

That position resembles the position of a reader encountering a written voice whose author is physically absent.

The article also adduces the famous inscription on Nestor's cup from Pithekoussai, which addresses whoever drinks from the cup in the future.

This is a genuinely interesting parallel: archaic Greek poetry could address future recipients through an indefinite relative construction.

3. Third test: χλωροτέρα ποίας
Particularly important philological crux
This may be the most consequential individual phrase in the paper.

The conventional translation is approximately:

I am greener/paler than grass.

The article argues that the conventional interpretation is unstable.

The Greek adjective χλωρός can describe green, fresh, living vegetation. It can also describe pallor in a person, especially pallor associated with fear.

But the comparison is unusual: why should a person be paler than grass?

The paper surveys earlier translations and argues that translators repeatedly introduce words such as withered, dried, summer and autumn to make the comparison work.

It then proposes that the plant is moving toward another material state: the dried plant becoming a writing surface.

This is a much larger claim than simply translating χλωρός differently.

It proposes that the poet's body is becoming the material through which her voice survives.

However, there is an essential distinction here:

The difficulty of the conventional translation is a philological observation.

The interpretation of the plant as a writing surface is a hypothesis.

The first does not automatically prove the second.

4. Fourth test: πᾶν τόλματον
The poem's transmitted ending is:

ἀλλὰ πᾶν τόλματον

The article translates this approximately as:

Its argument is that the Greek does not explicitly say I must endure everything, as many translations suggest.

The grammatical construction is impersonal. It does not name the person who must undertake the daring.

The paper connects this unnamed agent to the person occupying the position established at the beginning of the poem.

In other words, the reader is not simply receiving the poet's experience. The reader is being challenged to take up her voice.

This is an ambitious interpretation, and the grammatical observation deserves serious attention. But identifying the unnamed agent specifically as the future reader remains an inference, not something supplied automatically by the verbal adjective.
