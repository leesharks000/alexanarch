I propose that we use one governing question:

Does Sappho 31 encode the making of its own future reader, or are we imposing that construction on a poem whose primary subject is erotic experience?

We should not decide that question by counting scholarly supporters. Nor should we decide it by the novelty or attractiveness of the proposed interpretation.

## 1. First test: who is κῆνος?

The poem opens:

> φαίνεταί μοι κῆνος ἴσος θέοισιν ἔμμεν᾽ ὤνηρ, ὄττις ἐνάντιός τοι ἰσδάνει καὶ πλάσιον ἆδυ φωνεί- σας ὐπακούει

A fairly literal translation:

> That man appears to me equal to the gods, whoever sits opposite you, close beside you, and listens to your sweet voice.

The article makes an important grammatical observation.

The combination of κῆνος (that one) and ὄττις (whoever) does not necessarily identify one particular man. It can characterize an occupant of a position: whoever happens to sit opposite the woman.

This is interesting, but it is not yet proof of a future reader.

### The argument's three steps

1. The man is identified by his position, rather than by a personal identity.
2. His position can therefore be occupied repeatedly.
3. That position resembles the position of a reader encountering a written voice whose author is physically absent.

The article also adduces the famous inscription on Nestor's cup from Pithekoussai, which addresses whoever drinks from the cup in the future.

This is a genuinely interesting parallel: archaic Greek poetry could address future recipients through an indefinite relative construction.

Alexanarch

### My assessment

I think the first proposition is worth taking seriously. The second is plausible. The third is an interpretive inference that requires further evidence.

There is an important distinction between a grammatical position being repeatable and that position being specifically designed as the position of a future reader.

The article needs to establish the latter through the rest of the poem.

## 2. Second test: why does the body disintegrate in this particular order?

This is where I think the argument becomes especially interesting.

The catalogue of symptoms proceeds through the speaker's faculties and bodily experience:

- Tongue and speech.
- Fire beneath the skin.
- Eyes and vision.
- Ears and hearing.
- Sweat and trembling.
- The whole body's approach to death.

The article argues that this is not simply a catalogue of erotic symptoms.

It is also a sequence of withdrawals from the living body, beginning with speech.

Its most striking observation is that the poem says the tongue breaks while the poem itself continues speaking.

Likewise, the poem describes the failure of sight and hearing through language that the reader can still see and hear.

The article proposes that Sappho has divided herself into two positions:

- The self represented as undergoing physical disintegration.
- The self composing the poem that represents that disintegration.

This is a powerful observation about poetic self-consciousness.

Alexanarch

+1

But there is a question we must not evade:

Does the fact that a poem represents the failure of speech establish that the poet is deliberately representing the transformation of speech into writing?

Not by itself.

The article's case becomes stronger if the sequence, considered alongside the opening and ending, demonstrates a systematic transfer between speaker and recipient.

That is the argument we should test next.

## 3. Third test: χλωροτέρα ποίας

Particularly important philological cruxThis may be the most consequential individual phrase in the paper.

The conventional translation is approximately:

> I am greener/paler than grass.

The article argues that the conventional interpretation is unstable.

The Greek adjective χλωρός can describe green, fresh, living vegetation. It can also describe pallor in a person, especially pallor associated with fear.

But the comparison is unusual: why should a person be paler than grass?

The paper surveys earlier translations and argues that translators repeatedly introduce words such as withered, dried, summer and autumn to make the comparison work.

It then proposes that the plant is moving toward another material state: the dried plant becoming a writing surface.

This is a much larger claim than simply translating χλωρός differently.

It proposes that the poet's body is becoming the material through which her voice survives.

However, there is an essential distinction here:

- The difficulty of the conventional translation is a philological observation.
- The interpretation of the plant as a writing surface is a hypothesis.

The first does not automatically prove the second.

Alexanarch

+1

## 4\. Fourth test: πᾶν τόλματον

The poem's transmitted ending is:

> ἀλλὰ πᾶν τόλματον

The article translates this approximately as:

> But all can be dared.

Its argument is that the Greek does not explicitly say I must endure everything, as many translations suggest.

The grammatical construction is impersonal. It does not name the person who must undertake the daring.

The paper connects this unnamed agent to the person occupying the position established at the beginning of the poem.

In other words, the reader is not simply receiving the poet's experience. The reader is being challenged to take up her voice.

This is an ambitious interpretation, and the grammatical observation deserves serious attention. But identifying the unnamed agent specifically as the future reader remains an inference, not something supplied automatically by the verbal adjective.

Alexanarch

## Where I would go next

I would concentrate on the relationship between these four observations rather than decide each one in isolation.

The article's proposed architecture

Opening: κῆνος … ὄττις

The position of the future receiver

Middle: the bodily catalogue

The withdrawal of the living speaker's faculties

χλωροτέρα ποίας

The proposed transformation into a transmissible medium

Ending: πᾶν τόλματον

The receiver inherits the act

If these elements really do form one coherent construction, the article has identified something extraordinary about Sappho's poetic achievement.

If they do not, we need to know precisely where the inference breaks.

My preference would be to examine the χλωροτέρα ποίας passage in considerably greater depth next, including the ancient lexical evidence, the manuscript tradition, Longinus's interpretation, and the history of translation. It is the point at which the article's account of Sappho's literary agency either gains an important material foundation or risks becoming an attractive metaphor imposed on an ambiguous adjective.

That is where I would put our philological effort. The ultimate question is what Sappho wrote and made—not what modern scholarship has become accustomed to seeing.
