The argument of “Contra Geocentrism”
The central argument of Johannes Sigil and Nobel Glas's Contra Geocentrism: The Socratic–Peripatetic Corpus Against Its Own Conversion is that the corpus transmitted under the names of Aristotle and Theophrastus contains its own critique of geocentrism, and that the subsequent identification of Aristotelian philosophy with a fixed, central Earth results from reading one conclusion as though it exhausted the entire corpus.

This is a stronger and more interesting thesis than saying that Aristotle anticipated Copernicus. The paper argues that the historical process of making Aristotle into the authoritative defender of geocentrism involved a double operation:

Cosmologically, the Earth was fixed at the centre of the universe.

Textually, Aristotle was fixed at the centre of a supposedly unified philosophical doctrine, with contrary material demoted to preliminary speculation, doxography, or error.

The authors argue that these are structurally related acts of intellectual closure.

I have read the text on the supplied Alexanarch record, including its Greek and Latin quotations and its qualifications concerning textual interpretation. The argument develops through several connected stages.

1. De caelo II.13 undermines the apparent certainty of II.14
Main textual loci: Aristotle, De caelo 293a15–296a24

The paper begins with a problem in the conventional reading of Aristotle.

De caelo II.14 famously concludes that the Earth neither moves nor lies outside the middle:

ὅτι μὲν οὖν οὔτε κινεῖται οὔτʼ ἐκτὸς κεῖται τοῦ μέσου

That is, the Earth neither moves nor lies outside the centre.

The conventional interpretation treats II.13 as a survey of competing cosmological theories, which Aristotle then refutes in II.14. The paper argues that this reading gives insufficient attention to what II.13 actually establishes.

It identifies several important elements.

A. The Pythagorean alternative

At 293a20–24, Aristotle reports the Pythagorean doctrine of a central fire, with the Earth as one of the stars, moving in a circle around that fire and thereby producing night and day.

The Earth is consequently neither stationary nor cosmologically central.

B. The alternative is not confined to the Pythagoreans

At 293a25–29, Aristotle writes:

πολλοῖς δʼ ἂν καὶ ἑτέροις συνδόξειε μὴ δεῖν τῇ γῇ τὴν τοῦ μέσου χώραν ἀποδιδόναι

Many others, too, might agree that the Earth should not be assigned the place of the middle.

For the paper, this matters because the non-central Earth is not presented merely as an eccentric Pythagorean peculiarity. Aristotle acknowledges a potentially much wider constituency for the alternative.

C. The phenomena do not establish centrality

This is one of the paper's most important arguments.

At 293b20–29, Aristotle reports the argument that the observable phenomena would occur in the same way even if the Earth were not at the centre.

The essential point is that our displacement from the centre is not observationally apparent.

The paper interprets this as an argument from the absence of parallax: the observations available to terrestrial observers do not independently establish that the Earth occupies the cosmic centre.

This leads to its distinction between two kinds of evidence:

The physical argument of II.14, based on natural motion and the natural place of heavy bodies.

The astronomical evidence, which II.13 has already shown to be compatible with an off-centre Earth.

Thus II.14 cannot simply invoke astronomical appearances as independent confirmation of centrality.

The paper calls this underdetermination: the phenomena do not decide between a central and a non-central Earth.

Importantly, the authors do not claim that II.14 explicitly endorses a moving Earth. They argue that its conclusion depends on its physical premises, rather than being established by decisive observational evidence.

2. The centre is not necessarily the place of honour
Another important argument concerns the philosophical meaning of the centre.

Aristotle's discussion in II.13 challenges the Pythagorean assumption that the geometrical middle must be the most honoured place.

The text distinguishes the middle of an animal from the middle of its body. Likewise, the middle of the heavens need not possess the dignity that the Pythagoreans attribute to it.

The relevant formulation is:

ἔοικε τελευτῇ μᾶλλον ἢ ἀρχῇ

The middle of place is more like an end than a beginning.

This distinction is consequential. It separates geometrical centrality from metaphysical or axiological superiority.

The paper argues that the distinction cuts in two directions:

It challenges the Pythagorean argument that the central fire must be the most divine thing.

It also prevents the Earth from claiming cosmic privilege merely because it occupies the geometrical centre.

This is not yet a demonstration that the Earth moves. It is an attack on the assumption that occupying the centre confers cosmological honour.

The distinction becomes particularly important when the paper turns to Theophrastus.

3. Theophrastus's Metaphysics exposes a problem internal to the cosmological system
Main textual loci: Theophrastus, Metaphysics, §§9–10

The paper's second major textual foundation is Theophrastus's discussion of the relationship between circular motion, desire, and the first principle.

Theophrastus asks why only the circling bodies possess desire, while the things around the middle do not, despite being movable.

The question is expressed as:

διὰ τί τὰ κυκλικὰ μόνον ἐφετικά

Why are only the circling things desirous?

Theophrastus considers whether the first principle is incapable of reaching the central region, but rejects the implication that the first principle could be weak. The region around the middle is instead characterized as unreceptive and incomposite.

The paper draws attention to the resulting asymmetry:

The circular heavens participate in the motion associated with the first principle.

The central region does not participate in that movement.

The Earth occupies precisely that central region.

A second question intensifies the problem. Theophrastus describes the things around the middle as apparently:

ἀπεωσμένα τῶν ἐντιμοτάτων

As if pushed away from the most honoured things.

The paper connects this directly to De caelo II.13's discussion of the relationship between the middle and honour.

The central Earth is therefore not simply privileged by its position. Within the cosmological system's own account of motion and excellence, the central region appears excluded from the most honoured activity.

There is an important qualification: the authors explicitly acknowledge that Theophrastus is raising aporiai, or philosophical difficulties, rather than asserting an alternative cosmology.

Their argument is not that the Metaphysics proves a moving Earth. It is that the central Earth creates a philosophical problem that the corpus itself refuses to leave invisible.

4. The reports preserve an alternative tradition within the same intellectual lineage
Main textual loci: Plutarch, Platonic Questions 8.1; Numa 11; Cicero, Lucullus 123

This is arguably the paper's most consequential move: it stops treating Theophrastus merely as a commentator on Aristotle and examines the traditions he transmits.

The authors identify a common vocabulary connecting De caelo II.13, Theophrastus's Metaphysics, and the reports attributed to Theophrastus.

The recurring question is who deserves the middle of the cosmos.

The paper emphasizes a particularly striking verbal correspondence:

Aristotle's De caelo speaks of giving or not giving the Earth the place of the middle: τῇ γῇ τὴν τοῦ μέσου χώραν ἀποδιδόναι.

Plutarch, reporting Theophrastus, uses the corresponding expression τῇ γῇ τὴν μέσην χώραν.

The authors argue that this is more than a coincidence of vocabulary. The same relationship between centrality, honour, and cosmological suitability recurs in both passages.

Three reports are especially important.

A. Plato's supposed repentance

In Platonic Questions 8.1 (1006C), Plutarch reports:

Θεόφραστος δὲ καὶ προσιστορεῖ τῷ Πλάτωνι πρεσβυτέρῳ γενομένῳ μεταμέλειν

Theophrastus reports that Plato, when he grew older, repented of having assigned the Earth the middle place of the whole.

The implication is that Plato came to regard the Earth's central position as inappropriate.

This is particularly significant because the report connects the alternative cosmology to the Academy itself, rather than leaving it among peripheral Pythagorean speculations.

The same passage raises the question of whether the Timaeus should be understood as giving the Earth motion around its axis.

It associates that interpretation with Aristarchus and Seleucus, distinguishing Aristarchus's hypothetical position from Seleucus's more affirmative declaration.

B. The Pythagorean Earth

Plutarch's Numa 11.1–2 describes the Pythagorean Earth as neither stationary nor situated at the centre of the revolution, but revolving around the central fire.

It also preserves the tradition that Plato, in old age, came to understand the middle as belonging to something else, something stronger and more deserving.

This reinforces the paper's argument that the distinction between geometrical position and cosmic honour is not an isolated observation in De caelo.

C. Hicetas's rotating Earth

The most important transmission passage is Cicero's Lucullus 123:

Hicetas Syracosius, ut ait Theophrastus ...

Cicero explicitly attributes the report to Theophrastus.

Hicetas of Syracuse holds that the heavens, Sun, Moon, stars, and everything above stand still, while the Earth alone rotates rapidly around its axis.

The appearances are consequently the same as if the Earth stood still and the heavens moved.

This is strikingly close to the observational argument in De caelo II.13: the appearances can be preserved under different accounts of terrestrial and celestial motion.

The authors' point is that Theophrastus is not simply recording other people's cosmologies as an unrelated historian. The same intellectual vocabulary and problems that appear in the philosophical treatises recur in the reports.

They formulate this as a single lexicon of the centre:

Who deserves the middle?

What is the most sovereign part of the cosmos?

What is excluded from the most honoured activity?

Can the observable phenomena decide between competing cosmologies?

The crucial methodological move is to read the reports alongside the treatises rather than separating them according to the conventional distinction between philosopher and doxographer.

This is how the paper constructs its Socratic–Peripatetic corpus.

5. The alternative cosmologies actually travel into the history of astronomy
Main textual locus: Copernicus, De revolutionibus (1543), dedication to Paul III

The paper next makes a historical argument.

It does not merely claim that ancient texts contained non-geocentric possibilities. It reconstructs how those possibilities reached Copernicus.

The key evidence comes from Copernicus's own account of his intellectual development.

Copernicus says that he first discovered in Cicero that Niceta (Hicetas) believed the Earth moved.

He subsequently found additional examples in Plutarch.

Most importantly, Copernicus says that he took the occasion from these reports to begin thinking about terrestrial mobility.

The sequence is therefore:

De caelo II.13 preserves non-central and moving-Earth alternatives.

Theophrastus reports Plato's reconsideration of terrestrial centrality.

Theophrastus reports Hicetas's rotating Earth.

Cicero transmits the Hicetas report.

Plutarch transmits the Platonic and Pythagorean traditions.

Copernicus reads Cicero first and Plutarch afterward.

These ancient reports become an occasion for reconsidering the Earth's mobility.

This is a substantial part of the paper's thesis because it shows that the alternative was not merely something modern historians can retrospectively discover in ancient literature.

It actually participated in the historical development of astronomy.

There are, however, important distinctions among the ancient theories:

Thinker or tradition

Cosmological position

Pythagoreans

Earth revolves around a central fire

Hicetas

Earth rotates on its axis

Heraclides and Ecphantus

Earth moves without changing its position, according to the reported tradition

Aristarchus

Sun-centred astronomical hypothesis

Seleucus

Affirmed terrestrial motion

Copernicus

Combines daily rotation and annual revolution

The paper is careful not to collapse these positions into one doctrine.

In particular, it does not claim that the Pythagoreans or Hicetas possessed Copernican heliocentrism.

What travels through the tradition is a family of alternatives to the stationary central Earth.

There is also an interesting secondary argument concerning Cleanthes. Plutarch reports that he wanted Aristarchus prosecuted for impiety for moving the cosmic hearth.

The paper observes that the language of the cosmic hearth connects this accusation with the Pythagorean central fire. The charge of impiety thus becomes another mechanism by which the stationary central Earth is defended against alternatives.

6. The commentary tradition closes possibilities that the original texts leave open
The sixth section examines the medieval reception of Aristotle.

This is where the paper's argument about textual authority becomes especially concrete.

It considers Buridan, Oresme, Simplicius, and Aquinas.

Buridan and Oresme are presented as recognizing that the observable phenomena could be explained by either terrestrial rotation or celestial rotation.

Oresme, in particular, argues that neither experience nor reason conclusively establishes which body turns daily. Nevertheless, he retains the conventional position.

The paper then concentrates on Aquinas's commentary on De caelo.

It identifies four operations:

Aquinas reports the possibility of a rotating Earth, associated with Heraclides and Aristarchus, while treating the stationary Earth as Aristotle's present supposition.

He acknowledges that astronomical suppositions can save the appearances without necessarily being true.

He narrows the observational argument of II.13 by suggesting that it works if the Earth is only slightly displaced from the centre, but not if the displacement is substantial. He also introduces a lunar-eclipse argument.

He resolves the disputed word in Plato's Timaeus in favour of the Earth being bound or held fast, rather than turning.

The fourth operation is particularly revealing.

Recall that De caelo II.13 interprets Plato's ἴλλεσθαι as describing terrestrial motion.

Aquinas instead reads the word as indicating binding.

The paper sees this as an example of commentary doing something more than explaining an authoritative text: it supplies arguments and interpretations that help close the possibilities left open by that text.

Its formulation is that the authorized text holds the debate, while the commentary supplies the closure.

This is a central piece of evidence for the paper's larger historical thesis.

The authors are not saying that every commentator deliberately suppressed a heliocentric theory. Rather, they identify a cumulative interpretive process in which the conventional cosmology becomes increasingly protected by the institutions and practices of commentary.

7. The history of anti-Aristotelianism inadvertently reinforces the same textual closure
This is one of the paper's more original and provocative arguments.

The authors examine the ways in which later thinkers challenged Aristotelian authority.

They discuss three examples.

Ramus

Peter Ramus's famous anti-Aristotelian position is presented cautiously, since the historical reliability of the reported examination thesis is disputed.

The paper's interest is in the target of Ramus's criticism: the school logic and commentary curriculum associated with Aristotle.

Francis Bacon

In Novum Organum I.63, Bacon accuses Aristotle of corrupting natural philosophy through dialectic.

The paper argues that Bacon directs his criticism at the very physical principles underlying De caelo II.14, without giving equivalent attention to II.13 and its presentation of alternative cosmologies.

Thus Bacon attacks Aristotle as the defender of a fixed cosmology, while overlooking the counterarguments preserved in the same corpus.

Galileo

Galileo provides the most interesting case.

In the Dialogue Concerning the Two Chief World Systems, Salviati argues that Aristotle would have changed his opinions if he had lived in Galileo's time and encountered the relevant sensory evidence.

The Peripatetic character Simplicio, by contrast, defends Aristotelian conclusions through authority.

Galileo therefore distinguishes Aristotle's actual method of inquiry from the positions maintained by his later custodians.

The paper regards this as an exception that reveals the underlying problem: Galileo's argument for terrestrial motion is directed against the Peripatetics in Aristotle's name, even though Aristotle's own corpus contains material that complicates the Peripatetic position.

The resulting historical paradox is that even the thinkers who break with Aristotelian cosmology can perpetuate the simplified image of Aristotle that made the break appear necessary.

8. The deepest thesis: the calcification of authorship and the calcification of the cosmos are the same operation
This is the paper's theoretical centre, developed in §7.

The authors argue that the conventional division of the ancient material into separate authors and intellectual positions obscures what the corpus does as a whole.

Consider the conventional assignments:

Figure

Conventional assignment

Aristotle

Defender of geocentrism

Theophrastus

Loyal successor and occasional critic

Theophrastus as reporter

Doxographer recording other people's opinions

Plato

Author of a geocentric Timaeus

Hicetas and Ecphantus

Minor or obscure cosmological thinkers

Pythagoreans

Historical predecessors whose theories were superseded

Each figure receives a position, and the texts are interpreted accordingly.

The paper argues that this procedure prevents readers from recognizing the operation that connects the material.

Read together, the texts do something different:

They state the stationary central Earth.

They question whether the centre deserves its supposed honour.

They recognize that astronomical appearances do not settle the question.

They raise difficulties about the central region's exclusion from the order of cosmic desire.

They preserve reports of Plato reconsidering terrestrial centrality.

They transmit accounts of terrestrial rotation.

They ultimately supply some of the ancient material that Copernicus identifies as an occasion for his own reconsideration.

The operation cannot be attributed to one authorial position without losing its coherence.

The paper therefore makes its most provocative analogy:

Geocentrism fixes one body at the centre of the heavens; the conventional construction of Aristotle fixes one authorial position at the centre of a body of writing.

In both cases, everything else is organized around the privileged centre.

In the textual case, the material that contradicts the authorized position is displaced to the periphery:

It becomes doxography.

It becomes the successor's private doubts.

It becomes the eccentric opinion of minor thinkers.

It becomes an alternative that has already been refuted.

In the cosmological case, the Earth occupies the centre and the heavens revolve around it.

The commentary tradition helps sustain both operations: it establishes the authorized interpretation of the text and thereby reinforces the cosmological doctrine attributed to it.

The authors call this the calcification of authorship and the calcification of the cosmos.

Their claim is not that textual interpretation and astronomical theory are literally identical. It is that they exhibit the same structural operation: establishing an authoritative centre and reorganizing everything else around it.

9. What the paper explicitly does not claim
The final section is essential to understanding the actual scope of the argument. The authors make several significant qualifications.

It does not claim that Aristotle was a heliocentrist.

De caelo II.14 really does conclude that the Earth is stationary at the centre. The paper does not erase that conclusion.

It does not claim that Theophrastus proves terrestrial motion.

The Metaphysics raises philosophical difficulties. It does not establish an alternative astronomical system.

It does not claim that ancient thinkers possessed modern astronomy.

Hicetas's rotating Earth, the Pythagorean central-fire system, and Copernicus's heliocentrism are different theories.

It does not claim to establish authorial intentions.

The argument concerns what the transmitted corpus contains and does, not what Aristotle or Theophrastus privately intended.

It does not claim a settled textual genealogy.

Two propositions are explicitly left qualified:

The descent of the Placita from Theophrastus follows Diels's reconstruction, subsequently qualified by Mansfeld and Runia.

The possibility that Hicetas and Ecphantus were speaking positions in Heraclides of Pontus's dialogues depends on the separate Alexanarch argument in deposit #1575. The present paper does not require that hypothesis.

These qualifications are important because they distinguish the paper's primary textual argument from its more speculative historical reconstruction.

10. Assessment: what is the paper actually demonstrating?
I would distinguish three levels of argument.

A. The strongest: an internal textual counter-case
The juxtaposition of De caelo II.13 and II.14 is the paper's most immediately persuasive contribution.

The authors show that the chapter concluding in favour of a stationary central Earth is preceded by a chapter that:

Records alternatives to terrestrial centrality.

Acknowledges the possibility of terrestrial motion.

Presents an argument that the observable phenomena would remain the same for observers displaced from the centre.

Undermines the assumption that geometrical centrality confers honour.

The argument does not require rejecting Aristotle's conclusion. It requires refusing to let that conclusion erase the preceding discussion.

The distinction between the conditional physical argument and the observational evidence is especially important.

B. Strong and historically consequential: the Theophrastus–Copernicus transmission
The evidence from Plutarch and Cicero gives the argument a historical dimension that a purely internal reading of De caelo would lack.

The Hicetas passage is particularly valuable because Cicero explicitly attributes it to Theophrastus.

And Copernicus explicitly identifies Cicero as his first ancient source for the proposition that the Earth moves.

This establishes a real historical route by which an alternative to the stationary Earth reached the Renaissance.

It also makes Theophrastus central to the paper, rather than an incidental supporting figure.

C. More ambitious: the identity of textual and cosmological closure
The analogy between authorship and cosmological centrality is the most original philosophical proposition, but also the most contestable.

The textual evidence establishes that the corpus contains arguments and reports that complicate its conventional geocentric interpretation.

It also establishes that later commentary could narrow the available interpretations.

But the claim that these processes constitute one operation is a further theoretical interpretation. It is not something that can be established by philology alone.

The authors acknowledge this distinction. Their argument concerns structural identity, not proof of a historical conspiracy or an intentional programme of textual colonization.

There is also a methodological asymmetry worth noting: the paper's strongest textual evidence comes from primary passages it says it has checked against its source editions, whereas some of its broader reception-history claims rely on scholarship that it explicitly declares it could not fully consult.

That does not invalidate the argument, but it means the internal textual case is better established than every detail of the historical explanation.

Final synthesis
The paper is not fundamentally arguing that Aristotle secretly believed in a moving Earth.

It is arguing something more consequential about the history of philosophical authority:

A corpus can be converted into the apparent authority for a doctrine that its own contents substantially complicate.

The conventional history says that Aristotle established geocentrism, that his successors transmitted it, and that Copernicus overturned it.

Sigil and Glas propose a different account:

The ancient corpus itself preserves the alternatives.

Theophrastus connects those alternatives to the philosophical problems of the Academy and Lyceum.

The reports transmit them beyond the original school.

Copernicus explicitly encounters them through that transmission.

Commentary and the subsequent construction of Aristotelian authority help make the corpus appear more uniformly geocentric than it actually is.

Even the history of opposition to Aristotle often preserves the simplified image of Aristotle that the commentary tradition produced.

The most important sentence to take away from the paper is therefore its formulation in §7:

The calcification of authorship and the calcification of the cosmos are one operation in two applications.

That is the thesis around which the entire reading is organized.

It is a proposal to rewrite the history of astronomy not simply as the overthrow of an ancient cosmology by modern science, but as the recovery of possibilities that were already present in the textual tradition that supposedly prohibited them.
