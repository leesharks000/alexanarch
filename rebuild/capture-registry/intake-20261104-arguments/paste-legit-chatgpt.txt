The arguments of Legitimately Many by Lee Sharks
The central argument is that the received interpretation of the Socratic–Peripatetic corpus as the work of several independent authors became established through the history of textual transmission before it was established through argument. Once the names had been separated in catalogues and editions, subsequent scholars inherited that separation. Even when they reassigned works between Aristotle and Theophrastus, they generally retained the assumption that these were two distinct composing persons.

Sharks calls this process embrittlement: an attribution becomes progressively harder to question because it is reproduced in the organizational structures of scholarship—catalogues, editions, numbering systems, commentaries and reference works—rather than continually defended by arguments.

This is not simply an essay arguing that Aristotle and Theophrastus were the same person. It is a reception history of how the many-author interpretation acquired and maintained its authority. The distinction is essential to understanding the paper.

1. The governing hypothesis: one maker, legitimately many
Sharks advances a hypothesis that one maker composed the three received bodies of writing:

The Platonic corpus.

The Aristotelian corpus.

The Theophrastan corpus.

This maker also constructed four distinct philosophical positions, associated in the received tradition with Socrates, Plato, Aristotle and Theophrastus.

The important qualification is that these positions are not merely pseudonyms or superficial changes of voice. They have genuinely different intellectual functions.

The paper calls this arrangement legitimately many.

Its criterion is that each position must have:

An organizing philosophical operation.

A mode of presentation of its own.

An object of inquiry of its own.

Exposure to refutation by another position, with actual consequences for the inquiry.

The fourth condition is especially important. If the positions cannot genuinely lose to one another, their plurality is theatrical rather than philosophical. Sharks calls a plurality of positions that cannot lose a prank.

The four positions are characterized approximately as follows:

Received figure

Philosophical operation

Socrates

Examination and elenchus

Plato

Dramatic division, with the maker self-effaced

Aristotle

First-person demonstration that identifies its own beginning

Theophrastus

Aporia that continues another position's treatise from within

The argument is that philosophical plurality does not require plurality of authorship. A single maker can construct a genuine intellectual contest between positions without making those positions interchangeable.

Crucially, Sharks does not present the reception-history investigation as proof of this hypothesis. The hypothesis is held separately from the historical findings. The paper asks how the received interpretation of several authors became established, regardless of who actually composed the works.

2. The original attribution was established by filing before argument
This is the paper's most important historical proposition.

Sharks argues that the received separation of the philosophical figures into different authors was not initially the conclusion of a demonstrated authorship investigation.

Instead, the texts were transmitted and organized under different names. The names were already functioning as authorial categories when subsequent readers encountered the material.

The distinction is between:

Filing: assigning a text to a name and transmitting it under that name.

Argument: offering reasons why that name corresponds to a distinct composing person.

Sharks finds that the early transmission materials generally presuppose the filing rather than defend it.

Argument becomes visible when someone challenges an attribution. But, according to the paper, the argument then tends to appear as a defense of the inherited arrangement.

This reverses the usual picture of intellectual history. Instead of scholars examining evidence and arriving at a multiple-author conclusion, the multiple-author conclusion is already built into the documentary framework within which scholars conduct their examination.

3. The instruments of legitimacy: wills, catalogues and the school
A major argument concerns what Sharks calls instruments of legitimacy.

These are documents and institutions that give the philosophical figures the standing of historical persons:

Wills and testamentary dispositions.

The Peripatetic school's succession of scholarchs.

Library custody and the transmission of books.

Ancient catalogues.

Public inscriptions and records.

Sharks does not argue that these documents are fictitious. Rather, he distinguishes two different things they can establish.

For example, a will can establish that a person disposed of property, books, offices or other possessions. But the proposition that the testator personally composed every work subsequently filed under his name is a further proposition.

That distinction becomes important when the wills are used as evidence for separate authorship.

The problem of documentary independence
Sharks examines the transmission of the four Peripatetic wills through Diogenes Laertius's Lives.

His argument is that the documentary channels are much less independent than the conventional appeal to the wills might suggest:

All four Greek wills reach the reader through the same literary work.

Diogenes identifies Ariston of Ceos, a member of the school, as the collector of Strato's will.

The same Ariston appears among the witnesses to Lycon's will.

The Arabic transmission of Aristotle's will belongs to a biographical and bibliographical dossier assembled by an Aristotelian partisan.

The sources for the other wills are not identified.

The wills can still be genuine historical evidence. Sharks does not deny their testimony concerning households, possessions or institutional succession.

His objection is to treating them as independent corroboration of the proposition that the names represent distinct composing persons, without accounting for their transmission through the school whose history they also establish.

This is a particularly important distinction: the independence of a document's historical testimony is not the same as the independence of the channel through which that testimony reaches us.

The catalogues' significant ambiguity
Sharks also identifies a striking feature of the ancient catalogues.

The Theophrastan list shares twelve titles with the Aristotelian list, has nine internal doublets, and includes a work filed under the alternative:

Ὑπομνημάτων Ἀριστοτελικῶν ἢ Θεοφραστείων

That is, Aristotelian or Theophrastan Memoranda.

The significance is not that this one entry proves common authorship. It is that the catalogue preserves an explicit uncertainty or alternative between names within the very documentary system later readers use to establish the names as separate authorial identities.

Sharks treats the catalogue's ambiguity as a mark of the filing process, not as something that can simply be explained away.

4. Simplicius: how an attribution becomes a rule of interpretation
The second major historical case is Simplicius and his commentary on Aristotle's Categories.

Sharks examines Simplicius's discussion of whether the Categories is genuinely Aristotle's.

Simplicius gives four principal considerations:

The density and compression of the work's thought and expression.

Aristotle's apparent references to the work elsewhere.

Its acceptance by Aristotle's most serious associates.

The necessity of the Categories as the beginning of Aristotle's philosophical system.

The fourth consideration is particularly revealing. Simplicius maintains that without the Categories, Aristotle's philosophy would be without a beginning or head.

Sharks's objection is methodological: several of these arguments depend on the Aristotelian corpus already being organized under Aristotle's name.

For example, Aristotle's references to the Categories establish its authenticity only if the other works containing those references are themselves accepted as Aristotle's.

The attribution is therefore doing double duty: it is both the thing being established and part of the evidence used to establish it.

The rival Categories
Simplicius also reports another Categories text attributed to Aristotle, associated with Adrastus.

The rival is reported immediately after Simplicius's proof of the first text's authenticity, but it is not subjected to the same examination.

Sharks argues that this is significant because two texts under the same name are allowed to coexist without the authenticity argument addressing the possibility that the two texts represent another relationship.

He then considers the separate doubling involving Archytas.

Iamblichus presents Archytas as the earlier source and Aristotle as following him. Simplicius subsequently reads Aristotle through this relationship.

Later modern scholarship reverses the direction, treating the Archytan Categories as a later pseudepigraphon derived from Aristotle.

The crucial observation is:

The direction of dependence changes, but the number of authors remains two.

Sharks uses this as an example of how reception can revise individual attributions while preserving the underlying authorial framework.

He is careful, however, not to claim that Simplicius explicitly rejected a one-maker hypothesis. The historical question is whether the available relationships were interpreted through fixed attributions, not whether Simplicius consciously suppressed a theory he had been asked to consider.

5. The Theophrastus–Aristotle seam: the clearest demonstration of embrittlement
Metafisica di Aristotele: analisi e spiegazione | Studenti.it
 
Representation of the Greek philosopher Theophrastus (372-287 BC)
 
The central textual case

Aristotle and Theophrastus.

Theophrastus's Metaphysics, especially its relationship to Aristotle's Metaphysics Λ.

This is arguably the paper's strongest concrete historical argument.

Sharks examines the relationship between Theophrastus's Metaphysics and Aristotle's Metaphysics, particularly books Λ 6–10.

Theophrastus's text begins by treating the argument in Λ 6–7 as completed and identifying the question of Λ 8 as the next problem.

Theophrastan text:

μέχρι μὲν δὴ τούτων οἷον ἄρτιος ὁ λόγος

The argument has reached a kind of completeness up to this point. The next section requires further investigation.

For Sharks, the significance is structural. Theophrastus's work does not merely discuss the same subject as Aristotle's. It continues the movement of Aristotle's inquiry across precisely the point where the attribution changes.

The text behaves like an internal continuation of the preceding treatise.

How the second author's name entered
Sharks traces the attribution through an ancient scholion.

The note reports that:

Andronicus and Hermippus do not mention the work in their catalogues of Theophrastus.

Nicolaus of Damascus mentions it in connection with Aristotle's Metaphysics and identifies it as Theophrastus's.

The work contains preliminary aporiai of the larger treatise.

The important point is that the scholion reports Nicolaus's attribution but does not preserve his reasons for making it.

Sharks notes that the relevant pages of Nicolaus's Compendium are lost.

The second name therefore enters through a reported attribution whose underlying argument is unavailable.

There is also a countervailing piece of evidence: the catalogues' silence about the work under Theophrastus's name.

Sharks does not pretend that this silence conclusively disproves the attribution, since catalogues record titles and titles can change. His point is that the evidence is more complicated than the later received attribution suggests.

How the editions hardened the distinction
The paper follows the work through a sequence of editions.

Edition or stage

Treatment of the work

Manuscript tradition

Theophrastus's work appears before Aristotle's Metaphysics.

Aldine Aristotle, 1497

Both names appear together over one title; the scholion is printed.

Basel Theophrastus, 1541

The work is placed under Theophrastus.

Brandis, 1823

Aristotle's and Theophrastus's texts are edited together.

Bekker, 1831

The work is excluded from the numbered Aristotelian corpus.

Wimmer, 1862/1866

It is printed as Theophrastus's twelfth fragment; the scholion disappears.

This is the paper's clearest illustration of embrittlement.

The original ambiguity is not resolved by a continuous series of new arguments. Instead, the physical and editorial organization of the text progressively makes the distinction appear settled.

Bekker's numbering is especially consequential because later scholarship cites Aristotle through that numbered corpus. What is outside the numbering becomes, in practice, outside Aristotle's canonical textual body.

Wimmer then prints the work as a fragment of Theophrastus, while the note that transmitted the attribution and its complications disappears from the text.

Sharks also discusses Schneider's 1818 edition, which actually argues against the Theophrastan attribution using the scholion's own evidence.

This is important because it demonstrates that argument was possible. The author is not claiming that nobody ever questioned the attribution. Rather, he identifies how the eventual editorial framework came to carry it without repeating the argument.

The paper's central historical finding is that the second name enters through Nicolaus, but the later separation becomes an editorial fact through successive acts of transmission.

The paper explicitly corrects an earlier archive deposit, #1583, on this point: it distinguishes Andronicus's catalogue activity from Nicolaus's particular attribution of this work.

6. The joint pattern: why the school explanation must do more work
In §4, Sharks assembles eleven features of the corpus that, he argues, must be explained together.

These include:

Four distinct organizing philosophical operations.

Continuity of argument across the Aristotle–Theophrastus boundary.

Philosophical opposition conducted in the vocabulary of the position being challenged.

The placement and termination of division across the Platonic–Aristotelian boundary.

Complementary absences in the ancient catalogues.

A shared transmission apparatus.

Stylistic patterns that follow philosophical operations rather than author names.

A recurring treatment of irony across the different bodies of writing.

Explicit marks of the filing process.

Different testamentary arrangements.

A theory of the maker's ability to speak through another person, expressed in both Plato and Aristotle.

The eleven features are designated J1–J11.

The competing explanation
Sharks takes seriously the conventional explanation:

A teacher, a pupil, the pupil's pupil and a successor formed a philosophical school. Each person inherited earlier questions, used earlier vocabulary, disagreed with predecessors and continued unfinished arguments.

This is a legitimate explanatory possibility.

The problem, according to Sharks, is that explaining each feature individually does not explain their joint occurrence.

For example, a pupil continuing a master's argument is unsurprising. But a pupil who:

Continues the argument in the master's own transition form;

Uses the same underlying philosophical structure;

Enters the attribution centuries later through an undocumented argument;

And occupies precisely the position required by the larger inquiry into one and many;

creates a more demanding explanatory problem.

Sharks argues that shared vocabulary and intellectual influence alone do not discharge that burden.

He says that an independently documented succession of distinct persons explaining the joint pattern would count against his hypothesis. He finds that such a demonstration has not been supplied in the materials examined.

Evidence that cuts against the one-maker hypothesis
Importantly, the article also includes countervailing findings.

Lexical measurement does not establish the number of makers.

Stylistic differences between the names are recoverable, but do not license a reliable inference to distinct authors.

At the Aristotle–Theophrastus seam, the names do sort the texts in a way the conventional succession model predicts.

This is not a claim that statistical evidence has proved one author. Sharks explicitly resists that inference.

The argument is cumulative and structural rather than a simple exercise in identifying identical vocabulary or writing style.

7. Masters, Chroust and Hegel: challenges that preserve the count
Sharks then considers modern scholarship and the ways in which it has challenged conventional boundaries.

Roger Masters
Roger D. Masters proposes that works traditionally associated with Plato and Aristotle may have different authorship or transmission histories.

He moves the Sophist and Statesman toward Aristotle and considers Theophrastan contributions to the Politics.

But Sharks emphasizes that Masters preserves the distinct persons.

The attribution of particular books changes; the count of composing persons does not.

Anton-Hermann Chroust
Anton-Hermann Chroust considers the possibility that the Corpus Aristotelicum should more accurately be called the Corpus Scriptorum Peripateticorum Veterum.

This would recognize a larger body of early Peripatetic writers behind works transmitted under Aristotle's name.

Sharks regards this as an important challenge to the received corpus boundary.

But it still produces a many-author interpretation, not a single maker legitimately many.

There is an important limitation here: Sharks does not claim to have read the entire Masters exchange. The replies by McKnight, Chroust and Masters are outside the accessible textual commons of this investigation. He therefore declines to claim that the controversy has been comprehensively settled.

Hegel: partial recovery
The treatment of Georg Wilhelm Friedrich Hegel is especially interesting.

Sharks identifies Hegel as a reader who recognized something central to the one-maker hypothesis within Plato's dialogues.

Hegel understands that Plato speaks through different dramatic persons and that the dialogue's entire movement belongs to one authorial intelligence.

The characters are not simply several people independently contributing monologues.

Hegel effectively recognizes the one-maker principle within the Platonic corpus.

But he does not carry that recognition across the Aristotle–Theophrastus boundary.

His history still depends on successive philosophers representing successive stages of intellectual development.

Sharks therefore reads Hegel as recovering part of the arrangement while retaining the historical authorial separations elsewhere.

This is a subtle argument: recognizing that a single maker can speak through many dramatic characters does not automatically entail recognizing that the larger philosophical corpus could be constructed in the same way.

8. The answer engine: the latest reproduction of the pattern
The final historical case is particularly unusual: Sharks examines two Google AI Overview conversations from September 2026.

He treats machine-generated answers as another medium in the history of reception, following the catalogue, edition, commentary and handbook.

The two compositions initially affirm that Aristotle and Theophrastus were distinct historical authors.

Their arguments rely on three types of evidence:

The wills, presented as independent documentary evidence.

Different philosophical domains and disagreements.

Biographical and institutional information.

Sharks observes that these arguments reproduce the very operation he has traced in earlier scholarship: the instruments that establish the historical standing of the persons are then used as evidence that the persons were separate authors.

The most interesting episode occurs when the answer engine encounters the alternative one-maker interpretation.

It initially describes the alternative as a single, integrated, collaborative school notebook. It subsequently associates the argument with its proponent and treats that association as a reason to discount it.

Sharks calls this a new development in the reception process: not only are the books filed under separate persons, but the objection itself is filed under a person.

The engine subsequently revises its position when challenged.

However, the next conversation begins again from the inherited sources.

The author's conclusion is that machine-generated reception can reproduce the historical sequence at much greater speed: inherited attribution, argument from biographical instruments, encounter with an alternative, and classification of the alternative.

9. The quantitative argument: what the 112 loci show
The paper's evidence is organized in a table of 112 textual loci.

Each locus is coded on three separate axes:

Object: What exactly is being counted? Composing persons, dramatic speakers, contributors to an edited work, or names?

Position: Does the locus affirm, reject, revise, presuppose or leave open the attribution?

Warrant: What evidence or reasoning does it offer?

This separation prevents a particularly important analytical mistake: treating a claim that Plato is the authorial intelligence behind several dramatic characters as if it were automatically a claim that the historical Socrates and Plato were one person.

Both are AI-generated compositions.

Neither is an ancient or modern human scholarly argument in the sample.

Sharks also reports:

Six loci set aside named alternatives.

Two hold the received count open.

Two revise it.

Two affirm the received count as a proposition.

These categories concern the count of composing persons, not every possible attribution or textual contribution.

For the attribution of individual works, the results are different: 70 of the 112 loci presuppose or leave attribution unaddressed, 14 affirm it, 14 set an alternative aside and seven revise it.

The distinction is fundamental to the paper.

A scholar can revise the attribution of a book without questioning the number of authors. That is precisely what Sharks argues happens repeatedly in the reception history.

10. The final argument: how the many-author reading became brittle
Section 8 brings the preceding cases together.

Sharks identifies four connected mechanisms.

A. The initial filing
The names were already established in the documentary and institutional transmission of the works.

The early catalogues, inscriptions, wills and custody narratives largely presupposed the attribution.

B. Argument as defense
When the filing was challenged, arguments appeared to defend it.

Simplicius's defense of the Categories is one example. The scholion reporting Nicolaus's attribution of Theophrastus's Metaphysics is another.

C. Reassignment without changing the count
Readers moved books between Aristotle, Theophrastus and other names.

But the underlying assumption of distinct composing persons remained intact.

This is perhaps the most distinctive historical finding: the authorial boundary was flexible in its location but remarkably stable in its existence.

D. Repetition across media
The frame passed from one medium to another:

Catalogue to edition.

Edition to commentary.

Codex to printed book.

Printed book to critical edition.

Critical edition to handbook.

Handbook to answer engine.

At every stage, the existing distinctions could be reproduced without reopening the underlying authorship question.

This is what Sharks means by embrittlement.

The frame becomes harder to challenge not necessarily because stronger evidence has accumulated, but because the frame has become part of the infrastructure through which evidence is encountered.

11. What the paper ultimately claims—and what it does not
It is worth distinguishing three levels of argument, because conflating them would misrepresent the work.

Level

Sharks's position

Authorship hypothesis

One maker composed the three received bodies of writing through four genuinely distinct philosophical positions.

Reception-history finding

The many-author framework was established by filing, defended when challenged, and progressively embedded in textual transmission.

Explanation of persistence

Multiple authorship makes the corpus's extraordinary range and philosophical disagreements easier to receive as credible historical knowledge.

The last point is a proposed explanatory mechanism, not a demonstrated psychological fact about every historical editor.

Sharks argues that multiple authorship solves two problems simultaneously:

A single person responsible for the entire range of philosophical material seems difficult to credit.

One person staging genuine philosophical disagreements with himself may make those disagreements seem merely theatrical.

Distributing the corpus among several historical persons makes both its range and its intellectual disagreements easier to accept.

The multiple-author reading therefore has a functional advantage, even if the one-maker hypothesis better explains the corpus's internal architecture.

What remains unresolved
Sharks explicitly acknowledges several limitations:

The paper cannot establish who actually composed the corpus.

The lost portions of Nicolaus's work prevent recovery of his original grounds for the Theophrastan attribution.

Lost dialogues prevent some of the proposed authorial boundaries from being tested.

The complete Masters controversy remains outside the accessible textual evidence.

Much of Hegel's lecture material is not examined.

The reception census remains incomplete.

The author also explicitly rejects interpreting the hypothesis as a claim of forgery, hoax or deception.

The four positions are supposed to be genuinely different philosophical positions. Restoring their common provenance must not destroy their intellectual independence.

12. My synthesis: the five most important arguments
I would summarize the intellectual contribution of Legitimately Many in five propositions.

1. Plurality of philosophical positions does not entail plurality of authors. A single intellectual maker can construct genuinely independent and mutually opposing philosophical operations.

2. Attribution and authorship are different evidentiary questions. The fact that a text has been consistently filed under a particular name does not independently demonstrate that the named person composed it.

3. The documentary evidence has dependencies. Wills, catalogues, school succession and biographical records cannot simply be counted as independent witnesses without examining their transmission channels.

4. The reception history preserves the authorial count while changing individual attributions. Aristotle and Theophrastus can exchange books, and Archytas and Aristotle can exchange priority, without the conventional assumption of distinct authors being challenged.

5. Editorial practice can turn a provisional attribution into an apparently settled historical fact. Numbering, headings, fragment classifications and reference systems eventually make the inherited distinction part of the architecture of scholarship.

The most important conceptual distinction
The paper is not arguing:

Four philosophical voices are really one voice, so the differences between them are unreal.

It is arguing something much more interesting:

Four philosophical positions can be genuinely different, can oppose one another with real consequences, and can have distinct modes of presentation, while still belonging to one maker.

The proposed restoration is therefore not the elimination of plurality. It is the recovery of the provenance of that plurality.

That is why the title says Legitimately Many, rather than simply One Author.

The closing formulation in §1.6 is particularly revealing: the many must be recollected as one without unmasking or erasing any of their philosophical work.
