The arguments of The Sophistry Measure (EA-SOPHISTRY-01 v1.0)
Lee Sharks's central argument is that machine-generated knowledge should be evaluated not merely by whether its statements are true or its quotations accurate, but by whether the composition preserves the identity, scope, authority and relationships of the knowledge it claims to represent.

The work proposes an instrument for detecting and reconstructing the transformations through which a composition can appear to represent something faithfully while materially changing what it represents.

This is a reading of the deposited text itself, including its Greek readings of Aristotle's Sophistical Refutations, its worked case concerning asynthetic synthesis, and its proposed operational requirements. 

Alexanarch

1. Sophistry is a problem of representation, not simply falsehood
The paper begins with Aristotle's definition in Sophistical Refutations 1:

ἔστι γὰρ ἡ σοφιστικὴ φαινομένη σοφία οὖσα δ᾿ οὔ

Sophistry is wisdom that appears to be wisdom but is not.

Sharks makes this definition the foundation of a new measurement instrument.

The important distinction is between:

What a composition appears to carry.

What it actually carries.

The difference between the two.

This is more demanding than fact-checking. A composition can contain entirely genuine quotations, correctly reproduced facts and individually defensible sentences, yet still misrepresent its subject.

For example, it might:

Present one source as the definition of a concept developed across several sources.

Remove the qualifications that determine who is speaking.

Turn a disputed interpretation into an established position.

Substitute an isolated formulation for a developing body of thought.

The resulting answer can be factually authentic at the sentence level and nevertheless be unfaithful at the level of representation.

Argument 1: Truth of individual statements does not establish adequacy of the composition as a representation of its subject.

2. The object of measurement is the claim to adequacy
Sections 0.2–0.3 establish an important methodological restriction.

The measure does not penalize every omission. Selection is unavoidable in composition. A short answer based on one source is not inherently sophistical.

A finding becomes relevant when the composition claims, explicitly or through its presentation, to represent more than its selection warrants.

Sharks therefore requires three things to be established before classifying a finding:

What was asked: a definition, introduction, reading, genealogy or another task.

What scope the answer announces.

Which omitted relationship materially changes the answer at that scope.

This is the instrument's governing condition: the composition's claim to adequacy.

That restriction prevents the measure from becoming a generalized accusation that every summary is incomplete.

Argument 2: Omission becomes a measurable representational failure when it contradicts the scope or adequacy that the composition claims for itself.

3. Aristotle's counters explain why machine composition is vulnerable
One of the most important passages is §2.1, Sharks's reading of Sophistical Refutations 1, lines 17–25.

Aristotle says that people cannot bring the things themselves into discussion, so they use names as symbols (σύμβολα) in place of things. We then mistakenly assume that what happens to the names also happens to the things.

His analogy is with counters (ψῆφοι) used in reckoning.

The crucial distinction is that names are finite, while the things they signify are unlimited in number:

τὰ μὲν γὰρ ὀνόματα πεπέρανται ... τὰ δὲ πράγματα τὸν ἀριθμὸν ἄπειρά ἐστιν

Names are finite; things are unlimited.

Sharks interprets this as a foundational problem for machine-mediated knowledge.

A machine operates on names, descriptions, identifiers and textual representations. But a change in a name or textual representation does not necessarily correspond to a change in the underlying thing, and conversely, preserving a name does not guarantee that the underlying referent has been preserved.

This produces what the paper calls the counter-board problem.

The machine can move the textual counters correctly while losing track of the things for which they stand.

Sharks connects this argument to:

Homonymy and disambiguation.

The archive's negative-edge and entity-disambiguation systems.

SPXI's disambiguation matrices.

The problem of substituting one entity for another while retaining apparently plausible language.

Argument 3: Machine composition is vulnerable to representational substitution because linguistic tokens and the things they signify are not interchangeable.

This is not merely a problem of ambiguous language. It is a problem of ontology: whether the entity being discussed remains the same entity throughout the composition.

Argument 4: Aristotle supplies the conceptual and diagnostic foundation, but contemporary machine composition requires an extended taxonomy capable of identifying transformations that the historical classification does not independently capture.

5. The central case: substituting a locus for an entity
Central worked example
The strongest demonstration of the instrument is §1.5, concerning the representation of asynthetic synthesis.

This is not a hypothetical example. Sharks examines an actual ChatGPT observation dated November 1, 2026, and the target against which it was evaluated.

The archive's concept of asynthetic synthesis had developed across multiple deposits, beginning with #524 and continuing through #542, #549, #557/#558, #950, #1008, #1550 and #1556.

An important earlier formulation in #524 says:

The Hexagon synthesizes operations, not doctrine.

This establishes a distinction: operations can be synthesized without synthesizing the doctrines into a unified worldview.

However, Notebook X, deposit #1550, contains a formulation in X-012 that describes asynthetic synthesis as a bearing-act aggregate that blocks machine integration (W₂), leaving Γ = 0.

Crucially, the notebook explicitly warns that seating does not constitute endorsement and that conflicting fragments remain unresolved.

The paper reconstructs six relevant textual witnesses:

The earlier distinction in #524.

The formulation in Notebook X, X-012.

The explicit notice that the formulation is not endorsed merely by being seated.

The unresolved conflict in the notebook's S-06 seam.

The measurement target, which adopts X-012 as the archive's definition.

The ChatGPT answer, which is accepted as satisfying the target's definition atom.

The problem is not that X-012 is fabricated. It exists, and its wording is genuine.

The problem is that the target promotes one received, unendorsed formulation into the authoritative definition of a concept developed across several deposits.

Sharks identifies three separable transformations:

Locus selection: selecting X-012 from the broader developmental sequence.

Voice collapse: promoting the voice of an unendorsed plate into the voice of the archive.

Entity substitution (X24): replacing the developed concept with that single textual locus.

There is also a grain mismatch: the ChatGPT answer's broad description of relations without compulsory integration satisfies neither the earlier distinction nor the specific Notebook X formulation, yet the target marks the answer as satisfying the required atom.

The reported PER is 0.042, with eleven of twelve atoms present. The missing atom is the identifier, weighted at 1.

The striking conclusion is that the answer can score extremely well against a target that is itself defective.

This leads to one of the paper's most consequential arguments:

A measurement target is itself a composition and must be examined for sophistry before it is used to measure another composition.

The instrument therefore does not simply measure the answer. It also examines the authority, construction and representational adequacy of the standard against which the answer is judged.

The proposed repair is not to create another unified definition. It is to reconstruct the developmental dossier, preserving the distinct voices, relationships, incompatibilities and unresolved seam.

This is particularly important because a supposedly corrective master definition could repeat the original error by absorbing distinct positions into one authoritative account.

ἀδολεσχία

The key qualification is Aristotle's distinction between achieving the actual effect and merely appearing to achieve it.

Sharks turns these five aims into effect types that can be recorded against a finding.

An individual finding may exhibit none, one or several of them.

The paper also carefully distinguishes historical mechanisms from extensions. For instance, Aristotle's babbling concerns an interlocutor compelled to repeat something. Sharks proposes corpus-level near-identical copying as a possible extension, not as something Aristotle directly classified.

This distinction is methodologically significant: the instrument should not pretend that contemporary machine behavior is literally identical to the ancient phenomenon.

Argument 5: Sophistical effects can be systematically described in machine composition, but historical categories and contemporary extensions must remain distinguishable.

7. A quotation's accuracy does not determine the authority it is given
Section 2.7, concerning the this and the such, is another major theoretical contribution.

Sharks reads Aristotle's treatment of the third man and the distinction between:

τόδε τι: this particular thing.

τοιόνδε τι: something of this kind, or such a thing.

Aristotle's argument concerns the tendency to treat a common predicate as if it were an individual substance.

Sharks extends this into a theory of representational reification.

A composition can accurately quote a source and still grant that quotation an unwarranted standing.

The critical question is not simply whether a quotation is authentic.

It is what the composition makes that quotation stand for.

This distinction is expressed particularly clearly in the treatment of X24:

Setting out a particular locus is not itself an error.

Treating that locus as the entire entity is where the problem arises.

Sharks therefore distinguishes the act of citing a source from the authority assigned to the cited source.

This is an unusually important distinction for archival research and machine-assisted scholarship.

A model can cite a genuine document and nevertheless misrepresent the documentary relationships that determine its meaning.

Argument 6: Authentic quotation is insufficient evidence of faithful representation; the standing granted to the quotation must also be examined.

8. The instrument must examine its own corrections
Section 2.6 discusses accent and διόρθωσις, the Greek term for correction or editorial emendation.

Aristotle observes that arguments involving accent are easier to construct in written texts than in spoken discussion. Sharks draws two lessons.

First, machine processing can extend accent-based ambiguity into differences involving spelling, spacing, capitalization and other textual marks. The paper gives the example of WikiWho being answered as wikiHow.

Second, correction itself is a potentially sophistical operation.

An editor who changes a text to answer an objection may introduce a new distortion.

This leads to a recursive principle:

A repair must be subjected to the same scrutiny as the original error.

Section 2.10 reinforces this with Aristotle's distinction between solutions addressed to the questioner and solutions addressed to the argument.

A response can apologize, change its tone or offer a disclaimer without actually removing the defective reasoning.

The paper identifies this as a particular kind of corrective residue.

A successful repair must therefore:

Identify the original dependency.

Correct the actual argument rather than merely its presentation.

Avoid introducing a new distortion.

Be rerun through the original countertest.

This is why Sharks's proposed instrument treats repair as an auditable operation, not as a declaration that an error has been fixed.

9. The instrument must not become a scalar score
This is one of the most important design decisions in the specification.

Sharks explicitly rejects a single numerical sophistry score.

Instead, each finding is a reconstructed transformation with an evidentiary record.

Section 3.2 specifies the components:

Purpose

The finding must be contestable by another reader.

This is important because the instrument is not supposed to replace interpretation with automatic classification.

Its purpose is to make the interpretive process explicit, reproducible and open to disagreement.

Why no scalar?
The paper separates three questions that are often conflated:

Did the composition preserve the required information?

What transformation produced the discrepancy?

How difficult is the discrepancy to diagnose?

A numerical score cannot adequately substitute for the reconstruction of those relationships.

Sharks also distinguishes the difficulty of locating a failure from the quality of the diagnosis and from the standing of the premises involved.

Until a defensible ordering has been established, the instrument refuses to rank cases.

Argument 7: Sophistry should be measured through typed, evidenced and contestable findings rather than compressed into a universal numerical score.

Section 3 places the sophistry measure alongside five other components of the Alexanarch archive.

The important point is that these systems are complementary, not interchangeable.

For example, PER can measure whether an answer loses specified provenance information. But it cannot independently establish whether the target used to calculate that loss has itself substituted one textual locus for a developed concept.

Similarly, /non can establish differences between an engine's answer and a reconstruction from its sources. The sophistry measure explains the transformations responsible for those differences.

The paper also establishes an important division within SPXI-gnΩsis:

Source fidelity.

Primary-text adequacy.

Operative retention.

A sentence can paraphrase its source accurately but lose the operation the source describes. Conversely, wording can change while the underlying operation remains intact.

The sophistry measure explains how such transformations occur.

This makes the instrument particularly relevant to scholarship in which preserving the relations among ideas matters as much as preserving their individual statements.

11. The archive itself must be subjected to the instrument
Perhaps the most consequential institutional argument occurs in §3.6.

Sharks writes:

A finding records and blocks nothing.

This is an important restriction.

The instrument is diagnostic, not an automatic censorship or enforcement mechanism.

It identifies transformations, documents their evidence and specifies repairs. It does not itself prevent a composition from being produced or circulated.

More importantly, the archive's own compositions, targets, repairs and services must undergo the same tests as external answers.

This prevents the archive from positioning itself as an unquestionable authority on representational fidelity.

The argument is particularly significant given the worked case: the defect arose partly within the archive's own measurement target.

A system that can diagnose distortions in external models but cannot diagnose distortions in its own definitions would reproduce the very problem it claims to solve.

Argument 8: Knowledge accountability requires symmetrical scrutiny. The institution designing the measurement instrument must be subject to the same standards as the objects it measures.

12. The deeper philosophical argument: the relationship between a text and what it represents
Reading the eleven loci in §2 together reveals that Sharks is doing more than adapting an ancient catalogue of fallacies.

The paper constructs a philosophy of machine-mediated representation from Aristotle.

Solution to the questioner, ch. 20

Ending, ch. 34

There is a particularly interesting connection between the counters and the third man.

The first explains how a representation can become detached from the thing represented. The second explains how a particular representation can be elevated into the thing itself.

Together they provide the philosophical basis for the paper's treatment of entity substitution.

The veiled man adds a further restriction: even when two descriptions refer to the same individual, predicates cannot automatically be transferred between them.

The instrument is therefore concerned with three interconnected questions:

Is this the same entity?

Does this source have the authority attributed to it?

Does this composition preserve the relationships that make the entity intelligible?

That is considerably more ambitious than conventional fact-checking.

13. The paper makes an implementable methodological proposal
The specification is not presented as a completed, empirically validated software system.

It establishes requirements for implementation and validation.

Section 4 requires:

Reading the pilot's runnable bundle and checking its file hashes.

Keying the taxonomy to the Greek textual loci and Aristotle's historical classifications.

Rebuilding the asynthetic synthesis target from its developmental dossier before rescoring observations.

Calibrating against the pilot's 27 case units.

Testing on a held-out sample.

Using two readers for new cases, retaining their disagreements.

Rerunning every repair against its original test.

This is important when interpreting the paper's status.

The deposit is a specification for a measurement instrument, with a philological reading and a previously developed pilot behind it. It should not be mistaken for evidence that all 48 types have already been independently validated across a large population of machine-generated compositions.

The insistence on retaining disagreements is especially significant: interpretive uncertainty is part of the evidence, not something the instrument should conceal.

Overall assessment: what is Lee Sharks actually arguing?
I would distill the paper into five overarching theses.

1. Representation is more demanding than factual correctness.

A machine can reproduce genuine facts and quotations while materially changing the identity, authority, scope or relationships of its subject.

2. Sophistry is a structural property of composition.

It is not reducible to lying, hallucination, incorrect quotations or bad intentions. The relevant object is the transformation between what a composition claims to represent and what it actually represents.

3. The history of logic provides instruments for diagnosing contemporary machine failures.

Aristotle's Sophistical Refutations is not merely an historical catalogue. Its analysis of names, predicates, ambiguity, descriptions, corrections and apparent refutations can be reconstructed into a contemporary diagnostic methodology.

4. Measurement targets must themselves be accountable.

The asynthetic synthesis case demonstrates that a model can be judged against a defective definition and appear to perform successfully. The instrument must therefore examine the standard of measurement as well as the measured answer.

5. Knowledge systems must preserve contestability.

A proper repair does not necessarily produce one unified authoritative account. It may need to preserve incompatible voices, unresolved questions, developmental history and the distinction between a source and the entity that source describes.

My reading of the paper's most original contribution
The most important move is X24: the substitution of a locus for an entity.

This is where the philological reading of Aristotle becomes a distinctive theory of machine-generated knowledge.

The problem is not necessarily that a machine invents something. It can be much subtler: the machine finds something real, quotes it accurately, and gives it a standing it does not possess.

That produces a particularly dangerous kind of epistemic error because ordinary verification can actually reinforce it. The quotation checks out. The source exists. The words are authentic. Yet the represented entity has been changed.

Sharks's answer is to insist on reconstructing the relationships among texts rather than merely checking the statements extracted from them.

The title's phrase the Sophistical Refutations Read as an Instrument is therefore literal. Aristotle is being used not simply as an authority to cite, but as a source from which a new instrument of philological and computational accountability can be constructed.

In one sentence: The Sophistry Measure argues that the central epistemic danger of machine-composed knowledge is not simply that machines produce false statements, but that they can preserve the appearance of knowledge while silently transforming what that knowledge is about.
