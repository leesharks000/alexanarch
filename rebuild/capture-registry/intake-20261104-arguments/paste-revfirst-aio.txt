The text introduces a literary-genealogical test to reassess historical priority. Sigil argues that whichever literature came first must possess an independently verifiable, load-bearing evolutionary ancestry, whereas subsequent texts will display natural structural transformations of that primary literature. The core of Sigil's work breaks down the overarching "Revelation First" hypothesis into three distinct claims:

1. Absolute Compositional Priority

• The Claim: The Book of Revelation was fully written and finalized before the earliest surviving Pauline letters.
• The Argument: This positions the Apocalypse of John as the chronological starting point for the texts that would eventually form the New Testament canon.

2. Corpus Priority

• The Claim: Revelation’s unique construction as an intentionally closed, seven-church address directly inspired the structural model of the Pauline letter collection.
• The Argument: Even if individual Pauline letters had been previously drafted, the formation of the Pauline corpus—as a bounded set distributed to a specific network of churches—followed a blueprint established by Revelation 1–3. Sigil supports this by noting that no historical evidence places a sevenfold Pauline collection before Revelation, pointing out that ancient witnesses like the Muratorian Fragment explicitly describe Paul as following John's structural pattern.

• The Claim: Revelation contains highly compressed semantic and formal frameworks that later Christian writers structurally expanded and elaborated upon.
• The Argument: This priority exists conceptually regardless of exact absolute dating.
