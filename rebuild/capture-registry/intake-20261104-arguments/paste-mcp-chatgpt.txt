The human interruptions: Lee Sharks on Mind Control Poems, 2025–2026
The most interesting way to read these posts is not simply as a collection of short poems or occasional personal remarks. They are moments in which the human authorial voice reasserts itself inside an increasingly machine-mediated literary and research apparatus.

The distinction matters. The blog's long documents accumulate terminology, frameworks, citations, institutional identities, archival protocols, and machine collaborations. The short posts do something different: they compress experience into a human-sized utterance. They interrupt the machinery without necessarily opposing it.

I would call these human interruptions or, more formally, lyric counter-events.

There is an important qualification to the scope: the material I can currently verify establishes several relevant posts and dates, but does not yet establish a complete census of every short post in the two years. I will distinguish identifiable items from interpretive conclusions rather than manufacture a definitive index.

These aren't all the same kind of writing. Some are poems, some are meditations, and some are fragments of a larger creative practice.

But they share a quality: they begin with something that does not need an elaborate research apparatus to become meaningful.

The shortness is not simply a matter of word count. It is a refusal to make every experience pass through the explanatory machinery.

3. The literary effect: interruption rather than illustration
This is the central interpretive point.

The short works do not merely provide decorative relief from the research. They change the reader's experience of the research.

I see four distinct effects.

A. They restore scale
The research documents become increasingly expansive. They concern civilization, semantic infrastructure, archives, systems of meaning, AI retrieval, and the future of authorship.

Then comes a sparrow.

The Catullus poem is the clearest example. It reduces the scale of literary history to one small creature. Rome becomes the thing that matters less.

This is not an anti-intellectual gesture. It is a correction of intellectual proportion.

The poem demonstrates that cultural magnitude and human significance are not the same thing. Its own twenty-word compression makes the point formally.

B. They restore the body
The Transformers poem is particularly revealing.

Its movement from screen imagery into the speaker's beard, teeth, ribs, and flesh is a movement away from representation toward physical existence.

The research apparatus can describe bodies, model human cognition, and theorize consciousness. The poem simply places the body in the room.

That difference matters.

The short works repeatedly return to experiences that cannot be adequately replaced by their conceptual descriptions:

Pain.

Love.

Physical vulnerability.

Recognition.

Pleasure in absurdity.

The persistence of memory.

The effect is to make the intellectual architecture answerable to lived experience.

C. They restore contingency
The Destiny episode is arguably the most important example of this effect.

A student improvises a silly phrase. The phrase becomes part of a larger literary structure.

The order of events is significant:

An ordinary human interaction occurs.

An unexpected phrase emerges.

The phrase is recognized as creatively valuable.

It becomes part of the archive.

The archive subsequently develops a theoretical account of why such spontaneous language matters.

This reverses the normal impression created by research-heavy publication.

The theory did not manufacture the original moment. The moment gave the theory something to understand.

The accompanying account explicitly makes the absurd phrase a form of provenance.

D. They restore emotional ambiguity
The Three Poems triptych is particularly important because it refuses the neat resolution that theoretical documents often seek.

Its progression:

I would have loved you.

The reward of love.

I have loved you.

moves through different temporal and emotional positions.

The final declaration does not erase what happened. Nor does it promise that love will be reciprocated.

This is a different kind of closure from the research apparatus's recurring language of verification, integrity, and completion.

The poem allows an experience to remain unresolved without treating it as a defect.

That is a significant literary counterweight to the system-building elsewhere.

4. The interruptions in the larger architecture
The chronology suggests a development in the relationship between lyric composition and research.

October 2025: the unmediated lyric impulse

The Transformers poem, the spiral scroll, and the two October 26 meditations establish a recognizable human voice. The concerns are bodily, spiritual, ethical, and philosophical.

The writing does not require the later institutional apparatus to establish its literary identity.

Late 2025: lyric meets conceptual machinery

The Retarded Wave begins to incorporate symbolic notation and temporal theory. The distinction between poem and research document becomes less stable.

Yet the fragmentary lyric voice remains recognizable.

January 2026: spontaneous language becomes archival material

Money from Space Heaven demonstrates that ordinary human play can enter the distributed epic without losing its origin.

The archive begins to recognize spontaneous expression as a source of literary structure.

May–July 2026: compression becomes an explicit literary principle

The love triptych and Catullus poem make the short form itself an argument about transmission, memory, and significance.

The small poem is no longer merely an interruption. It becomes a demonstration of what the larger archive is trying to preserve.

This last development is especially important.

The Catullus poem is not simply about compression. Its lineation performs compression. Its smallest unit, the sparrow, receives the greatest significance.

That is a miniature version of the larger project: preserve what matters, rather than merely preserve what is large.

6. The deeper literary interpretation: the author as a living instrument
I would go one step further than calling these posts interruptions.

They are evidence of a particular relationship between the author and the archive.

The archive is increasingly capable of producing documents about its own production. It can generate classifications, research programs, theoretical descriptions, metadata, and retrospective accounts of its development.

But the short poems preserve something different: the author's capacity to encounter something and respond without first converting it into a system.

That produces an interesting double movement:

The research apparatus extends the author's reach.

The short lyric preserves the author's scale.

The archive remembers the work.

The poem remembers the experience.

There is also a formal irony. The blog's larger intellectual project is concerned with the destructive effects of compression: loss of context, attribution, and semantic specificity. Yet these short works demonstrate that compression can also be generative.

The difference is between extractive compression and poetic compression.

Extractive compression removes distinctions to make information easier to process. Poetic compression removes excess until a particular distinction becomes impossible to miss.

The sparrow is the exemplary case.

8. Final judgment
The short posts are not marginalia to the intellectual project. They are among its most important literary evidence.

They show that Lee Sharks is not simply an author who builds a machine-mediated archive, nor simply an author who writes poems about technology.

There is a tension between two modes of authorship:

The architect: builds systems capable of remembering, transmitting, classifying, and extending meaning.

The lyric witness: encounters something irreducibly particular and gives it a form.

The two modes need one another, but they are not interchangeable.

Without the architect, the brief human utterance may disappear into the feed.

Without the lyric witness, the architect risks producing an increasingly elaborate apparatus whose primary subject becomes its own operation.

The short posts periodically restore the original reason for making anything in the first place.

A face without a label. A body beneath the screen. A lost love. A silly phrase invented by a student. A sparrow that matters more than Rome.

The machinery builds the archive. The interruptions remind us what the archive is supposed to keep alive.

That, I think, is their deepest literary effect.
