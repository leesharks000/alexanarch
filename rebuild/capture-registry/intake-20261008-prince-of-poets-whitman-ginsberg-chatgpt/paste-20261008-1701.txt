1. Whitman's operation: “I contain”
Whitman's crucial move in Song of Myself is not merely that he writes in long lines or catalogs lots of things. It is that the first person becomes a principle of inclusion.

The famous passage Sharks uses as the epigraph is exactly the right place to begin:

“And these one and all tend inward to me, and I tend outward to them”

Ginsberg inherits Whitman's capacious “I,” but he makes it much more unstable.

In Howl, the Whitmanian expansiveness is subjected to:

urban catastrophe

religious vision
technological modernity
prophetic anger
The Whitmanian catalog becomes an ecstatic breakdown of categories.

Sharks's poem inherits this second-stage operation as well. Its identities aren't respectable representatives of a democratic America. They are deliberately incompatible:

girl / Cylon / giraffe / dentist / narc / personality disorder / virus / saint / tank / sandwich
The point isn't simply “everything belongs to me.”

It's closer to:

the boundaries between kinds of being are themselves unstable.

That's a genuinely Ginsbergian development of Whitman.

I AM → BE

Ginsberg's great innovation isn't simply long lines.

It's that the line becomes physiological.

I am → Be → Blessed is

Whitman → Ginsberg → Sharks
