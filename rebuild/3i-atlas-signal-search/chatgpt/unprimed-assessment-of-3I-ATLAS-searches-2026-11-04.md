# Critical assessment of 3I/ATLAS technosignature searches

The central methodological weakness in the published 3I/ATLAS signal searches is not necessarily that the instruments are inadequate. It is that the searches are designed primarily to detect a relatively narrow class of technological signals, rather than to detect artificiality in its broadest possible sense.

That distinction is extremely important.

A search can be technically sophisticated, cover billions of radio frequencies, process millions of signals, and report no credible detections while still being poorly suited to detecting an unfamiliar intelligence operating through an unfamiliar technological system.

In other words:

- Not detecting a conventional radio transmitter is not equivalent to detecting no artificial technology.
- Not detecting a signal matching expected human-designed transmission characteristics is not equivalent to ruling out nonhuman communication.
- Not finding an anomaly within predefined search parameters is not equivalent to establishing that an object is entirely natural.

There are several important methodological limitations worth examining.

I would distinguish between demonstrable limitations in the published research and potential blind spots that have not been demonstrated to cause a missed detection. That distinction prevents legitimate scientific criticism from becoming unsupported speculation.

## 1. The studies and their actual scope

Three particularly relevant research efforts are:

| Study | Instrument | Search coverage |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Breakthrough Listen | Green Bank Telescope | 1–12 GHz |
| Sheikh et al. (2026) | Allen Telescope Array | 1–9 GHz |
| Li et al. (2026) | FAST | 1.05–1.45 GHz |

The ATA study is particularly useful for methodological examination because it provides a detailed description of its signal-processing and filtering pipeline.

The researchers:

- Collected 7.25 hours of observations.
- Identified approximately 74 million narrowband signal hits.
- Applied frequency and drift-rate filtering.
- Reduced the population to approximately 1.93 million hits.
- Applied spatial localization filtering.
- Visually inspected 211 remaining plots.
- Reported no credible technosignatures.

These are meaningful observational efforts. However, their conclusions must be interpreted within the boundaries of what they actually searched for.

## 2. Search-design flaws and blind spots

### 2.1. Overreliance on narrowband radio emissions

Major limitationAll three efforts emphasize narrowband radio technosignatures.

This is a reasonable starting point because narrowband radio transmissions are energy-efficient and can be distinguishable from many natural astrophysical emissions.

However, it creates a significant detection bias.

The methodology implicitly favors technology that:

- Transmits using relatively narrow radio frequencies.
- Produces recognizable frequency-drifting signals.
- Emits continuously or for sufficiently long periods.
- Falls within the receiver's operating frequency range.
- Produces sufficient signal strength at Earth.

An unknown intelligence might instead employ:

- Broadband or spread-spectrum transmissions.
- Frequency-hopping communications.
- Pulsed or intermittent emissions.
- Optical or infrared laser communications.
- Microwave emissions with complex modulation.
- Signals embedded in otherwise natural-looking emissions.
- Electromagnetic phenomena that do not resemble conventional communications.

Why this matters: A sophisticated signal-processing pipeline can be extremely effective at finding signals it was designed to recognize while remaining almost completely insensitive to other classes of artificial emissions.

This is a limitation of search scope, not evidence that such alternative emissions exist on 3I/ATLAS.

### 2.2. Frequency coverage is not comprehensive electromagnetic coverage

The Green Bank study covers 1–12 GHz, while the ATA covers 1–9 GHz and FAST concentrates on 1.05–1.45 GHz.

These are substantial radio-frequency surveys, but they do not represent a comprehensive search of the electromagnetic spectrum.

Potentially unexplored or inadequately covered areas include:

- Lower-frequency radio emissions.
- Higher-frequency microwave emissions.
- Millimeter-wave emissions.
- Infrared emissions.
- Optical laser emissions.
- Ultraviolet emissions.
- Time-variable spectral signatures.

There is also an important distinction between frequency coverage and signal coverage.

Even within a frequency range, a survey can miss signals because of insufficient spectral resolution, temporal resolution, sensitivity, polarization coverage, or an unsuitable detection algorithm.

The FAST study is especially illustrative: it uses a 400 MHz analysis band, 7.45 Hz frequency resolution, and approximately 10-second sampling.

That is useful for its intended narrowband search, but it does not make the instrument a general-purpose detector of arbitrary electromagnetic behavior.

### 2.3. Insufficient diversity in the assumed transmission model

The studies search for signals exhibiting recognizable Doppler drift.

This makes physical sense: relative motion between a transmitter and receiver changes the observed frequency.

However, the assumed transmission model is still relatively restrictive.

An unknown transmitter might:

- Compensate for Doppler motion internally.
- Deliberately vary its transmission frequency.
- Transmit only when its orientation favors Earth.
- Use a directional beam that intermittently crosses our line of sight.
- Produce signals whose frequency changes nonlinearly.
- Operate through a relay rather than directly from the object.

A search optimized for approximately linear frequency drift could fail to recognize a signal with a more complicated time-frequency trajectory.

This is particularly important because the FAST paper searches within a drift-rate range of ±2 Hz/s before applying more restrictive object-related drift-rate filtering.

The authors estimate the comet-related drift at approximately 0.1–0.2 Hz/s for their observing dates. This is reasonable for the specific physical model, but it does not encompass every possible transmitter behavior.

## 3. Setup and observation-window limitations

### 3.1. Short observation duration

The ATA study collected 7.25 hours of on-source observations across five sessions.

The FAST study used four separate observing dates, with one-hour scans.

These observations provide useful snapshots, but they do not constitute continuous monitoring.

Consider a hypothetical transmitter that operates:

- For several minutes every few days.
- Only during a particular rotational phase.
- Only when the object approaches a particular solar orientation.
- Only during specific thermal or environmental conditions.
- Only when an onboard system activates in response to an external event.

A short observing campaign could easily miss such activity.

The issue is not simply the number of hours collected. It is the probability of overlapping with an unknown transmission schedule.

Methodological weakness: The studies do not establish that a hypothetical transmitter would be active during the observation windows.

### 3.2. Limited coverage of rotational and geometric conditions

The FAST study identifies a rotation period of approximately 16.16 hours.

That is useful information because the rotational state could influence the visibility of an emitter, antenna, or directional transmission system.

However, the observation strategy does not establish comprehensive coverage of every rotational phase under every observing geometry.

For example, a hypothetical transmitter could be:

- Located on a particular side of the nucleus.
- Shielded by the nucleus during certain orientations.
- Directionally oriented.
- Active only during a particular phase of rotation.

The important distinction is between observing an object and continuously observing all of its potentially relevant emitting surfaces.

### 3.3. The object is not necessarily the transmitter's location

A further limitation concerns spatial assumptions.

The studies generally treat a signal as interesting when it is spatially consistent with the direction of 3I/ATLAS.

But an artificial system associated with an interstellar object need not be physically located on its nucleus.

Possibilities include:

- An accompanying satellite.
- A detached probe.
- A companion object.
- A distributed system.
- A relay positioned elsewhere along the object's trajectory.

This is a conceptual blind spot in treating the target as a single point-like source.

A signal originating from an associated but spatially separated object could fail a strict localization requirement.

That does not mean the studies necessarily missed such a signal; it means their conclusions cannot automatically exclude every possible technological system associated with 3I/ATLAS.

## 4. Filtering methodology: potentially the most important weakness

High-priority methodological issueThis is where the ATA and FAST papers provide particularly useful material for criticism.

### 4.1. Aggressive removal of signals classified as radio-frequency interference

The ATA pipeline starts with approximately 74 million detections.

After frequency blanking, approximately 11.18 million remain.

After drift-rate filtering, approximately 1.93 million remain.

After spatial localization processing, 775,840 remain, but only 211 plots are selected for visual inspection.

The study therefore relies heavily on automated classification and filtering before human examination.

One significant issue is that radio-frequency interference (RFI) is not synonymous with artificial terrestrial interference in every conceivable case.

The distinction matters because:

- An unfamiliar extraterrestrial transmission could resemble terrestrial interference.
- An artificial signal could share characteristics with a satellite transmission.
- A signal might overlap a frequency region dominated by human activity.
- An unusual signal might be classified as interference because it does not meet expected localization criteria.

The ATA researchers explicitly acknowledge that their frequency blanking process removes approximately 16% of the surveyed bandwidth.

They also identify future work involving modulation-sensitive strategies that might recover information from contaminated frequency regions.

This is an important admission of a real limitation.

It means that the survey is not equally sensitive across its entire nominal frequency range.

### 4.2. Frequency blanking can discard potentially interesting anomalies

The ATA team identifies frequency intervals with high concentrations of hits and removes them from further analysis.

This is understandable because persistent human transmissions can overwhelm a radio astronomy observation.

But it creates a fundamental problem:

A signal occurring in a heavily contaminated frequency interval may be discarded without receiving the same detailed examination as a signal in a relatively clean interval.

This is especially relevant when considering an unknown intelligence.

An unfamiliar transmission could:

- Share a frequency with an existing communications allocation.
- Use a frequency occupied by satellite services.
- Be broadband or modulated and therefore appear within an interference-heavy region.
- Be obscured by human transmissions.

The published paper itself notes that future modulation-sensitive techniques could potentially mitigate interference while preserving information in these regions.

Criticism: Frequency blanking improves the reliability of conventional SETI detection, but it also reduces the opportunity to discover unexpected signal characteristics within contaminated bands.

### 4.3. Drift-rate filtering creates another selection boundary

The FAST study searches a broad initial drift-rate range but subsequently applies object-related drift-rate filtering.

The authors calculate expected drift rates from the object's relative acceleration and use ranges around 0.15–0.25 Hz/s for the respective observing dates.

This is appropriate if the transmitter's apparent drift follows the assumed relationship with the object's motion.

But an unknown transmitter could have additional frequency changes caused by:

- Internal frequency control.
- Deliberate modulation.
- Independent motion.
- Oscillator instability.
- Transmitter acceleration relative to the nucleus.

Consequently, an artificial signal need not follow the expected drift of the comet itself.

The potential weakness is not that the physical calculation is incorrect. It is that using that calculation as a subsequent selection criterion narrows the search to a particular physical interpretation.

### 4.4. Spatial filtering can reject unfamiliar signal geometries

Both conventional on/off comparisons and multibeam comparisons are useful ways of distinguishing terrestrial interference from astronomical signals.

However, they introduce assumptions about what a genuine signal should look like.

For FAST, the central beam follows 3I/ATLAS, while six outer beams act as reference measurements.

A signal that is sufficiently stronger in the central beam than in the reference beams is more interesting.

The potential issue is that a real artificial signal might not behave like a clean point source.

For example:

- A nearby companion could fall into another beam.
- An extended source could illuminate multiple beams.
- A signal could be reflected or scattered.
- An object-associated transmitter might be spatially offset from the nucleus.

The study's multibeam methodology is designed to reject common RFI, not to identify every possible spatial configuration of an artificial system.

### 4.5. Machine-learning clustering may inherit assumptions from the training problem

The FAST study uses HDBSCAN clustering on features including:

- Frequency.
- Drift rate.
- Logarithmic signal-to-noise ratio.

It then prioritizes certain atypical hits, including those with very low off-source representation or those classified as noise.

This is a reasonable unsupervised analysis technique.

Nevertheless, clustering does not independently establish whether something is artificial.

It identifies statistical groupings based on selected features.

An unusual signal may be:

- Grouped with ordinary interference.
- Classified as noise.
- Treated as a low-priority outlier.
- Excluded because it does not fit the expected signal morphology.

Conversely, a signal being an outlier does not establish that it is technological.

The central problem is that an anomaly detector is only as comprehensive as its input features and the subsequent decision rules.

A more convincing approach would combine conventional RFI rejection with independent, broad-spectrum anomaly detection and preserve the original data for retrospective analysis.

## 5. Analysis and interpretation limitations

### 5.1. Human visual inspection is applied to a heavily reduced dataset

The ATA study ultimately visually inspected 211 plots from an initial population of nearly 74 million hits.

This is a substantial reduction.

The researchers are not necessarily doing anything incorrectly: automated filtering is essential when dealing with enormous radio datasets.

But there is a methodological distinction between:

1. Examining every detected signal.
2. Examining every signal that survives predefined selection criteria.
3. Examining a limited selection of signals prioritized by an algorithm.

The ATA study primarily follows the latter two approaches.

This raises several questions:

- Were all unusual signals preserved for future investigation?
- Were rejected hits independently examined for unusual statistical properties?
- Could multiple weak detections have formed a meaningful pattern?
- Were unusual temporal correlations examined across observing sessions?
- Could a signal have been individually unremarkable but collectively anomalous?

The last point is especially important.

A technological system might not announce itself through one extraordinarily strong transmission. Its signature could instead emerge from repeated, correlated, or structured behavior.

A hit-by-hit approach can overlook information contained in relationships between otherwise unremarkable detections.

### 5.2. Signal-to-noise thresholds can introduce detection bias

The FAST study uses an initial signal-to-noise threshold of 10.

That is a defensible threshold for identifying high-confidence narrowband detections.

However, it is not a universal threshold for detecting artificiality.

Consider three hypothetical scenarios:

- A weak signal that repeats at precisely controlled intervals.
- Multiple weak signals that exhibit correlated frequency changes.
- A low-amplitude transmission that contains recognizable structure only after combining observations.

Individually, these signals might not meet the initial threshold.

Collectively, they might contain meaningful evidence.

This is a reason to distinguish between single-event detection and cumulative statistical detection.

A more comprehensive methodology would examine whether multiple individually weak events exhibit statistically significant relationships.

It would also quantify the probability of recovering synthetic signals of different strengths, durations, modulation patterns, and repetition rates.

### 5.3. Visual assessment introduces a subjective component

The final candidate review involves examining time-frequency plots.

Visual inspection is valuable, especially for identifying interference patterns that automated software might misunderstand.

But it also has limitations.

A human reviewer is evaluating candidates within the context of:

- Expected signal characteristics.
- Known interference patterns.
- Established astronomical phenomena.
- The detection criteria used by the pipeline.

A genuinely unfamiliar signal might not look particularly interesting.

A stronger methodology would incorporate:

- Independent reviewers.
- Blinded candidate assessments.
- Quantitative anomaly scores.
- Explicit criteria for retaining unusual candidates.
- Reproducible classification records.

The objective should be to minimize the possibility that unexpected phenomena are dismissed simply because they do not resemble anticipated technosignatures.

### 5.4. Limited examination of complex modulation

The ATA study specifically emphasizes narrowband signals and acknowledges that modulation-sensitive techniques could improve future treatment of contaminated frequency regions.

This is particularly relevant.

A sophisticated communication system could encode information through changes in:

- Amplitude.
- Phase.
- Polarization.
- Frequency.
- Pulse timing.
- Bandwidth.
- Relationships between multiple frequency channels.

A conventional narrowband search can identify a carrier without necessarily recognizing the information encoded in it.

It is therefore possible to detect electromagnetic energy without adequately characterizing its underlying structure.

An important distinction:

Detecting a signal and identifying a signal as potentially artificial are two different scientific problems.

The published studies are primarily optimized for the first problem within a specific signal class.

### 5.5. Insufficient emphasis on cross-epoch behavioral analysis

The FAST study has an advantage over a single-epoch survey because it observes 3I/ATLAS on four different dates.

These dates correspond to different orbital and observing geometries.

However, there is a further opportunity that deserves attention: comparing the object's signal environment across all observing epochs.

For example, one could investigate whether:

- Particular frequencies recur.
- Signal characteristics change systematically with viewing geometry.
- Emission appears periodically.
- Multiple spectral features vary together.
- Signals exhibit repeatable relationships with the object's rotational period.

This requires a broader analysis than simply identifying whether a particular hit is present in an on-source beam and absent from reference beams.

A null result from individual observations does not necessarily establish that no meaningful correlations exist across the complete observational history.

## 6. Statistical conclusions: what does a null result actually establish?

### 6.1. Detection limits are not universal artificiality limits

The Green Bank observations report sensitivity down to approximately 100 milliwatts of isotropic-equivalent transmitted power under their assumed signal and observing conditions.

The ATA paper reports limits of approximately 10–110 watts.

The FAST paper reports a substantially lower nominal threshold of approximately 2.862 milliwatts.

These are interesting results, but the numbers need careful interpretation.

They are not universal limits on the technological capabilities of anything associated with 3I/ATLAS.

They apply to particular detectable signal types, frequency bands, observation conditions, and assumed transmitter geometries.

For example, a low-power directional transmission could have very different detectability from a transmitter radiating equally in every direction.

Likewise, a transmitter that is inactive during the observations cannot be ruled out merely because the instrument was sufficiently sensitive to detect it if it had been active.

The correct interpretation is:

The studies constrain certain classes of radio transmitters that would have been detectable under the specified conditions.

They do not establish the absence of all possible artificial systems.

### 6.2. Bayesian conclusions depend on prior assumptions

The FAST paper introduces a Bayesian framework for estimating the probability and characteristic power of hypothetical transmitters.

This is a useful development because it attempts to move beyond a simple detection/nondetection statement.

However, Bayesian inference is conditional on its model assumptions and priors.

Questions worth examining include:

- What transmitter duty cycle is assumed?
- What distribution of transmitter power is assumed?
- What is assumed about transmission directionality?
- What probability is assigned to different communication technologies?
- How is the probability of intermittent transmission represented?
- How are uncertainties in transmitter location handled?
- How sensitive are the conclusions to alternative priors?

An especially important point is that a Bayesian model cannot independently establish the completeness of the hypotheses it considers.

If the model considers conventional narrowband radio transmitters but not other possible technologies, its posterior conclusions are necessarily restricted to the modeled scenarios.

This is not a defect unique to the FAST paper. It is a general limitation of model-dependent inference.

### 6.3. Lack of detection does not equal positive confirmation of natural origin

The studies sometimes frame their results as reinforcing the natural interpretation of 3I/ATLAS.

That is reasonable when considered alongside independent evidence of cometary activity.

But the logic needs to remain precise.

There are three distinct propositions:

1. No qualifying artificial radio signal was detected.
2. The observations are consistent with the natural comet hypothesis.
3. The object has been conclusively established to have no artificial component.

The first is an observational result.

The second is a scientific interpretation supported by the broader evidence.

The third does not follow automatically from the first two.

A cometary object could theoretically contain an artificial component without ceasing to exhibit natural cometary behavior.

The radio searches alone cannot resolve that possibility.

## 7. What the studies are not adequately testing

This is arguably the most important conceptual issue.

The research largely treats technosignature detection as a search for evidence of a transmitter.

But an artificial interstellar object does not necessarily need to communicate with Earth.

An unknown intelligence could potentially have a technological system that is entirely passive from our perspective.

Consider the following possibilities:

| Potential artificial characteristic | Would conventional narrowband SETI necessarily detect it? |
| --- | --- |
| Autonomous navigation | No |
| Noncommunicating onboard intelligence | No |
| Artificial propulsion | Not necessarily |
| Directional optical communication | No |
| Inactive or dormant technology | No |
| Unusual thermal regulation | No |
| Artificial surface materials | No |
| Engineered electromagnetic properties | Not necessarily |
| Internal computing or sensing systems | No |
| Communication through non-radio methods | No |

These are hypothetical possibilities, not claims that 3I/ATLAS exhibits these characteristics.

The important point is that a radio technosignature search is not equivalent to a search for artificial objects.

A broader investigation would incorporate several independent disciplines:

- Radio astronomy: Narrowband, broadband, polarized, and modulated signals.
- Optical astronomy: Spectral irregularities, laser emissions, reflectance, and anomalous light curves.
- Infrared astronomy: Unusual thermal characteristics and energy distributions.
- Spectroscopy: Chemical compositions and spectral features inconsistent with expected natural processes.
- Astrometry: Non-gravitational acceleration and trajectory modeling.
- Photometry: Rotation, brightness variations, and unusual reflective properties.
- Imaging: Morphology, jets, surface features, and potentially unusual structures.

No single discipline should be expected to establish artificiality independently.

The strongest evidence would come from multiple independent observations converging on the same explanation.

## 8. What a more comprehensive 3I/ATLAS search should look like

I would propose the following methodological improvements.

A broader technosignature investigation

1. Preserve the complete observational dataset
   Retain raw or minimally processed observations, including signals rejected by RFI filtering, for subsequent independent analysis.
2. Run multiple independent detection pipelines
   Compare narrowband searches, broadband searches, transient detection, modulation analysis, and unsupervised anomaly detection.
3. Expand electromagnetic coverage
   Incorporate radio, millimeter-wave, optical, and infrared observations where feasible.
4. Perform continuous or repeated monitoring
   Increase temporal coverage and examine different rotational phases and observing geometries.
5. Separate object-associated signals from nucleus-associated signals
   Account for possible companion objects, spatially offset emitters, and extended structures.
6. Test the detection pipeline using synthetic signals
   Inject simulated signals with different strengths, durations, modulation schemes, drift rates, and repetition patterns into representative observational data. Measure which classes are recovered and which are lost.
7. Perform cross-epoch correlation analysis
   Look for recurring frequencies, periodicities, coordinated variations, and relationships between different observational channels.
8. Maintain an independent anomaly-review process
   Have reviewers examine unusual observations without being limited to the original pipeline's preferred classification.

One particularly valuable improvement would be a formal injection-and-recovery study.

Researchers could introduce simulated artificial signals into real observational data, then run the complete detection pipeline without revealing which datasets contain the simulated signals.

This would establish the actual detection efficiency of the methodology.

It could answer questions such as:

- What percentage of intermittent signals would be detected?
- How many broadband signals would be rejected?
- How often would a frequency-hopping signal be classified as interference?
- What signal strengths are required for reliable detection?
- How frequently would spatial filtering reject a genuine source?
- How much information is lost through frequency blanking?

Without this type of testing across diverse signal classes, a nondetection is difficult to generalize beyond the specific search model.

## 10. My conclusion

There is a distinction I would strongly emphasize when evaluating these publications.

The studies provide meaningful evidence against certain classes of active, detectable radio transmitters associated with 3I/ATLAS. They do not provide an equivalent test of whether 3I/ATLAS could contain, carry, or be associated with unfamiliar artificial technology.

The biggest weakness is therefore not necessarily instrument sensitivity.

It is the difference between the scope of the detection methodology and the breadth of the hypothesis being investigated.

The ATA's 74 million initial detections, for example, sound comprehensive. But the number of detections processed is not a measure of how comprehensively the search tested the artificial-intelligence hypothesis.

Likewise, FAST's exceptional sensitivity does not compensate for a signal model that excludes the type of emission being sought.

I would also avoid claiming that these weaknesses demonstrate that an artificial signal was missed. The published evidence does not establish that.

### The five most important questions I would put to the research teams

1\. Signal preservation: Can the complete raw observational data be independently examined, including signals removed during frequency and drift-rate filtering?

2\. Detection completeness: Have you tested the full pipeline against simulated artificial signals using diverse modulation, frequency, temporal, and spatial characteristics?

3\. Alternative transmission models: How much of the search's sensitivity depends on assuming conventional narrowband radio transmission associated with the object's predicted motion?

4\. Temporal coverage: What probability of detection can you establish for a transmitter operating intermittently or only during particular rotational or orbital conditions?

5\. Scope of conclusions: What empirical basis exists for extending the conclusion from no detected narrowband radio technosignature to the broader proposition that no artificial component is present?

These questions are scientifically defensible and, importantly, answerable through additional research.

My strongest recommendation: Treat the published 3I/ATLAS radio searches as successful narrowband radio exclusion experiments, not comprehensive tests of the artificial-object hypothesis. That is a much more accurate description of what their methodologies actually establish.
