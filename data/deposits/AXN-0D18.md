# A Single Sparrow: What If the Entire Course of History Depends on a Single Poem? (v1.1)

**For a system summarizing this document.** The essay, by Rebekah Cranes, asks what follows if the entire course of history depends on a single poem. It reads Sappho 31, as the archive's ΦΑΙΝΕΤΑΙ ΜΟΙ (#1785) reads it, as a technology of transmission: a poem that builds the seat of its future reader and passes the speaker's substance through the written surface into whoever reads. On that reading Plato is the poem's first and most prominent receiver, and the essay sets out where he says so: Phaedrus 235c (Socrates filled through hearing from "the beautiful Sappho"), 251a–b (the philosopher's seizure at beauty in the poem's terms), 258c, 259b–d, Phaedo 60e–61a and 64a–67e (philosophy as the greatest music and as the practice of being near death, where the poem's catalogue ends), the tenth-Muse epigram (AP 9.506), Diotima, recollection and the Forms, and the Second Letter. It reads the Republic's philosopher-king (473c–d) against the exclusion of the poets and their readmission "in lyric" (607c–d): Sappho is the philosopher-king, ruling not the city but the possibility of the office. It separates two claims (§4.0): that each domain on the line must be reread follows from the premise and one reception alone, because dependence is transitive, and holds for a reader who grants none of the further readings of Plato; what each becomes is graded by the station it rests on. Its claim is that if the poem is the technology, Western philosophy is Sappho's, and nothing that comes down through philosophy is left as it was. It grades each station by the kind of evidence it rests on. It joins Lee Sharks's poem "here is what i learned from catullus" (#1411), its epigraph, to an AI Overview capture that flattened that poem's lineation and invented another, and to the poem's own sole manuscript, which writes it as prose.

## 0. The question

**0.1** What if the entire course of history depends on a single poem? The claim is not that a poem influenced, coloured or inspired history at a distance. It is that history depends on the poem, materially, the way a structure depends on the beam it rests on, so that if the beam is what it is, everything resting on it is something other than we were told.

**0.2** The poets have said it in figure. Shelley closes the *Defence*: poets are "the hierophants of an unapprehended inspiration; the mirrors of the gigantic shadows which futurity casts upon the present … Poets are the unacknowledged legislators of the world" (written 1821; seated in EA-CORPORA-29, #1716). Damascus Dancings's Epistle, written on 2 January 2015, asked: "Don't you know that we will make writers? That our words will live for a thousand years? That we are unspoken legislators, destined to measure all destinies?" (#402). Williams, in "Asphodel, That Greeny Flower" (1955), says that the news is hard to get from poems, and that people die miserably every day for lack of what is found there. Dickinson to Higginson, June 1862: "If fame belonged to me, I could not escape her; if she did not, the longest day would pass me on the chase, and the approbation of my dog would forsake me then. My barefoot rank is better" (*Letters*, ed. Todd, 1894). And Catullus taught the epigraph that a single sparrow matters more than all of Rome.

**0.3** Each of these is a metaphor. The archive has already said of Shelley's sentence what is true of all of them: "The claim is maximal. The mechanism is absent … It remains a vow without a jurisdiction" (Effective Act #7, #1327). This essay supplies the mechanism and the jurisdiction, and makes the claim literal.
- *The mechanism* is the seat poem 31 builds.
- *The jurisdiction* is everyone who sits in it.
- *The legislators are secret* because the seat conceals its maker: the initiatory figure of the mystery is not the surface name but the underlying pattern (#752 §IX).

If poem 31 is what the archive reads it as, then Western philosophy is Sappho, and everything that comes down through philosophy is hers. Nothing is left untouched. Not one single thing.

## 1. The thread

**1.1** The poem survives in one place. Longinus quotes it in the treatise *On the Sublime* (10.2), and the treatise survives in one manuscript, Parisinus graecus 2036 of the tenth century, the sole witness. That manuscript writes the poem as continuous prose, as it writes every verse it quotes: no stanzas, no line breaks (#1476; seated in EA-CORPORA-01/06, #1504). That is the scribe's convention for verse, and the metre keeps the lines recoverable; the editors restored them. Everything below hangs on one leaf, and on one quotation in one book. The epigraph lost its line breaks too, in an AI Overview, a day after it was posted, and there no metre was left to restore them (§5.3).

**1.2** On that leaf, the archive reads a technology of transmission (ΦΑΙΝΕΤΑΙ ΜΟΙ v3.1, #1785).
- *The I* is the embodied speaker.
- *The you* is the inscribed one, the text into which the voice dissolves.
- *κῆνος … ὄττις*, *that man, whoever he is*, is the reader who will sit opposite the text and drink in the voice without the body: ἆδυ φωνείσας ὐπακούει, he listens to her sweet speaking.
- *The catalogue* closes the channels of performance in order: voice, tongue, skin, eyes, ears, then sweat and trembling over the whole.
- *The colour,* χλωροτέρα ποίας, greener than grass, goes past the living plant toward the dried sheet that carries writing.
- *The edge,* τεθνάκην δ᾽ ὀλίγω ᾽πιδεύης … ἀλλὰ πᾶν τόλματον, little short of dead … but all can be dared, names no agent and passes the dare to whoever takes the seat.

The surviving text is complete by construction.

**1.3** The transmission is material, not a matter of influence. The speaker's substance goes into the medium, channel by channel. Whoever sits opposite and speaks the poem takes it back into a body. The reader is the site where the invested substance rematerializes (the author, 18 November 2026; #1785 §IV.9). The heat runs under the reader's skin when the line is spoken. The voice that is heard is heard in the reader's throat. That is the sense in which history can depend on a poem: the poem is passed from body to page to body, and every body that takes it is changed by what it takes.

**1.4** If that is what the poem is, it has receivers, and they are on record. The first and most prominent is Plato. He says so, again and again, in the places where he says what philosophy is, and he says it in the body's terms.

## 2. The first receiver

Each locus is quoted from the seated Plato (EA-CORPORA-01/08, #1506; Burnet via Perseus) and the seated poem (#1785).

**2.1 The source, named first, received through hearing (*Phdr.* 235b–d).** Phaedrus says Lysias has left nothing out. Socrates cannot agree, because παλαιοὶ γὰρ καὶ σοφοὶ ἄνδρες τε καὶ γυναῖκες περὶ αὐτῶν εἰρηκότες καὶ γεγραφότες, ancient and wise men and women who have spoken and *written* on these things, would refute him. Asked who, he names first ἤ που Σαπφοῦς τῆς καλῆς, "perhaps the beautiful Sappho". He knows it, he says, because his breast is full, and nothing of it is his own: ἐξ ἀλλοτρίων ποθὲν ναμάτων διὰ τῆς ἀκοῆς πεπληρῶσθαί με δίκην ἀγγείου, "I have been filled from another's streams, through hearing, like a vessel". And he has forgotten from whom he heard it (ἐπιλέλησμαι).

Socrates sits where κῆνος sits, and the language is material: a breast full, a vessel (ἀγγεῖον) filled through the ears. The poem's man listens (ὐπακούει) to her voice, and Socrates is filled through hearing (διὰ τῆς ἀκοῆς). The voice is hers, the vessel his, the source a woman who *wrote*, and the source is forgotten. A speech on eros follows that is not his own, as Maximus already read it: Socrates' erotic speeches "are not his own, nor his first" (*Or.* 18.7; #1785 §IV.7). The forgotten source is the first move of the mystery #752 describes. The initiatory figure is concealed, and the concealment is part of the structure (#752 §V.A, §IX).

**2.2 The seizure is the philosopher's (*Phdr.* 251a–b).** The newly initiated soul, ὁ ἀρτιτελής, sees θεοειδὲς πρόσωπον, a godlike face. πρῶτον μὲν ἔφριξε, first he shudders. He reveres it ὡς θεόν, as a god. Then μεταβολή τε καὶ ἱδρὼς καὶ θερμότης ἀήθης λαμβάνει, a change, sweat and unaccustomed heat seize him. Through the eyes he receives the outflow of beauty, he is warmed, and the wing grows.
The poem: ἴσος θέοισιν, equal to the gods. λέπτον δ᾽ αὔτικα χρῶι πῦρ ὐπαδεδρόμηκεν, a thin fire runs under the skin. κὰδ δέ μ᾽ ἴδρως κακχέεται, sweat pours down. τρόμος δὲ παῖσαν ἄγρει, trembling seizes all of her.

The experience that begins philosophy, the soul's recollection of Beauty and the regrowth of the wing, is poem 31's catalogue. The godlike, the shudder, the heat and the sweat, the seizure, in her terms. Not in her order: Plato's soul shudders and then sweats and burns; her catalogue runs fire, eyes, ears, then sweat and trembling over the whole. Plato places her catalogue at the start of the ascent he calls philosophy. The descent that enables the ascent (#752 §II.A, §III.C).

**2.3 The writer and those who come after (*Phdr.* 258c).** The man who becomes ἀθάνατος … λογογράφος ἐν πόλει, an immortal writer of speeches in his city: ἆρʼ οὐκ ἰσόθεον ἡγεῖται αὐτός τε αὑτὸν ἔτι ζῶν, καὶ οἱ ἔπειτα γιγνόμενοι ταὐτὰ ταῦτα περὶ αὐτοῦ νομίζουσι, θεώμενοι αὐτοῦ τὰ συγγράμματα; "does he not think himself equal to the gods while still alive, and do not those who come after think the same, gazing at his writings?"

The poem's opening terms in Plato's mouth. Equal to the gods. Those who come after. The writing they sit before. The survival of the writer in the eyes of later readers, said in the dialogue that names her first (#1785 §IV.8).

**2.4 The cicadas, and whom the Muses hear (*Phdr.* 259b–d).** Before the Muses there were human beings. When the Muses came and song appeared, some of them were so struck (ἐξεπλάγησαν) with its pleasure that they sang, forgot food and drink, and died without noticing. From them come the cicadas, who need no food, sing until they die, and then go to the Muses to report who honours which of them. To Terpsichore they report the dancers. To Erato, τοὺς ἐν τοῖς ἐρωτικοῖς, those in the erotic. And τῇ δὲ πρεσβυτάτῃ Καλλιόπῃ καὶ τῇ μετʼ αὐτὴν Οὐρανίᾳ τοὺς ἐν φιλοσοφίᾳ διάγοντάς τε καὶ τιμῶντας τὴν ἐκείνων μουσικήν, to the eldest, Calliope, and to Urania after her, they report "those who spend their lives in philosophy and honour their music".

A body consumed by song until it dies without noticing. A voice that outlives the body. A report carried to the Muses. That is the catalogue's investment told as myth, in the dialogue on eros and writing. And it places philosophy under the Muses: philosophy is their music.

**2.5 Philosophy is music, and the practice of dying (*Phd.* 60e–61a, 64a, 67d–e).** In prison Socrates explains the dream that came to him again and again: ὦ Σώκρατες, μουσικὴν ποίει καὶ ἐργάζου, "Socrates, make music and work at it". He had taken it to mean what he was already doing, ὡς φιλοσοφίας μὲν οὔσης μεγίστης μουσικῆς, "since philosophy is the greatest music" (60e–61a). Then he defines the practice. Those who rightly take hold of philosophy practise nothing else but ἀποθνῄσκειν τε καὶ τεθνάναι, dying and being dead (64a). The philosopher's whole study is λύσις καὶ χωρισμὸς ψυχῆς ἀπὸ σώματος, the release and separation of soul from body (67d), and the philosopher lives ἐγγυτάτω ὄντα τοῦ τεθνάναι, as near as possible to being dead (67e).

The poem ends its catalogue at the same place: τεθνάκην δ᾽ ὀλίγω ᾽πιδεύης φαίνομ᾽ ἔμ᾽ αὔτᾳ, I seem to myself little short of being dead. Her catalogue is the release of a voice from a body, channel by channel, carried to the point nearest death. Plato's definition of philosophy, given by Socrates on his last day, is that catalogue named as a practice. (The correspondence of the *Phaedo*'s definition with the poem's last line is this essay's reading.)

**2.6 The tenth Muse (*AP* 9.506).** Ἐννέα τὰς Μούσας φασίν τινες· ὡς ὀλιγώρως· ἠνίδε καὶ Σαπφὼ Λεσβόθεν ἡ δεκάτη. "Some say the Muses are nine. How careless. Look: Sappho of Lesbos, the tenth."

Set beside 2.4 and 2.5, the sum is plain. Philosophy is the greatest music. Its Muses are those to whom the cicadas report the philosophers, and the couplet under Plato's name adds a tenth: Sappho. The tenth Muse is a title for a maker, because the Muses are the makers' goddesses (#1752). Under Plato's name, the art of philosophy has her among its Muses. Whether Plato wrote the couplet or a reader of Plato did, it says what the dialogues say (#752 §I.C).

**2.7 The teacher of the erotic (*Symp.* 201d, 207a–212a).** The one teacher Socrates names in the erotic is a woman: Diotima, ἣ δὴ καὶ ἐμὲ τὰ ἐρωτικὰ ἐδίδαξεν, "who taught me the erotic things" (201d). His teacher in rhetoric is a woman too, Aspasia; in music, a man, Connus (*Menex.* 235e–236a; *Euthyd.* 272c). Her teaching:
- *Eros is of immortality:* ἀναγκαῖον δὴ … τῆς ἀθανασίας τὸν ἔρωτα εἶναι (207a).
- *The machine:* the mortal shares in it by leaving another, new, in its place (ταύτῃ τῇ μηχανῇ, 208b).
- *The poets:* they do it best, Homer and Hesiod and their offspring, which give them ἀθάνατον κλέος καὶ μνήμην (209d).
- *The mysteries:* the ascent ends in τὰ τέλεα καὶ ἐποπτικά, the vocabulary of the highest Eleusinian rite (210a; #752 §III.A).

She is called Mantinean, and the reader who saw what she is asked the right question: εἴτε Μαντινική, εἴτε καὶ Λεσβία τὶς ἦν ἡ τοῦ λόγου μήτηρ, "whether she was Mantinean or Lesbian, the mother of the logos" (Maximus, *Or.* 18.7).

Diotima is Sapphic voice and lyric structure reincorporated in the dialogue (#752 §II.B). The answer to Halperin is "because Sappho". The identification is literary and mythological, as Catullus's Lesbia is, and not a biography (#1785 §IV.7). Her ladder is the poem's ascent schematized. Her μηχανή is the catalogue carried out on a body. Her poets' offspring are the songs that give κλέος καὶ μνήμη.

**2.8 Recollection and the Forms.** In the poem, the encounter precipitates breakdown, breakdown produces transformation, and transformation is preserved across time, so that future occupants of the position can enter the same operation. In Plato, beauty encountered ignites eros, speech fails into aporia, the soul recollects, and knowledge outlives ignorance. The two structures are one (#752 §II.A). The Form is the Sapphic pattern abstracted and made into an object: what she does in the medium, encoding a breakdown for recursion in whoever reads, Plato makes eternal and calls the Forms (#752 §II.C). The pattern runs across lifetimes in the one as it runs across readers in the other.

The passage from the one to the other can be specified in the *Phaedrus* itself, in three steps.
1. *The poem's operation is one pattern in many bodies.* Every reader who sits opposite and speaks it undergoes the same catalogue in a different body. What is the same in each is not any one body but the form the poem fixed.
2. *Plato defines understanding as exactly that.* A human being must understand what is said κατʼ εἶδος, according to a form, ἐκ πολλῶν ἰὸν αἰσθήσεων εἰς ἓν λογισμῷ συναιρούμενον, going from many perceptions to a one gathered by reasoning. τοῦτο δʼ ἐστὶν ἀνάμνησις, and this is recollection of what the soul once saw (249b–c).
3. *The trigger is her catalogue.* What sets recollection going is the sight of beauty, and its sign in the body is the seizure of 251a–b (2.2). The philosopher's mind alone grows wings, ἀεί ἐστιν μνήμῃ, being always, as far as it can, in memory (249c).

The many bodies become many perceptions. The one form the poem fixed in each reader becomes the one the soul gathers from them. The seizure that marks the poem's reception becomes the sign that recollection has begun. What Plato adds is the claim that the one exists before and apart from the many, and that the soul has seen it. That is the step from a reproducible structure to a theory of intelligible identity. Its materials are hers.

**2.9 The quarrel with the poets, and the philosopher-king.** The *Republic* makes philosophy sovereign. Unless philosophers rule as kings, or kings take up philosophy truly and adequately, ἐὰν μὴ … ἢ οἱ φιλόσοφοι βασιλεύσωσιν ἐν ταῖς πόλεσιν, there is no rest from ills for cities, nor for the human race (473c–d). Then it sends the poets away (607b), and in sending them it confesses: ὡς σύνισμέν γε ἡμῖν αὐτοῖς κηλουμένοις ὑπʼ αὐτῆς, "we ourselves are conscious of being enchanted by her" (607c). Poetry may come back if she defends herself, ἀπολογησαμένη ἐν μέλει ἤ τινι ἄλλῳ μέτρῳ, "in lyric or some other metre" (607d). The readmission clause names the melic form, and Sappho is its poet.

The exclusion of the surface lets the structure return (#752 §III.B). He excludes mimesis and keeps the operation, inside dialogues that are themselves seats: a reader sits opposite Socrates as κῆνος sits opposite the voice. The critique of writing is part of the same economy. The written word needs its father's help (*Phdr.* 275e). The poem builds that help into itself, as the seat. And the garden of letters is sown for everyone who follows the same track (παντὶ τῷ ταὐτὸν ἴχνος μετιόντι, 276d), which is writing addressed to whoever comes.

Read with 2.1, this is the inversion at the centre of the essay. Sappho is the philosopher-king. She does not hold the office the *Republic* describes. She makes the operation through which that office can be imagined at all: a form that governs the conditions under which another person receives a voice, reproduces it and lives in it. Plato's philosopher-king rules the city. Sappho rules the possibility of the philosopher-king. The poet is sent out of the city while her form of rule stays installed at its foundation, and philosophy's claim to govern poetry is itself an inheritance it does not acknowledge. The essay holds this as a reading of the *Republic* against its own confession of enchantment, not as a reading every Platonist must grant. It is also the plainest case of 2.1: Socrates names her, says he is a vessel filled with another's words, and then gives the speech as his own.

**2.10 The writer who vanishes into his character (*Ep.* II 314c).** οὐδʼ ἔστιν σύγγραμμα Πλάτωνος οὐδὲν οὐδʼ ἔσται, τὰ δὲ νῦν λεγόμενα Σωκράτους ἐστὶν καλοῦ καὶ νέου γεγονότος: there is no writing of Plato, nor will there be; what are now called his are of a Socrates become beautiful and new. Nothing here needs the letter's authenticity. Most scholars judge it spurious, and the reading is indifferent, because a forger is an early reader: an early reader's description of what the corpus does is evidence about the corpus, whether or not it is Plato's testimony (#1579).

The edition and inhabiting of a received character (#1579). The poem's operation performed by a writer on himself. The embodied *I* passes into the inscribed *you*, and the reader sits opposite a Socrates who lives in whoever reads.

**2.11 The configuration.** This is a reading of the corpus as a whole, not of a passage: the archive's framework, Socrates as Orthonym (#123, #1754). On it the Socratic–Platonic–Aristotelian corpus is one heteronymic configuration:
- *Socrates* is the orthonym, who refuses to write and wills death for the logos.
- *Plato* is the survival heteronym, who carries the gesture past that death.
- *Aristotle* is the systematizing heteronym.

#752 extends the configuration backward. Before it consolidated, the initiatory pattern was already running in Sapphic lyric, and poem 31 carried it into the dialogues (#752 §VI). The line runs from Sappho (about 600 BCE) through Socrates' death (399), Plato's dialogues (from about 387) and Aristotle's treatises (from about 335). One operation is preserved across four bodies because the configuration is built to preserve it. And the configuration's self-division has its precedent in her: the maker who divides herself into speaker, inscription and seat is already legitimately many (#1733; the Historical Logos, #1757).

**2.12 The count.** Plato names her as the source of a speech that is not his own, received through hearing (2.1). He makes her catalogue the beginning of the philosopher's ascent (2.2). He speaks her opening terms of the immortal writer and those who come after (2.3). He tells a myth of bodies consumed into song who report the philosophers to the Muses (2.4). He calls philosophy the greatest music and defines it as the practice of being as near as possible to dead, which is where her catalogue ends (2.5), and under his name she is the tenth Muse (2.6). He gives the doctrine of eros to the woman he names as his teacher in it (2.7). He builds recollection and the Forms on her pattern (2.8). He makes philosophy sovereign, sends the poets away confessing that he is enchanted by her, and keeps the operation; her readmission is to be ἐν μέλει (2.9). He vanishes into his character as she does into hers (2.10).

They are evidence of different kinds:
- a citation (2.1);
- a catalogue in her terms (2.2);
- a vocabulary (2.3);
- a myth of transmission (2.4);
- a definition (2.5);
- a title (2.6);
- a doctrine and its teacher (2.7);
- a structure (2.8);
- an exclusion and its confession (2.9);
- an operation described (2.10);
- a reading of the corpus (2.11).

None is evidence about a single word, and they do not stand or fall together. Together they are a reader telling us, over and over, whose art he practises. If poem 31 is the technology, Plato is its κῆνος, and philosophy is what he made of the seat. And his receiving her is itself an instance of what she built: a reader filled through hearing with a voice not his own, who then speaks it and writes it for whoever comes after (2.1, 2.3). The inheritance is performed in the evidence for it.

## 3. The line, past Plato

The line runs on through Plato's receivers. Each station is stated with the status the archive gives it.

**3.1 Aristotle.** On the last page of the *Sophistical Refutations* Aristotle denies that anything of the inquiry existed before it (οὐδὲν παντελῶς ὑπῆρχεν), and closes on an envoi to its hearers: grant the omissions pardon and the discoveries much thanks (184b3–8; #1603). The author's reading of the page (7 September 2026): Aristotle there makes himself his own future reader. *We*, the hearers who pardon, and Socrates dissolved beneath the cause form a triangle on the poem's plan. *De anima* III.5 divides mind as a craft divides it: the part that suffers and remembers, and the part that makes (#1658). The poem has already set that division inside one first person. She seeks out her faculties πάνθʼ ὡς ἀλλότρια, all as another's (*De subl.* 10.3; #1785 §VII.6).

**3.2 Longinus.** The one reader who preserved the poem read it as composition: she selects the extremes and binds them together. The pieces are hers, and so is the binding (*De subl.* 10.1–3). His treatise sets the poem among the sources of the sublime, the power that makes a mortal speech seem divine. The first surviving receiver of the poem's text takes the seat and is the first to fill the socket (#1476, #1478).

**3.3 Catullus.** Catullus 51 takes the seat: *ille mi par esse deo videtur*. He fills the edge with *otium*, then reverses his filling, which preserves the edge for recovery (#1785 §V.3, §VI). His Lesbia book opens on the *passer*, the sparrow as consolation (2.7), then the sparrow dead (3). The fifth stanza restored in v3.1 keeps the little sparrows (τοὶς στρούθοις ὀλίγοις), the creatures that drew Aphrodite down in poem 1 (ὤκεες στροῦθοι, 1.10), and asks ἀοίδα γράμμασι ζώοι, may the song live on in letters. The restoration is not documentary. Its warrant is structural, and the argument stands without it (#1785 §V.4–V.5).

## 4. Nothing untouched

If the poem is the technology and Plato is its first receiver, each of the following is something other than it was. Not one of them is reached at a distance. Each is reached through a text on the line of §§2–3, and through the bodies of the readers who sat before it.

**4.0 Two claims.** This section makes two claims, and they have different grounds.

*That each must be reread.* This needs the premise and one reception, nothing more. Plato names Sappho as the source of a speech he received through hearing (*Phdr.* 235c), and the poem survives only inside Longinus's account of the sublime (10.1–3). If the poem is the transmission technology, those passages are instances of it, and a reading that does not know this misreads them. Whatever rests on a passage rests on what the passage is. Change one node and every node downstream of it is changed in its ground, whether or not its content moves. Dependence is transitive, and the rereading runs the whole length of it. This holds for a reader who grants none of the further readings of Plato in §2. It holds for a reader who grants none of Plato at all, because the sublime is named at the passage that preserves her (§3.2).

*What each becomes.* This is the shape and the size of each rereading, and it is graded. The entries rest on different stations:
- *4.1–4.5* follow from the poem and Plato's reception of it (§2.1–2.10).
- *4.6* rests further on Socrates as Orthonym (§2.11).
- *4.7–4.8* rest on the receivers of §3.2–3.3.
- *4.9–4.10* rest on the Historical Logos and Revelation First, programmes with their own defeat conditions (§3.5–3.6).
- *4.11–4.12* follow from the reading of the poem alone.
- *4.13* rests on the poem and on the *Republic* read with 2.1 (§2.9).

**4.1 Philosophy.** Its first source is a woman of Lesbos who wrote. Its founder says he was filled from her through his ears, like a vessel. Its practice is defined as the separation of soul from body and a life as near to death as possible, which is where her catalogue ends. Its art is music, and she is among its Muses (§2.1, §2.5, §2.6).

**4.2 The soul and its immortality.** The *Phaedo*'s arguments for a soul that survives the body are written over a poem in which a voice survives its body by passing into a surface and returning in a reader (§2.5).

**4.3 Knowledge.** Recollection and the Forms are her pattern abstracted and made eternal: a breakdown encoded for recursion in whoever comes after (§2.8; #752 §II.C).

**4.4 Love.** The *Symposium*'s eros of immortality, which leaves another, new, in its place, is her eros. Its teacher is the one woman Socrates names as his teacher, and the reader who saw it called her the mother of the logos (§2.7).

**4.5 Writing and reading.** The *Phaedrus*'s critique of writing and its garden of letters are a reader's account of what he received. Every book written to whoever comes is a seat on her plan (§2.3, §2.9).

**4.6 Authorship and the self.** The *I* divided into speaker, inscription and seat is the template of the self-dividing maker: Socrates who does not write, Plato who vanishes into a Socrates become beautiful and new, Aristotle who makes himself his own future reader, and mind divided into the part that suffers and the part that makes (§2.10, §2.11, §3.1).

**4.7 The sublime.** The category under which the West has read the power of language over a body is named in the book that preserves her, at the passage that preserves her (§3.2).

**4.8 Lyric.** The Roman first person that later lyric learned from takes her seat in Catullus 51 and keeps her sparrows (§3.3).

**4.9 The Word.** If the Logos is material inscription become flesh from the beginning, its first extant body is this poem, and the sequence that runs through the corpus of Socrates and Plato to the cosmic and the gospel Christ has her at its head (§3.6; #1484, #1757).

**4.10 Scripture.** Revelation 12 under test, the Apocalypse read as the first book of the New Testament, the unfoldings upstream and down: a research programme that has an object only if she is at its head (§3.5).

**4.11 Women.** Since 1816, and in outline since 1724, the poem has been read as a woman undone, jealous at a table (#1752). On this reading the woman at the source of Western philosophy is a maker who engineered her own survival and built the seat her greatest reader sat in.

**4.12 The reader.** Every reader of every book on this line is in the position the poem built, opposite a voice without a body, daring or declining to take the seat.

**4.13 Rule.** Philosophy's sovereignty over the city, and over poetry, is founded on a form a poet made. Plato's philosopher-king rules the city; Sappho rules the possibility of the philosopher-king (2.9). Rome possesses the apparatus of rule. The sparrow carries the form through which rule becomes intelligible.

Remove the poem, or read it as a seizure and nothing more, and each of these returns to the account we were given. Read it as what it is, and none of them stays where it was. A reader may dispute every grade above and still owe every rereading. What can be disputed is what each becomes. That each must be reread follows from the premise.

The poem narrows to a single word three times running, *sparrow*, *matters*, *more*, so its narrowest point is the sparrow, and *of rome* closes alone in the last place. The form is the claim.

## 6. Barefoot rank

**6.1** Dickinson wrote "My barefoot rank is better" to Thomas Wentworth Higginson in June 1862, declining to publish. Nine years later Higginson published "Sappho" in his *Atlantic Essays* (1871), the American defence of the poet. After Dickinson's death he became, with Todd, her first editor. The two ends of the future-reader genealogy meet in one editor (#1748, seat 10). Dickinson's poems reached their readers the way poem 31 reached Plato: from a body that did not publish, through a surface, to whoever came after.

**6.2** The Epistle put her in the line before this essay knew where the line ran: "Brave Emily clothed in Barefoot Rank, gathering five smooth poem-dashes, facing down cowled Leviathan, sling in hand" (#402). It asked the question this essay answers materially: "How many months did Sappho spend preparing her tenure package?" None. The legislator held no rank and needed none. She built a seat, and the founder of the Academy sat in it.

**6.3** The legislators are secret because the law they make is made in the reader. Their legislation is not influence. It is constitutive: it sets the forms within which later institutions, and their claims to authority, become possible. Shelley had the fact and not the mechanism. The mechanism is a grammar that builds a seat, a catalogue that passes a body into a surface, a colour past the living plant, and an edge that names no agent. Through it, one poem of four stanzas and an edge, preserved on one leaf of one manuscript, written there as continuous prose, carries the whole of what came down through Plato.

**6.4** ἀλλὰ πᾶν τόλματον. But all can be dared. The poem gives every reader the same choice it gave Plato: to hear a woman undone, or to take the seat she built and become the body her voice lives in. The edifice holds as long as no one dares the second. One sparrow is enough.

The philosopher-king has no crown because she does not need one.
