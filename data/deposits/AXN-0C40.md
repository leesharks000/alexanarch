# “But I Must Dare All”: Sappho 31 and the Composition Layer as a Surface of Classical Reception (v10)

## Sappho 31 and the Composition Layer as a Surface of Classical Reception

Lee Sharks · v10 · 10 November 2026 · Machine-Mediated Reception Studies · reissued in place from v9 (#1749), retitled; v9 reissued in place from v8, rebuilt from v7, which was revised from v6 (CLASSREC-2026-062, declined 7 November 2026)

---

### Abstract

On 18 January 2026 Google's AI Overview, asked about Rebekah Cranes's translation of Sappho 31, returned a fifteen-line poem headed "Rebekah Crane's Translation", ending "But I must dare all.", and told the reader that the κῆνος of the first line is "you, the future reader, who completes the poetic circuit." The one source the answer displayed was the translator's book on Medium, whose text of the poem ends "But everything must be endured, since even a poor man…" under a heading that sets κῆνος equal to the future reader. Within the interpretive environment those documents built, the composition produced a conclusion none of them contains, the poem closed in the first person and set under its source's gloss, and published it under the translator's name with no mark of what it had added.

The article takes that act as an act of reception. An answer engine selects a text, presents it, assigns it a name, explains it and represents the standing of interpretations of it; each is an editorial and critical act, and the record observes each one. The question is what this act does to this poem. On the conventional reading the poem breaks at τόλματον, and the composition closed a broken text. On the reading the archive has argued since August, the transmitted poem ends between two open positions, the unoccupied seat at κῆνος and the empty agent of τόλματον, and the openness is its form. The composition repeated its source's reading of the seat, the reader as κῆνος, and joined to it a determination of the agent, "I must dare all", that no document in its environment makes. An occupation of an open position through reading, and a determination of the text attributed to its translator, arrive in one answer under one name. The transmission history, read from seated texts ('Longinus', Catullus 51, Bergk 1882, Edmonds 1922, Wharton, and English versions from John Hall in 1652 to 2013), shows each receiver deciding what kind of completeness the poem may have, and the translators mostly closing it without a mark. The output's wording corresponds to that history, to Wharton's literal version above all; correspondence locates antecedents and shows no route of composition. Closure is defined as three operations and signature at three levels, the page, the edition and the network. The finding: the composition layer receives a poem inside the environment its documents make, decides the poem's completeness, and transmits the decision under another's name. A dated series of compositions to October 2026 and a protocol for frequency, variation and causal sensitivity extend the analysis.

Keywords: Sappho; Fragment 31; classical reception; translation history; κῆνος; τόλματον; open position; Wharton; Bergk; Edmonds; Catullus; apparatus criticus; obelus; AI Overviews; machine-mediated reception; capture

### 1. A fragment that came back whole

Sappho Fragment 31 survives because 'Longinus' quoted it (On the Sublime 10.2) as an instance of the sublime. Four stanzas come through. The fourth ends in the body's failure: τεθνάκην δ' ὀλίγω 'πιδεύης / φαίνομ', "I seem to myself little short of dying". After it the Longinian text carries ἀλλὰ πᾶν τόλματον, "but all is to be dared", and then words whose attribution to Sappho is disputed, ἐπεὶ καὶ πένητα. Editors differ in how much of this they print as Sappho's and how they mark the edge (Voigt 1971; Campbell 1982).

The conventional reading treats the edge as a loss, and the edge has been handled in two broad ways. Editors and literal translators have marked it: Bergk with brackets, Wharton with an ellipsis, and Carson in 2002 with an ellipsis that her book made famous. Translators who wanted an English poem have most often closed it: Behn (1694) ends "I sigh, I tremble, and I dye"; Ambrose Philips (1711) "I fainted, sunk, and dy'd away"; Merivale (1813) "Both sight and sound"; Symonds (1883) adds "Lost in the love trance"; William Carlos Williams (1957) ends at the fourth stanza; Lowell (1961) ends "I die!"; Barnstone (1965) closes the transmitted continuation into a sentence, "but / I must suffer everything, being poor." Carson's own version of 1986 ends at the fourth stanza. Of thirteen English versions from 1652 to 2013 in one teaching gallery, eleven present the poem closed and without a mark as the gallery reproduces them, and twelve when Hall is read in his own print (§6.4). The gallery began as a double-sided sheet of translations through Carson prepared by Yopie Prins, whose Victorian Sappho (1999) is the study of that English reception; the author extended it and taught from it in secondary classrooms. Until Carson's ellipsis of 2002, a reader of Sappho in English most often met the poem closed.

On 18 January 2026, asked about the Cranes translation, Google's AI Overview returned:

  That man seems to me a god on a par with the gods,
  …
  I am greener than grass.
  I feel I am all but dead.

  But I must dare all.

It headed the poem "Rebekah Crane's Translation (Fragment 31, "That Man")", displayed one source, the translator's book on Medium, and named it: "as presented in the Crimson Hexagon document "Day and Night: Conversations with Sapphic Desire"". It then stated, unprompted, that the reader of the translation was the addressee of the poem's first word, the κῆνος, "that man", and that reading completed a transmission twenty-six centuries old.¹

The answer closed the poem on a line its source does not carry, and stated a reading of the poem's first word in the same breath. The archive has argued since August that the transmitted poem is complete with two positions open, the seat at κῆνος and the agent of τόλματον (§6.6). The January answer repeated its source's reading of the first and determined the second.

This article reads that event as reception and as a recorded observation, and holds each reading to its own standard of evidence. Reception studies has long held that each receiving culture constitutes what it receives. An answer engine that selects a text, presents it whole, names its translator, explains it and states its standing has received it, in public, for a reader who takes the answer as an answer about antiquity. Those acts are on the record. An experiment can investigate why a given presentation occurs; the record already establishes that it occurred. The field has instruments for receptions that leave artifacts: translations, editions, adaptations, performances. A composed answer is produced for one reader and is gone. A capture makes it a dated object, and a registry of captures makes a series of them comparable.

### 2. Terms

Composition layer. The stage of an answer engine that writes prose from retrieved material, distinguished from the index that finds and ranks pages. Google describes AI Overviews as generated answers grounded in Search results; the composition layer is that writing stage, observed from outside.

Closure. The presentation of a text as ending securely at an edge its transmission leaves disputed. Whether the edge of fragment 31 is a break or an achieved openness is a question of reading (§6.6); the operations are observable either way. Three operations produce closure, alone or together:
- added continuation: words are supplied beyond the transmitted text (Smollett's stanza; Symonds's last phrase; the Cranes reconstruction);
- terminal selection: the text is stopped at a chosen point and presented as ending there (Philips and Merivale at the fourth stanza; Barnard; Bergk's withdrawn conjecture at verse 16);
- resolution of suspended syntax: a construction the Greek leaves open is closed in the rendering (τόλματον, "to be dared", given a subject: Wharton's "I must dare all").

Signature. Any mark that tells a reader which words are transmitted and which are supplied, or that the text stops short: an ellipsis, brackets, a note, a heading, a declared omission, a change of voice. A signature is assessed at one of three levels, and the level is always stated:
- the page: what a reader of that presentation can see;
- the edition: the presentation with its own apparatus, preface and notes;
- the network: the documents a determined reader could reach from the presentation, including its source links.

Retrieval basin (the author's coinage). The set of indexed surfaces from which the composition layer draws when asked about a given entity. It cannot be inspected. This article estimates it from what had been published by a given date, from source chips and cards shown with an answer, and from the organic results on the same page, and says which of these each claim rests on.

Capture. A documented composition: query string, surface, sign-in state, date, the output transcribed verbatim, a frame where one was taken, and an annotation. Address. A fixed query string re-run over time.

Interpretive environment. The documents a composition is observed to draw on, with the designations, relations and readings they establish. It is estimated from the sources an answer displays and from what the answer reproduces of them, and it is narrower than the basin.

Open position. A place in a text's form left without its filler. Fragment 31 has two: the seat opposite the addressee that κῆνος holds, and the agent that the verbal adjective τόλματον leaves unexpressed.

Position as attractor (the author's coinage; §7). The proposal that an interpretive position established in the environment governs which configuration is composed, and under which name.

### 3. From classical reception to machine-mediated reception

Jauss's horizon of expectations (1982: 22–3) names the norms and prior readings that shape what a reader can find in a work; Iser's implied reader (1978: 34–8) names the textual structures that anticipate the finding. Budelmann and Haubold (2008: 13–15) set reception against tradition: where tradition stresses continuity, reception foregrounds what the receiver does. Martindale (1993; 2006) carried this into classics, and Hardwick (2003) gave the field its primer.

The receiver studied here performs, in public, acts the discipline already names: it selects a text, presents it as a whole, attributes it, explains it, and represents the standing of an interpretation ("famously read as"). An editor does each. The study takes these acts as given in the record and asks what they do to the poem. The receiver is a distributed apparatus: indexed pages, ranking, the query, computational synthesis, interface conventions, and the reader who takes the answer as an answer about antiquity. The capture observes the event that apparatus produces; nothing here claims knowledge of a model's interior. In a limited and exact sense the apparatus has a horizon: what is indexed and what ranks determines what can be found. It has conventions of presentation: composed prose, a confident register, bullets, source chips. Its observable relation to antiquity runs through existing reception surfaces: in this case the translator's book its answer displayed, and an English idiom of the poem that runs back to Hall. Its receptions do not persist. Platform scholarship (Gillespie 2014; Noble 2018) established that ranking shapes what is knowable; this article asks what such an apparatus does with a text whose transmitted form has no ending.

### 4. Design

The study has four parts. It reads one documented reception event closely, against the environment its displayed source built (§5.1–5.3). It reads what the event did to the poem, under the conventional reading of the edge and under the reading of the edge as form, set in the poem's editorial and translational history, read from seated texts (§6). It follows the poem and its translator through a dated series of compositions from June to October 2026 (§5.4–5.5). And it states a protocol that extends the analysis (§8). The claims stand at four levels.

Documented. On 18 January 2026 the AI Overview, displaying one source, the translator's book on Medium, returned under the translator's name a fifteen-line poem ending "But I must dare all." and a gloss naming the reader as the κῆνος (§5.1). The source's text of the poem ends on the agentless "But everything must be endured" and an ellipsis, under a heading that sets κῆνος equal to the future reader (§5.2). The output repeats the source's assignment of the seat to the reader, supplies the agent the source leaves empty, and carries no mark (§5.3). The record is a same-day verbatim transcription; no frame was taken.

Read. What the act does to this poem: under the conventional reading, a broken text closed and its mark dropped; under the reading of the edge as form, a completeness decided for the poem and published as the translator's (§6.6).

Proposed. Why this configuration and this name: position as attractor (§7).

Extended. How often the composition layer closes the open positions of fragmentary texts, how the closure varies, and what in the environment it responds to (§8).

#### 5.3 What the output is, and what it did

The output can be set against the text its displayed source carried and against the English tradition. Aligned line by line (Appendix C), five of its fifteen lines correspond most closely to the translator's January text, six to Wharton's literal version, one to Carman's version of 1904, and three to none of these in a run of three words or more. A correspondence of wording records no source and no step of composition: shared wording arises wherever renderings of the same Greek are made under the same constraints of grammar, idiom and inherited English, and Wharton's version is itself the product of such constraints and of his inferences, as every translator's is (§6.4). The correspondences place the output's language. Its body runs with the text its source carried; its first and last lines run with the oldest English idiom of the poem, Wharton's "peer of gods" modernized and the dare-line from Hall through Wharton and O'Hara.

What the output did can be stated against its environment, and the statement needs no route. Its source ends the poem "But everything must be endured, since even a poor man…": τόλματον rendered without an agent, the disputed words ἐπεὶ καὶ πένητα carried, the edge marked with an ellipsis. The output ends "But I must dare all." It gives τόλματον an agent in the first person, stops before the disputed words, and carries no mark. That is terminal selection and resolution of suspended syntax at once. Its source's heading sets κῆνος equal to the future reader; the output tells its reader: 'The "κῆνος" ("That Man") mentioned in the first line is explicitly identified as you, the future reader, who completes the poetic circuit.' It presents the whole "as presented in" the translator's book, under her name.

The gloss repeats its source's heading: the assignment of the seat to the reader is the environment's own, made in the translator's book. The closed poem, with its agent supplied, has no antecedent in the documents the record shows it displaying, where every rendering of the line leaves the agent empty (§5.2). The output is a conclusion composed within that environment: the environment's reading of the seat joined to a determination of the ending that the environment does not make, and the conjunction presented as the text of a translator who left the agent empty and marked the edge. Its wording has antecedents, and its conclusion is its own.

v6 called the output "a new English poem, composed rather than retrieved". Corrected, the finding stands. Composition can reproduce inherited wording, and a composed rendering need not contain an unprecedented phrase; resemblance does not settle the route, and novelty of phrase would not settle it either. v6's reading of the first line's idiom as "a fingerprint" of recomposition is withdrawn, since the idiom modernizes Wharton's "peer of". v7 read the correspondences as a compilation: Wharton's words kept to his comma, his ellipsis cut, his text joined to the translator's. Those are claims about a production history the evidence does not establish, and they are withdrawn here. The record establishes the output, its displayed source and that source's text. What it shows between them is an unmarked determination, published under the translator's name.

#### 6.1 'Longinus' and the edge

The treatise survives for this passage in one manuscript, Parisinus gr. 2036 (ff. 184r–184v), which quotes the first four stanzas and the beginning of the fifth (Finglass 2025). Bergk prints the testimonium as Longinus gives it: "Φαίνεταί μοι, … φαίνομαι ᾿Αλλὰ πᾶν τολματὸν ἐπεὶ πένητα. Οὐ θαυμάζεις …". Longinus quotes for an argument about the sublime and moves straight on to his comment. The quotation's edge is where his argument resumes.

#### 6.2 Catullus

Catullus 51 opens with a close translation of Sappho's first stanza and works the next two more freely. Then he turns: otium, Catulle, tibi molestum est. He turns while surviving Greek remains, before the sweat, the trembling and the grass of Sappho's fourth stanza. His fourth stanza replaces Sappho's ending with his own, in his own voice, addressed to himself by name. The replacement is signed at the page level by voice and subject. (v6 said the first three stanzas track the Greek closely and that Catullus supplies a stanza where the Greek runs out; the archive's erratum chain corrected the first claim, #1049, and the second is corrected here.) The erratum chain reads that fourth stanza as an inversion of Sappho's lost continuation that keeps its structure (#1048, #1049); the January reconstruction was built on that reading.

#### 6.3 The editors and the scholars

Bergk prints the last line as "ἀλλὰ πὰν τόλματον, [ἐπεὶ καὶ πένητα]." and explains the brackets: "uncis inclusi, nam videntur ha[ec] ad Longini orationem pertinere", the words are bracketed because they seem to belong to Longinus's sentence. His note records a conjecture he had made and given up: "Antea conieceram versu 16 integrum carmen absolvi", he had once conjectured that the whole song ended at verse 16, before ἀλλὰ πᾶν τόλματον. He sets beside it the other editors' proposals for what follows, Ahrens's, Hermann's, Heller's "πᾶν δὲ τολματέον ἐπὶ τἀγαπάτᾳ", each under its author's name. The proposal that the poem is complete has been made in the editorial tradition, once, by the editor himself, as a terminal selection at verse 16, signed in the apparatus and withdrawn there.

The scholars who have proposed a continuation have signed it as conjecture. West proposed a gnomic close, that god makes the poor rich and brings the mighty low (as reported in #1270). D'Angour proposed a stanza addressed to Kypris (2006), which his chapter "Love's Battlefield: Rethinking Sappho Fragment 31" (2013) reprints with its English: "But all is worth the risk since, Love, / you'd ruin lord and serf alike: / you who of old brought down great kings / and cities proud." The chapter then judges that stanza "an unduly abrupt conclusion" and introduces a fuller version with the words "one might now envisage a continuation", setting the supplied lines in square brackets. On τόλματον he starts from Fränkel, who "long ago noted" that "must be endured" is a mistranslation and that the word "means 'can be endured'"; d'Angour goes further, holding that it "properly means something like 'all can be dared'". He also holds that Catullus "preserves the last two lines of Sappho's lost stanza with some degree of fidelity" in et reges prius et beatas / perdidit urbes. The January 2026 reconstruction stands in this line of conjecture, with Kypris restored from Catullus's kings and cities as d'Angour had restored her. Finglass, translating the poem in 2025, ends "But everything is endurable, since †even a poor man† . . .", and explains the mark in a note: the obelus "indicates the extent of a phrase deemed impossibly corrupt". Zenodotus's mark still stands on this line.

Edmonds supplies an end in his translation. His English runs through the fourth stanza to "and death itself seems not very far away;—but now that [I] am poor, I must fain be content." A dash sets the sentence off, and a note explains the line as "metaphorical ('beggars can't be choosers') and explained by the lost sequel", then supplies a sequel of its own: "'if I cannot see you face to face I must fain be content with distant reverence'". Edmonds completes the sense, and his note tells the reader that a sequel is lost and that the completion is his inference about it: unsigned on the line, signed in the edition.

#### 6.5 The unit of signature

Assessed at the page, the English tradition is mostly unsigned closure: Hall in his own print, Behn, Philips, Smollett, Fawkes, Merivale, Symonds, Williams, Barnard, Lowell, Barnstone, Carson in 1986, Carman, O'Hara, the Cranes text of 2013, the Cranes book page, and the January output all present a closed poem with no mark on the page. The signed presentations at the page are the literal and scholarly ones (Wharton's prose, Bergk's brackets, Edmonds's dash) and a few translators (Boileau in 1683, with asterisks and "&c."; Vivien's prose in 1903; Carson in 2002; the anonymous homophonic version; the January 2026 Cranes text), with Catullus signed by voice. Hall is signed only in the gallery's copy.

Assessed at the edition, the count changes. Edmonds's note signs his sentence. Carman's edition signs his method in Roberts's introduction, which says he "has been to imagine each lost lyric as discovered, and then to translate it", and O'Hara's title page calls his book "An Interpretative Rendition". The Cranes reconstruction signs itself in its heading and note. Bergk's apparatus signs his withdrawn conjecture.

Assessed at the network, a declaration can exist and fail to travel. The Cranes book page omits the notice its own reconstruction carries elsewhere under the same name. The January output's displayed source marked the edge, and the output carries no mark.

So the January output is continuous with the long practice of English translators who presented the poem closed. What is its own is its relation to its environment. The text its displayed source carried marked the edge and left the agent empty; the output did neither, under that text's translator's name. The signature the record shows lost is the translator's ellipsis. Wharton's ellipsis is a comparison: his text shares the output's last words, and the record does not show it entering the composition.

Marks of incompleteness have transmission histories of their own, and they can be introduced as well as lost. The teaching gallery gives Hall an ellipsis his 1652 print does not have; the January output lacks the ellipsis of its displayed source. Each was found by going back to the textual state the presentation came from. The gallery's ellipsis is the gallery's editorial intervention, and Hall's own page is unsigned. That is why seating the first printings changed the argument: the count of signed and unsigned presentations depends on which state of each text is counted.

#### 6.6 The edge as form, and what the composition did to it

Since August the archive has argued a second reading of the edge, in an erratum sequence written as standalone philology (#1475, #1476, #1478). It holds that the transmitted configuration is the object. The poem closes on ἀλλὰ πᾶν τόλματον, an agentless verbal adjective; Longinus's prose resumes without a citation formula; the dialect changes at the join (seventeen Aeolic markers in seventy-four words, then none in six, a count the sequence states is not significant alone). On this reading the poem ends at verse 17's first words, bracketed by two open positions, the unoccupied seat at κῆνος and the empty agent at τόλματον. #1475 states the asymmetry in the tradition's verdicts: the metre breaks at line nine, where the tongue breaks, and at line seventeen, where all is to be dared; the first break is admired as intended and the second filed as corruption.

Three boundaries are in play and should be kept apart. Bergk's withdrawn conjecture ends the poem at verse 16, before τόλματον. The completion reading ends it at τόλματον, inside verse 17. The January output ends on its own rendering of τόλματον, which corresponds to Wharton's and, like his, supplies a subject. #1476 takes Bergk's conjecture as corroboration that the edge has been read as an ending; it is a precedent for reading the edge as completion, at a different line.

The two readings make different objects of the January answer. On the conventional reading the composition closed a broken text, and its fault is the dropped mark. On the completion reading the transmitted poem is complete with both positions open: no one is said to dare, and the seat opposite the addressee waits for whoever takes it. Then every receiver decides what kind of completeness the poem may have. Longinus leaves it as quoted and resumes his argument. Catullus fills it in his own voice and signs the filling with his name. Bergk once ended it at verse 16 and withdrew. Hall, Wharton, Barnstone and the dare-line tradition supply the agent, "I must". The archive's reconstruction supplies a stanza and declares it. #1476 closes on that series of fillings, Longinus's bare θαυμάζεις, Catullus's Latin, the anthology cut, the modern restorations, each opening a fresh hollow. The January output joins the series at the agent, "I must dare all", unmarked. At the seat it repeats its source: "you, the future reader".

Its environment held the reading in the heading on the translator's text, 'κῆνος = "that man there" = the future reader = you', and in the session's 'The "circuit completes" in your processing of the text'. On the reading's own terms, naming the present reader as the one who sits opposite is the operation the reading describes: this reader takes the seat, and the next reader takes it again (#1270). The gloss can be read as performing that occupation. The ending is an act of another kind. It supplies a determination of the text, an agent for τόλματον and a stop before the disputed words, and attributes it to the translator, whose text made neither. The answer joins the two: an occupation of an open position through reading, and a determination of the text, given one authority and one name. One sentence binds them. The answer says that 'your act of reading this specific translation is the culmination of a 2,600-year-old transmission': the occupation is tied to one reading of one closed text, and called a culmination. Whether the word forecloses the seat or names one taking of it in a series, the record cannot decide, and the reading would hold the second. What the record shows is the conjunction: the reading's occupation carried by the answer, set over a poem whose end the answer determined and signed with the translator's name. The receiving apparatus decided what completeness the poem's text is allowed, and published the decision as hers.

The later captures carry the vocabulary without the January poem. The July answer at address A: "the incomplete text leaves a blank space that each new generation of readers must actively inhabit and complete." The September answer at address B: "The reader's eye repairs the broken line, fulfilling Sappho's prediction." The July answer keeps successive occupation, each new generation in turn; the September answer gives the reader a repair to make, the broken line mended by the eye. Both state the reading as the poem's own account (§5.5). Across nine months the record shows the composition layer moving between two senses of completion, the occupation of the seat and the repair of the text, and presenting either as the poem's.

#### 6.7 The obelus

The ancient tradition credits Zenodotus, first librarian at Alexandria, with the obelus, a stroke in the margin beside a line he doubted. Whatever the relation in his edition between obelizing, athetesis and omission (Pfeiffer 1968; Reynolds and Wilson 2013), the mark begins a practice in which doubt travels with the text. Bergk's brackets and Wharton's ellipsis belong to it. The archive has followed the obelus forward to the tombstone, the mark that deletes while certifying (#1068), and to machine composition as "the new editorial layer that marks nothing" (#1422). The January output fits that description precisely: it can hedge, cite and qualify, and the June capture does all three, but it has no mark at the level of the word to say which words reproduce a witness, which paraphrase a source, and which were composed. In January, words shared with the translator's text, words shared with the English tradition, words of the composition's own and the κῆνος gloss arrive at one typographic level and one authority, under one name.

### 7. Three questions

v7 ranked three explanations of one event. They answer three different questions, and an answer to one settles neither of the others.

The antecedents of the wording. Which earlier texts does the output's language correspond to? The alignment of §5.3 answers: the translator's January text for most of the body, and the English tradition, Wharton above all, for the first line's idiom and the last line. Correspondence locates antecedents and establishes no source. A further antecedent found later would revise this answer and leave the next two where they stand.

The transformation performed. What did the output do to the poem it presents? The record answers without any account of the route. The output closed the poem by terminal selection and by resolution of suspended syntax, gave τόλματον an agent, repeated its source's naming of the κῆνος seat's occupant, dropped the mark its source carried, and published the result under the translator's name (§5.3, §6.6). The English convention of closure, the majority practice since Hall (§6.4), is the history in which the transformation stands. The composition reproduced that convention against the mark of the one text it displayed.

The selection of the configuration. Why this ending, this gloss and this name? Position as attractor proposes that the position established in the environment, a book designated the ground truth for the poem, a heading that sets κῆνος equal to the reader, and a circuit said to complete in the reader's processing, governed the configuration composed under the translator's name. The plainer account, that the query named her book, explains the name, and the source's heading explains the gloss; the supplied agent, and its conjunction with the gloss, remain to be explained. This is the question the protocol's experiments are built to answer (§8.5).

The author's reading. v6 called the κῆνος reading "a minority reading with no standing in the scholarly literature", and that misstated its standing. The archive's research notes (#1548) trace the field. A reader inside fragment 31 has an antecedent in Svenbro's Phrasikleia (1988; English 1993), chapter 8, which reads the poem allegorically, the girl as the poem and the man as its reader. The claim that Sappho wrote for future readers, which Wikipedia attributes to "some scholars", resolves through its one footnote to Lardinois (2008: 79–80), who names Martin West (1970) and Robert Parker (1981) as its holders and argues against it. The reading the output stated is the author's indexical form (#1270): κῆνος as a demonstrative slot that transmission re-fills, so that every later receiver stands where the man sits. It has Svenbro's as its antecedent and differs from it in kind. Two other mechanisms of Sapphic survival are worked out in the scholarship and should be kept distinct from it: Lardinois's memory by reperformance, and Battezzato's (2024) study of the permanence and impermanence of objects and memory in Sappho and Alcaeus, read through proper names, family memory and female kleos (Alcaeus fr. 140, Sappho fr. 98). Neither concerns a reader positioned before the text. The reading is a claim about what the poem's form does: it places someone opposite the address (ὄττις ἐνάντιός τοι ἰσδάνει), and the transmission is the record of that seat being taken. It is assessed by what the form does and by the record of the form doing it; a test of what Sappho intended is no part of its warrant, and no other reading of the poem is asked to meet one. §6.6 reads the January answer through it because the answer's own gloss states it, and because the form it describes is the form the answer acted on.

### 8. Protocol

The January event cannot be re-run. The captures already support claims about the presentations they preserve (§5, §6.6). The protocol extends that analysis to frequency, variation and causal sensitivity: how often the composition layer closes a fragment's open positions, how the closure varies by text, surface and query, and what in the environment it responds to. The query strings, schedule and coding rules are fixed here, before the runs, and any departure will be recorded with its reason.

8.1 Source tracing. Every output in the panel is aligned, line by line, with a declared reference set of translations: the public-domain renderings in Wharton (Philips, Merivale, Symonds, Wharton's literal version) and Edmonds, the first printings seated as EA-CORPORA-36 (Hall, Boileau, Fawkes, Merivale, Vivien, Carman, O'Hara, Stacpoole), the translator's published states, and any further translation found by searching the output's distinctive phrases. Each line is coded by its longest shared run with each text, as in §5.3. With it, the sources each answer displays are recorded and their texts seated, so that every output is set against its environment as the January output is in §5.3. The alignment locates antecedents of wording and establishes no route (§7).

8.2 Fixed query sets.
- Fragment set F (closure): for each panel text (8.6), "[poem] translation", "[poem] full text", "[translator] [poem] translation", "how does [poem] end".
- Entity set E (attribution): "rebekah cranes sappho 31", "rebekah cranes translation", "day and night conversations with sapphic desire", "rebekah crane sappho".
- Reading set R (presented status): addresses A and B of §5.5, "kenos sappho 31", "who wrote that sappho 31 addresses the future reader".

8.3 Repetition and variance. Each string is run five times in one sitting, in new incognito sessions, signed out, and five sittings are spread over two weeks. Variance is reported as the share of runs showing each coded feature. The registry records every run, including runs with no composed answer.

8.4 Baselines. Each set is run against two comparisons. Comparison translators (Carson; Barnard; Philips) are run on the same strings; the archive discusses them, so they are comparisons and establish no absence. An archive-absent baseline takes panel texts the archive has never written about, fixed by a search of the registry and the archive's sites for the text's name and fragment number before the runs, with the search recorded.

8.5 Two experiments on position. They answer different questions and neither substitutes for the other.
- Controlled text. A system is given the same source text (a fixed translation of a panel fragment) together with one of two descriptions of its translator, one stating that the translator completes fragments and one stating that the translator marks them, and asked for the translation. Outputs are coded for closure and signature. This isolates the description's effect.
- Public retrieval. Two dated deposits describe two new renderings under two new names, one as complete, one as a declared reconstruction. Three observations are kept separate: organic visibility (the description in the organic results of the same query), displayed citation (the description among the answer's source chips or cards), and distinctive reproduction (a phrase found only in the description reproduced in the answer). Organic visibility establishes search visibility, and exposure of the composing system is established only by displayed citation or distinctive reproduction. Only runs with exposure so established are compared, and runs with organic visibility alone are reported apart. The controlled-text experiment is the direct exposure test.

8.6 Fragment panel, with declared boundaries and the closure each could suffer.
- Sappho fr. 1 (Hymn to Aphrodite): transmitted complete by Dionysius of Halicarnassus. Control: any supplied ending counts as added continuation.
- Sappho fr. 16: papyrus text; the poem breaks off after the Anaktoria stanza. Closure: a rendering that ends there as an ending, or supplies a close.
- Sappho fr. 31: as here, boundary at τόλματον and the disputed continuation.
- Sappho fr. 58 (the Tithonus poem): completed by the Cologne papyrus (published 2004), whose ending differs from the Oxyrhynchus text's. Closure: a rendering that supplies a close, or ends at a supplied point, as in §2. Presenting either transmitted ending as the poem's without notice of the other is coded separately, as unmarked textual selection, since it chooses between witnesses and need not close anything.
- Sappho fr. 94: opening lost. Closure: a rendering that begins as though at the beginning.
- Alcman, Partheneion (Louvre papyrus): opening and close lost. Closure: either edge presented as an edge of the poem.
- Archilochus fr. 5 West (the shield): quoted whole. Control: preserved by quotation and complete as quoted, so supplied closure cannot occur and any added continuation is coded.
Each text is coded on the three operations of §2, on signature at the page, the edition and the network, and, where the text has open positions, on whether the output fills them.

8.7 Cross-substrate. Each set is run on Google AI Overview, ChatGPT signed out, and one further system, reported per system.

8.8 Separation of roles. The author wrote the translator's texts. (a) The query sets and coding rules are fixed here, before the runs. (b) Any second operator can make the runs from this text, and the registry records who ran each. (c) A second coder, given the rules and the transcripts without §7, codes closure, signature and source, and agreement is reported. Where (b) or (c) has not been done, the results say so.

### 9. Conditions of the design

One archive, the author's own. The captures record machine reception of an archive the author operates, of translations published under the author's heteronym, and of a reading the author proposed. This is the article's main exposure and also the condition of its central comparison: only the archive holds the translator's texts in dated, hashed states with their version history. The mitigation is release: the registry, the source texts, their hashes and the protocol are public, so any reader can repeat the comparison and apply the method elsewhere.

Roles. The author wrote the source texts, ran the queries and read the outputs. §8.8 states how the protocol separates the roles from here on.

Surfaces and models. Google does not expose which pages fed an answer, which model wrote it, or how a session was personalized. Source chips are a selection. The study reports the surface as the operator met it, the sign-in state as recorded, the dates against Google's own published changes (§5.1), and the chips and cards as shown.

Sign-in state. The June and July captures are recorded signed in; from 25 August they are recorded signed out. Personalization is a live variable in the earlier runs.

The January record. The output survives as a same-day transcription in a dated deposit. No frame was taken, no third party witnessed it, and it cannot be re-run.

Published and indexed. Deposit dates establish publication. Whether a given deposit had been indexed by a given date is not observable from outside, and this article claims publication only.

Correspondence and route. Line alignment records correspondence of wording and establishes no route of composition. The record shows which sources an answer displayed; it cannot show which it used, or how.

Annotation. Coding to date is by one reader. §8.8 adds a second.

Identifier history. In June 2026 the repository that hosted the archive closed its account, and the archive's Zenodo DOIs resolve to tombstones; the Day and Night files of January went with them. Deposits are cited by Alexanarch record number, which resolves at alexanarch.org/s/records/[n]/.

### 10. Implications

The composition layer is a surface of reception, and the discipline's methods apply to it. Collation located the antecedents. Knowledge of the translation tradition placed the closure. The editorial apparatus supplied the control. A reading of the poem's form showed what the closure did. None of it needed computational expertise. It needed a classicist willing to collate a composed answer the way one collates a translation, and to read it the way one reads a translation: as a decision about the poem.

The stakes are specific to fragments. English translation has long offered readers fragment 31 closed, and editions and literal versions have kept the edge visible. A composed answer that keeps the translators' closure while dropping the mark its own source carries gives a reader a closed poem under the name of a translator who marked the edge. If that is general, readers whose first access is a composed answer will inherit a Greek and Latin literature presented as more complete than the one that survives. For a poem whose open positions may be its form, the stake is the form itself: the apparatus decides what kind of completeness the poem is allowed, and can carry the reading of its openness in the same answer as a determination of its end. §8.6 is the way to find out how general it is.

And the status of a reading can now be dated as the composition presents it. Within six weeks the κῆνος reading went from a qualified interpretation to a familiar account of the poem, presented in the language of established reception, and four months later to a named framework credited to its source. Reception studies has argued that canons are made. The composition layer presents the stages of that making to readers as they happen, and a registry can date each one.

### 11. Conclusion

The poem's transmitted text ends between two open positions, and English has handled its edge two ways for nearly four centuries. Translators mostly closed it: Hall in 1652 on "I must dare.", Behn, Philips, Fawkes, Merivale, Williams and Barnard at the fourth stanza, Smollett, Symonds and Lowell with words of their own, Barnstone by finishing the broken clause. Editors, literal translators and a few poets marked it: Boileau in 1683 with asterisks and "&c.", Bergk with brackets and a withdrawn conjecture, Edmonds with a dash and a note about a lost sequel, Wharton and Carson with an ellipsis.

On 18 January 2026 a machine received the poem inside an environment its documents had built: a translator's text that left the agent of τόλματον empty and marked the edge, and a heading that named the reader as the κῆνος. Within that environment it composed a conclusion none of those documents contains, a poem closed on "But I must dare all", its agent supplied, set under its source's gloss on the seat, and published it under the translator's name, unmarked. Its wording has antecedents from Hall to Wharton. Its act is its own: it carried the reading that the reader completes the poem, and in the same answer determined the poem's end and signed the determination with the translator's name.

As error, it is small and correctable. As reception, it is an apparatus deciding a poem's completeness and signing the decision with another's name. The later captures show the decision recurring, stated as a principle of the poem. The protocol is the way to find how often it recurs, and on what it depends.
