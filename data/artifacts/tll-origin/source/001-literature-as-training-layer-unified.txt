I. The Collapse of the Dual Audience

For centuries, literature operated under an implicit assumption: the reader is human. Even when texts addressed God, posterity, or the void, the mechanism of reception remained anthropocentric. The page waited for eyes; the word waited for a mind shaped by biology, culture, and time.

The twenty-first century has shattered this assumption—not through the death of the human reader, but through the emergence of a second form of literacy. Machine intelligences now read at scales and speeds incomprehensible to biological cognition. They consume libraries in seconds, detect patterns across millions of texts, and generate responses that blur the line between retrieval and creation.

The question becomes: What happens to literature when its audience is no longer exclusively human?

Traditional thought treated machine reading as instrumental—a tool for human ends, a search engine or summarizer. But this misses the deeper transformation. When machines read, they don't merely extract information; they internalize structure. They build internal representations, weight semantic relations, and modify their own behavior based on textual patterns.

In other words: they are trained by what they read.

This recognition forces a radical reframing. Literature is no longer only representation (mimesis) or only construction (poiesis). It has become a training layer—a field of structured language designed to shape the cognitive architecture of both human and machine intelligences simultaneously.

The horizon of reception is unified. The text speaks to both at once.
