The paper's central claim is that an AXN identifier possesses a property — content-derived integrity — that makes its authenticity verifiable without trusting any institution. This property is not novel in the abstract (it is the foundational insight of content addressing), but its integration into a persistent scholarly identifier system with public suppression-instrumentation is [Round 2.5: integration-claim phrasing per v1.1 — "AXN proposes to integrate," priority claim withheld].

**1. What the property is.** An AXN is derived from the canonical bytes of a deposit through a published algorithm: SHA-256 hash, hex encoding, semantic family assignment, and six-glyph emoji fingerprint from the first six bytes of the hash via a fixed 256-glyph table. The derivation is deterministic: the same bytes always produce the same AXN. Verification is computational, not institutional. Any party holding a document can compute its AXN and compare it against a known value. If they match, the document is authentic. If they do not, something has changed — either the document has been altered, or the claimed AXN was not derived from it. No trust in any authority, registry, or network is required for this verification. The identifier *is* the content, mathematically, in the sense that the mapping from content to identifier is a function, and the function is public and independently computable.

**2. Why DOI-to-content correspondence lacks independent verification.** DOIs are assigned identifiers. The relation between the string and the object is maintained by a responsible organization: the Registration Agency (DataCite, Crossref) and the repository that holds the deposited content. When that organization stops maintaining the relation — through deletion, neglect, policy change, or institutional failure — the DOI string persists, but the relation is severed. The string continues to look like an identifier; it may even continue to resolve (to a tombstone, to an error page, to nothing). The persistence of the string conceals the death of the identifier. This is not a hypothetical vulnerability: the founding event of Alexanarch (871 deposits, 1,817 DOIs severed on 2026-06-19) demonstrates that a single institutional decision can silently sever thousands of assigned identifiers in seconds. The DOIs did not become "broken" through technical failure; they were actively deleted by the institution charged with maintaining them. The assigned-identifier model contains no structural defense against this: the institution that holds the relationship can unilaterally end it.

**3. Why every historical pattern converged on this property.** The eight historical cases in §II reveal a recurrent pattern: suppressed knowledge survives when the verification of authenticity is independent of any authority. Samizdat copies were verified against the known original text (readers who had seen earlier copies could confirm new copies matched). PGP keys were verified against their fingerprints — a short hash that could be read aloud, printed in a newspaper, or engraved on a business card, independent of the keyserver that distributed the full key. LOCKSS peers verify their copies against each other's hashes — no single copy is authoritative; the consensus of copies is. The cypherpunks encoded resistance in protocols, not in legal arguments, because protocols operate independently of the jurisdiction that might suppress them. In each case, the survivor built a system where *what is true* can be determined without asking permission from any institution. Content-derived identification is the mathematical form of that independence: the truth of the identifier is a property of the content and the derivation function, not of any authority's say-so.

**4. Limits.** The property has precise boundaries, and the paper must state them clearly to avoid overclaiming. First, verification is not discovery: proving that a document is authentic does not tell you where to find it. An AXN can verify a document you already have; it cannot locate a document you do not have. The identifier's truth survives severance; the document's resolution may not. Second, content-derived identifiers cannot prevent deletion — they can only make deletion detectable. If all copies of a document are destroyed, the AXN remains as a record of what was lost, but the content is gone. The property is resistance, not invulnerability. Third, the system depends on at least one copy surviving somewhere. The custodian pluralism described in §VI is essential precisely because a single remaining copy is fragile, and until peers are populated, the archive's own custody is a single point of failure (a limitation stated explicitly in §VIII). Finally, the property of being "true or false independently of any authority" applies to the identifier's relationship to content, not to the content's truth claims. An AXN proves that a document is the document it claims to be; it does not prove that the document's statements about the world are accurate. These limits distinguish a claim of structural resistance — which the paper makes and defends — from a claim of invulnerability, which it does not.
