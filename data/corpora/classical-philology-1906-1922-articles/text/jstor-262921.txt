ends may be reached occupy the main body of the book. The latter part is de- 
voted to suggestions intended to add to the interest of the department.

young teacher who means business cannot afford to be without this book.

fitted to the needs of the high-school teacher.

HOW THE BIBLE GREW 

By FRANK G. LEWIS 

Crozer Theological Seminary 

This is the first single work to record the growth of the Bible from its 
beginning up to the present time. It presents in an interesting way the 
entire literary development of both Testaments and shows how they have 
been handled by translators in the production of the many versions which 
have appeared through the centuries. Thus it answers many perplexing 
questions about authorship, sources, time of writing, and variation of 
versions. 

The author has given the Bible an opportunity to tell its own story: 
how it came into existence, and how it came to be the Bible. The general 
reader will find the book of unusual interest and every student of the Bible 
should have a copy-in his library. It may be used as a textbook for col- 
lege or adult Sunday-school classes.

LATIN SONGS 

FOR YOUR CLASSES, CLUBS, PROGRAMS, ENTERTAINMENTS 

IN A POCKET-SIZE PAMPHLET 

CARMINA LATINA 

Selected and Edited by ROY C. FUCKINGER 

Professor of Greek and Latin 

Northwestern University 

Most energetic teachers of Latin nowadays recognize the advisability of 
relieving the steady pull of their teaching by some kind of diversion. Some devote 
a few minutes of each recitation to such an object, others set apart a certain portion 
of the week's work, but a majority maintain a Latin club which meets outside of 
school hours. In any case music is one of the teacher's most valuable aids, 
reviving the flagging interest of the pupils and fixing Latin words in their memories. 
The difficulty, however, has been that suitable texts have been accessible only to 
the instructors, who have been compelled to copy the words on the blackboard and 
to have them recopied by the students. What teacher has not wished that the 
songs might be available in some convenient, inexpensive form which could be 
placed in each pupil's hands without waste of time ? 

This need is now fully met by Professor Flickinger's Carmina Latina, a sixteen-page booklet 
which sells for ten cents a copy. This contains such indispensable favorites as Gaudeamus Igitur, 
Integer Vilae, Lauriger Horatius, and Adeste Fideles. It also presents versions of America and The 
Star-S pangled Banner. There is a hitherto unpublished version of Lead, Kindly Light, as well 
as a hymn attributed to St. Augustine which can be sung to the tunc of Glorious Things of Thee 
Are Spoken. Mica, Mica, Parva Stella is already a general favorite but is usually spoiled by an 
unworthy musical setting. The wistf ulness and true nobility of the poem are much better brought 
out by the tune which the editor has here proposed. Up to this point we have texts without the 
notes, since the tunes are all familiar and can be found in all hymnals and college songbooks. But 
for the noble hymn of Northwestern University, Quaecumque Sunt Vera, both words and music are 
given. Who ever heard of a Latin round ? Yet here are words and music for an easy round which 
has already been used with great success at the Chicago Classical Club and in several classrooms. 
Finally comes the Greek text of Tyrtaeus' Marching Song which Professor Flickinger has discovered 
can be sung to the same tune as Columbia, the Gem of the Ocean. 

The convenience and inexpensiveness of this collection, containing both old and new materials, 
with fresh suggestions on every page, insure a widespread demand in classical circles.
