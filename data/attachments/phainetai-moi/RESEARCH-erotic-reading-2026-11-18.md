## Open sources read

1. **Wills, Garry. "Sappho 31 and Catullus 51." *GRBS* 8 (1967): 167–97.** Open: https://grbs.library.duke.edu/index.php/grbs/article/download/11111/4201/14033
   - The jealousy reading "does not take seriously the general statement" (ὡς … ἴδω) (p. 170).
   - "the fact that the man is given prominence and then ignored" (p. 170).
   - Welcker's reading "denies that the only prominent role the man can play is that of a rival" (p. 173).
   - "Sappho, too weak to do what the godlike man of the first lines does, is dizzied by the mere sight of the girl." (p. 173)
   - "in fr.31 we do not witness Sappho paling, sweating, fainting at the sight of a particular scene." (p. 189)
   - "These reactions are offered as an explanation of her general inability to stand the girl's bright presence." (p. 189)
   - "Sappho is telling herself that all these symptoms can be borne" (p. 190)
2. **Lefkowitz, Mary R. "Critical Stereotypes and the Poetry of Sappho." *GRBS* 14 (1973): 113–23.** Open: https://grbs.library.duke.edu/index.php/grbs/article/download/9201/4573/14413 (the fetched text confirms author, title and pages; the journal and volume are from the URL)
   - "Again a portrait emerges of an emotional deviant" (p. 116).
   - Of Wilamowitz: "there is no evidence at all in the text that 'that man' is a husband" (p. 117).
   - Page's view of the language: "realistic, severely plain and candid, unadorned by literary artifice" (p. 118).
   - Devereux "sees the poem rather as an emotional outpouring of 'envy' of 'that man'" (p. 119).
   - "The man has no specific identity" (p. 120).
   - "There is nothing specifically stated in the poem about jealousy of a rival." (p. 121)
3. **Tsagarakis, Odysseus. "Some Neglected Aspects of Love in Sappho's Fr. 31 LP." *RhM* 122 (1979): 97–118.** Open: https://journals.ub.uni-koeln.de/index.php/rhm/article/download/10183/10401/21508
   - The survey: the jealousy camp is Page (1955) and Devereux (1970); the erotic-love camp is Marcovich 1972, Wills 1967, Kirkwood 1974 and Privitera 1969.
   - Its own view is a third: "a negative emotional disturbance, not a response to love" (p. 104).
   - "The feelings expressed abound in Greek love songs." (p. 117)
4. **D'Angour, Armand. "Love's Battlefield: Rethinking Sappho Fragment 31."** In E. Sanders, C. Thumiger, C. Carey and N. J. Lowe (eds.), *Erôs in Ancient Greece* (Oxford, 2013). Open proof on the author's site: https://www.armand-dangour.com/wp-content/uploads/2018/02/Loves-Battlefield-.pdf. The proof's pages are 60–71; the volume details are not on the proof and are to be confirmed.
   - Interpretation "has, moreover, been heavily and misleadingly influenced by Catullus' near-translation of these stanzas" (p. 62).
   - "for whom 'that man' is an object of admiration rather than envy" (p. 62).
   - "Arguably, however, such feelings have no place in the circumstances surrounding Sappho's poem" (p. 62).
   - Of the wedding reading: "This once dominant interpretation … has now largely fallen from favour" (p. 62 n. 12).
   - "the change of tone at this point in the poem strikes a note of defiant resolve, rather than one of resignation" (p. 67).
5. **Engelmayer, Caroline. "A Lyric *Aristeia* and a Lover's Rout: Gender and Genre in Sappho 31."** *Classics@* 16 (Center for Hellenic Studies), open: https://classics-at.chs.harvard.edu/classics16-engelmayer. The year is not on the page.
   - "He himself is divine within the logic of the poem."
   - Six of the eight symptoms have Homeric counterparts; "love is more perilous than battle".
   - "Hers is a heroism characterized by fearless *attempts*."
   - πὰν τόλματον "suggests that the Speaker is determined to look at the woman again".
   - On Marcovich's contrast-figure: "I find the argument that he *is* her lover to be convincing" (n. 10).
6. **Furley, William D. "Fearless, Bloodless … like the Gods: Sappho 31 and the Rhetoric of 'Godlike'." *CQ* 50 (2000): 7–15.** The article is walled. Its extract and footnotes are public on Cambridge Core: https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/classical-quarterly/article/fearless-bloodless-like-the-gods-sappho-31-and-the-rhetoric-of-godlike/62A90B4D6C9098B1FD17CA075E90045A
   - The extract frames the debate as whether Sappho is moved by "jealousy at seeing her beloved girlfriend in intimate colloquy with a man".
   - n. 2 quotes Most: "a clear drift in the last decades towards interpreting the poem as an expression of homoerotic passion".
   - n. 2 quotes Campbell: "Sappho sets out the physical concomitants of her love when jealousy inflames it."
   - n. 8, Devereux: "the symptoms of love Sappho describes are typical of a lesbian lover".
   - n. 28, Winkler: "The anonymous 'that man whosoever' … is a rhetorical cliché, not an actor in the imagined scene."
7. **Marcovich, M. "Sappho Fr. 31: Anxiety Attack or Love Declaration?" *CQ* 22 (1972): 19–32.** Walled; the abstract summarizes Devereux's position. Marcovich's own thesis, as Engelmayer (n. 10) and Tsagarakis report it: the man "is not the woman's lover" and is a contrast-figure, and the poem is a declaration of love. Declared, not read.
8. **Hays, Gregory. Review of Greene (ed.), *Reading Sappho* (1996).** BMCR 1999.05.01, https://bmcr.brynmawr.edu/1999/1999-05-01.html. Winkler's "Gardens of Nymphs" (1981) reads frr. 1 and 31 against Homeric parallels; Race's analysis of "that man" is named; Devereux 1970 is called "notorious".
