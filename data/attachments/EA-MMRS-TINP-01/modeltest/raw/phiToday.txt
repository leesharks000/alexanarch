Hypothetical Articles for a Forthcoming Issue of Philosophy Today
1. The Unfinished Subject: Becoming and the Limits of Self-Identity
This essay reconsiders the philosophical problem of personal identity through the notion of unfinished subjectivity. Drawing on the work of Søren Kierkegaard, Jean-Paul Sartre, and Judith Butler, it argues that the subject cannot be adequately understood as a stable entity possessing determinate characteristics. Rather, subjectivity emerges through an ongoing process of becoming in which possibilities, social relations, and temporal discontinuities continually reshape the self. The essay pays particular attention to the tension between self-determination and the conditions that precede individual agency. By distinguishing incompletion from mere instability, it develops an account of subjectivity that preserves the possibility of ethical responsibility without presupposing a fixed identity. The unfinished subject, it concludes, is not a deficiency to be overcome but a constitutive feature of human existence.

2. After the Human: Phenomenology and the Problem of More-than-Human Experience
Recent debates concerning posthumanism have challenged the anthropocentric assumptions underlying much of modern philosophy. Yet the displacement of the human subject raises a further question: how might phenomenology describe experience without reinstating human consciousness as its privileged center? This article approaches the problem through the writings of Maurice Merleau-Ponty and contemporary environmental phenomenology. It argues that embodiment provides a conceptual resource for understanding forms of relationality that exceed the boundaries of individual human existence. Rather than treating nonhuman beings as objects encountered by consciousness, the essay considers how organisms, environments, and material processes participate in the constitution of perceptual worlds. This expanded phenomenological perspective suggests that the human is neither an autonomous center nor an irrelevant category, but a situated participant in a shared field of becoming.

3. The Ethics of Ambiguity Revisited: Freedom, Vulnerability, and Responsibility
Simone de Beauvoir's account of ambiguity remains an important resource for contemporary ethical theory, particularly where questions of freedom and responsibility intersect with conditions of vulnerability. This essay revisits The Ethics of Ambiguity to examine the relationship between existential freedom and the unavoidable limitations of human action. It challenges interpretations that understand Beauvoirian freedom primarily in terms of individual self-assertion, arguing instead that freedom becomes ethically meaningful through its recognition of other freedoms. Particular attention is given to dependency, unequal social conditions, and the ways in which political institutions shape the possibilities of action. The essay ultimately proposes that ambiguity should be understood not simply as an existential predicament but as the ground of an ethics responsive to the fragility and interdependence of human existence.

4. The World Before Us: Heidegger, Dwelling, and Ecological Responsibility
Martin Heidegger's reflections on dwelling have received renewed attention amid contemporary ecological crises. Nevertheless, the political and ethical implications of his account remain contested. This article examines the relationship between dwelling, worldhood, and responsibility in Heidegger's later philosophy. It argues that dwelling should not be interpreted as a nostalgic return to a harmonious relationship with nature, but as a mode of inhabiting the world that resists its reduction to an inventory of resources. By bringing Heidegger into conversation with contemporary environmental philosophy, the essay identifies a tension between the ontological significance of dwelling and the practical demands of ecological politics. It concludes that a phenomenology of dwelling can contribute to environmental thought only when supplemented by an explicit account of historical responsibility, technological power, and unequal exposure to ecological destruction.

5. The Politics of Recognition and the Possibility of Misrecognition
Recognition has become a central concept in contemporary political philosophy, particularly in discussions of identity, social justice, and the constitution of collective belonging. Yet recognition is frequently treated as an unambiguously emancipatory process. This essay questions that assumption by examining the relationship between recognition and misrecognition in the work of Hegel, Axel Honneth, and contemporary critical theorists. It argues that the desire for recognition can reproduce the very normative structures through which subjects become socially intelligible. Recognition, in other words, may simultaneously enable political agency and constrain its expression. The essay develops the concept of critical recognition to describe practices that acknowledge persons without requiring their assimilation to established categories. Such an approach makes it possible to understand recognition not as the final achievement of social justice but as an ongoing and contestable political practice.

6. Waiting for the Other: Temporality and Ethical Responsibility in Levinas
Emmanuel Levinas's philosophy of responsibility is often discussed in terms of the asymmetrical encounter with the Other. Less frequently considered, however, is the temporal structure through which this responsibility emerges. This article argues that waiting, interruption, and delay are essential to understanding Levinas's account of ethical subjectivity. Through a close reading of Totality and Infinity and Otherwise than Being, it examines how responsibility unsettles the subject's ability to organize experience according to its own temporal expectations. The Other does not simply appear within an already constituted present but interrupts the subject's possession of time. By developing the significance of ethical waiting, the essay offers a temporal interpretation of Levinasian responsibility that challenges contemporary models of agency grounded in immediacy, calculation, and control.

7. Technology and the Disappearance of Distance: A Phenomenology of Digital Presence
Digital communication increasingly reorganizes the experience of proximity, absence, and interpersonal encounter. This article investigates the phenomenological implications of technologically mediated presence, drawing on the work of Husserl, Merleau-Ponty, and Don Ihde. It argues that digital technologies do not simply eliminate geographical distance but transform the structures through which distance becomes meaningful. The apparent immediacy of online communication can coexist with new forms of absence, temporal dislocation, and perceptual fragmentation. Against accounts that regard digital mediation as either the overcoming or the impoverishment of embodied experience, the essay proposes a more differentiated understanding of technological presence. It concludes that digital interaction should be understood as a distinctive mode of embodied relationality, one that both extends and reorganizes the conditions of interpersonal recognition and encounter.

8. Melancholy and Historical Consciousness: Benjamin's Philosophy of Loss
Walter Benjamin's reflections on history are marked by an enduring concern with loss, interruption, and unrealized possibility. This essay explores the philosophical significance of melancholy within his conception of historical consciousness. Rather than interpreting melancholy as a merely psychological response to historical catastrophe, it argues that melancholy can disclose the unfinished character of the past. Through an examination of Benjamin's writings on allegory, remembrance, and the philosophy of history, the essay considers how attention to historical loss might resist narratives of inevitable progress. It further suggests that remembrance possesses an ethical dimension insofar as it restores visibility to experiences excluded from dominant historical accounts. Benjamin's philosophy thus offers an alternative understanding of historical responsibility grounded not in the recovery of an intact past but in the critical recognition of its unresolved claims upon the present.

9. The Body as Archive: Memory, Trauma, and Phenomenological Time
The relationship between bodily experience and historical memory presents a significant challenge to philosophical accounts that locate recollection primarily in conscious representation. This essay examines the body as an archive of experience, drawing on phenomenology, psychoanalysis, and contemporary trauma theory. It argues that memory is not simply the retrieval of past events but a process through which earlier experiences continue to organize present possibilities of perception and action. The analysis engages the phenomenological accounts of Merleau-Ponty alongside the work of contemporary theorists of embodied memory. Particular attention is given to the temporal disturbances associated with trauma, where the past may persist without becoming fully available to reflective consciousness. The essay concludes by proposing that embodied memory complicates linear conceptions of temporality and requires a more relational understanding of historical experience.

10. The Limits of Hospitality: Derrida and the Politics of Welcoming
Hospitality occupies an unstable position in contemporary political and ethical discourse. It is frequently invoked as a moral ideal, yet its institutional realization depends upon distinctions between those who welcome and those who are welcomed. This essay examines Jacques Derrida's distinction between unconditional hospitality and the conditional laws governing actual practices of welcome. It argues that the tension between these two forms should not be resolved through the abandonment of either ethical aspiration or political limitation. Rather, their relationship reveals the constitutive instability of hospitality as a political concept. By placing Derrida in conversation with contemporary debates concerning migration, borders, and national belonging, the essay develops an account of hospitality as a practice of negotiating the limits of political community. Its central claim is that hospitality becomes philosophically significant precisely where the possibility of welcoming exceeds the authority to determine who belongs.

11. Nihilism After Metaphysics: Nietzsche and the Question of Affirmation
The philosophical significance of Nietzsche's critique of nihilism remains unsettled, particularly with respect to the possibility of affirmation after the collapse of transcendent values. This article revisits Nietzsche's diagnosis of European nihilism through the distinction between the destruction of inherited meaning and the creation of new values. It argues that affirmation should not be understood as an uncomplicated celebration of existence or as a replacement metaphysics. Rather, affirmation involves a transformation in the subject's relationship to contingency, suffering, and becoming. The essay examines the significance of eternal recurrence as a test of existential commitment, while questioning interpretations that reduce it to a doctrine of personal empowerment. Nietzsche's response to nihilism, it concludes, requires neither the recovery of metaphysical certainty nor the resignation to meaninglessness, but an experimental practice of value creation.

12. The Everyday and the Extraordinary: Rethinking Authenticity in Contemporary Life
Authenticity has become a familiar term in contemporary discussions of personal identity, consumer culture, and social media. Yet its philosophical meaning remains deeply contested. This article returns to the existential analyses of Heidegger and Sartre to reconsider the relationship between authenticity and everyday existence. It challenges the assumption that authenticity requires escape from ordinary social practices or the discovery of an allegedly original self concealed beneath cultural expectations. Instead, it argues that authenticity emerges through a critical transformation of one's relationship to inherited possibilities. The essay further considers how contemporary forms of self-presentation complicate the distinction between genuine expression and social performance. By situating authenticity within the ordinary practices through which identities are negotiated, it proposes an account of existential responsibility that avoids both individualistic self-invention and passive conformity.

13. The Violence of Clarity: Language, Power, and Philosophical Explanation
Philosophy has traditionally regarded conceptual clarity as an essential condition of rigorous thought. However, the demand for clarity may itself conceal assumptions about which experiences can be rendered intelligible and which forms of expression are excluded. This essay examines the relationship between philosophical explanation and epistemic power through the writings of Theodor Adorno, Michel Foucault, and Jacques Derrida. It argues that conceptual precision is not necessarily opposed to ambiguity, opacity, or contradiction. Indeed, certain experiences become distorted when subjected to explanatory frameworks that demand immediate conceptual resolution. The essay develops a distinction between productive clarification and reductive intelligibility, suggesting that philosophical rigor sometimes requires preserving what resists assimilation. Its central claim is that critical thought must remain attentive not only to what concepts reveal but also to what their explanatory authority makes difficult to articulate.

14. Solidarity Without Identity: Rethinking Collective Political Agency
Political solidarity is frequently understood through shared identities, common interests, or collective historical experiences. Yet these foundations can become exclusionary when they demand a degree of sameness among political participants. This essay explores the possibility of solidarity without identity through a dialogue between Hannah Arendt, Judith Butler, and contemporary theories of political pluralism. It argues that collective agency need not depend upon the elimination of difference or the establishment of a unified political subject. Instead, solidarity can emerge through practices of mutual commitment among individuals and groups whose experiences and interests remain irreducibly distinct. The essay examines the implications of this account for democratic participation, political movements, and struggles against structural injustice. It concludes that solidarity is best understood not as the expression of an existing collective identity but as a fragile achievement sustained through the negotiation of difference.

15. The Meaning of Finitude: Mortality and the Possibility of Ethical Life
Finitude occupies a central position in existential philosophy, where mortality is frequently understood as a condition through which human existence acquires its distinctive significance. This essay examines whether an emphasis on individual mortality can adequately account for the ethical dimensions of finitude. Drawing on Heidegger, Beauvoir, and contemporary phenomenological ethics, it argues that finitude should be understood not only in relation to one's own death but also through exposure to the vulnerability and mortality of others. This shift complicates the tendency to interpret being-toward-death as an exclusively individual existential achievement. The essay develops an account of shared finitude in which mortality becomes a condition of responsibility, interdependence, and historical situatedness. It concludes that an adequate philosophy of finitude must move beyond existential self-understanding toward an ethics attentive to the lives and losses of others.

16. Attention as a Philosophical Practice: Perception, Ethics, and the Ordinary
Attention is commonly treated as a cognitive capacity, yet its philosophical significance extends beyond the organization of perception. This essay investigates attention as a practice through which the world becomes ethically and existentially significant. Drawing on Simone Weil, Iris Murdoch, and phenomenological accounts of perception, it argues that attention involves a transformation in the subject's relationship to what appears. Rather than imposing predetermined meanings upon experience, attentive perception requires a willingness to encounter objects, persons, and situations beyond the limits of habitual expectation. The essay distinguishes attention from mere concentration, emphasizing its receptive and ethical dimensions. It also considers the contemporary conditions under which distraction and instrumental forms of perception threaten sustained engagement with the ordinary world. Attention, the essay concludes, offers a neglected model of philosophical and ethical practice grounded in receptivity rather than mastery.

17. The Affective Foundations of Critique: Anger, Grief, and Political Judgment
Critical theory has frequently emphasized the importance of rational judgment in identifying and contesting relations of domination. Yet political critique is also shaped by affective experiences that disclose the significance of injustice. This article examines the philosophical role of anger and grief in contemporary critical practice. Drawing on Adorno, Sara Ahmed, and Judith Butler, it argues that affective responses are neither merely subjective disturbances nor self-sufficient grounds for political judgment. Rather, they can function as modes of orientation through which structural conditions become perceptible and ethically significant. The essay considers how anger can reveal violations of social norms while also reproducing exclusionary forms of political identification. It similarly examines grief as a response to unequal distributions of vulnerability and public recognition. The article concludes by proposing an affective conception of critique that integrates emotional responsiveness with reflective and political judgment.

18. Can the World Be Shared? Arendt, Plurality, and the Fragility of Public Space
Hannah Arendt's account of plurality offers a distinctive approach to the philosophical problem of shared political existence. This essay revisits her conception of the common world in light of contemporary transformations in public space and political communication. It argues that the world is not shared simply because individuals occupy the same geographical or institutional environment. Rather, worldliness emerges through practices of appearance, speech, and action in which different perspectives become mutually accessible without being reduced to a single standpoint. The essay examines the fragility of this condition, particularly where political polarization and the erosion of common institutions undermine the possibility of meaningful public encounter. It further considers whether digital environments can sustain forms of plurality comparable to those described by Arendt. Ultimately, the essay presents the shared world as an ongoing political achievement rather than a preexisting condition of collective life.

19. Beyond Instrumental Reason: Education and the Formation of Judgment
The contemporary emphasis on measurable educational outcomes has intensified longstanding philosophical questions concerning the purposes of education. This essay examines the relationship between education, judgment, and instrumental rationality through the work of Kant, Arendt, and the Frankfurt School. It argues that education cannot be adequately understood as the transmission of information or the acquisition of competencies directed toward predetermined ends. Its more fundamental task involves cultivating the capacity to judge situations that cannot be resolved through the mechanical application of established rules. The essay distinguishes judgment from both technical expertise and unrestricted personal opinion, emphasizing its dependence upon reflection, imagination, and engagement with plural perspectives. It concludes that a philosophical understanding of education must preserve the possibility of intellectual transformation, even where such transformation resists standardized assessment and immediate practical utility.

20. Philosophy After Certainty: Hermeneutics, Dialogue, and the Future of Understanding
Contemporary philosophical debates increasingly question whether understanding requires stable foundations, universal methods, or the possibility of final interpretation. This essay examines the implications of these questions through the hermeneutical philosophies of Hans-Georg Gadamer and Paul Ricoeur. It argues that the absence of absolute interpretive certainty need not lead to relativism or skepticism. Rather, understanding emerges through historically situated encounters in which prior assumptions are exposed to revision. The essay pays particular attention to dialogue as a philosophical practice, emphasizing its capacity to transform the perspectives of those who participate in it. It also considers the difficulties of dialogue under conditions of ideological polarization and unequal interpretive authority. The article concludes that philosophy's future depends less upon securing uncontestable foundations than upon cultivating forms of understanding capable of remaining open to difference, disagreement, and self-criticism.
