The following twenty abstracts are conceived as a plausible table of contents for a forthcoming issue of Apeiron, reflecting the journal's interdisciplinary focus on ancient Greek and Roman philosophy, science, mathematics, metaphysics, logic, and intellectual history. The topics, arguments, and titles are original scholarly proposals rather than descriptions of existing publications.

# Proposed Articles for a Forthcoming Issue of Apeiron

### 1\. Aristotle on the Unity and Priority of Substance

**Abstract**

Aristotle's claims concerning the priority of substance in _Metaphysics_ Ζ have generated persistent disagreement about the precise grounds on which substance is accorded explanatory and ontological primacy. This article argues that these claims are best understood through a distinction between definitional priority and causal priority. Although Aristotle sometimes appears to privilege form because it explains what a substance is, his account also assigns form a distinctive role in explaining the persistence and intelligibility of composite substances. By examining the relationship between _Metaphysics_ Ζ and Η, together with relevant passages in the _Physics_, the article reconstructs a unified account of substantial priority. It concludes that Aristotle's conception of substance cannot be reduced either to an exclusively material or to an exclusively formal principle.

### 2\. The Problem of False Belief in Plato's Theaetetus

**Abstract**

The problem of false belief occupies a central position in Plato's _Theaetetus_, particularly in the discussion of the wax tablet and aviary models. This article reconsiders the relationship between these two models and argues that they address distinct dimensions of the epistemological problem. Whereas the wax tablet model attempts to explain the persistence of perceptual and cognitive impressions, the aviary model concerns the acquisition and subsequent retrieval of knowledge. Their respective limitations therefore should not be treated as repetitions of a single failure. The article further examines the implications of Socrates' criticism for the distinction between possessing knowledge and exercising it. It suggests that the dialogue's inconclusiveness reflects a deeper difficulty concerning the relationship between cognitive content and the activities through which that content is recognized.

### 3\. Demonstration and Explanation in Euclid's Elements

**Abstract**

Euclid's _Elements_ presents mathematical knowledge through an ordered sequence of definitions, postulates, common notions, and propositions. Yet the epistemological significance of this organization remains contested. This article examines the relationship between demonstrative validity and explanatory force in Euclidean geometry. It argues that Euclid's proofs do more than establish the truth of individual propositions: their arrangement also reveals systematic dependencies among mathematical objects and principles. Particular attention is given to the distinction between constructions and theorems, and to the role of diagrams in making geometrical relations intelligible. The analysis suggests that Euclidean demonstration should not be understood simply as a formal procedure for deriving conclusions. Rather, it constitutes a distinctive practice of mathematical explanation in which logical necessity and constructive insight operate together.

### 4\. Heraclitus on Measure, Change, and Cosmic Order

**Abstract**

Heraclitus' conception of cosmic order is frequently interpreted through the apparent opposition between universal stability and perpetual change. This article argues that such an opposition rests on an insufficient appreciation of the role played by measure in the surviving fragments. By examining the language of proportion, exchange, and alternation, it reconstructs an account in which transformation is not opposed to order but is constitutive of it. Particular attention is given to the fragments concerning the exchange of fire, the common logos, and the limits of the sun. The article proposes that Heraclitus understands cosmic regularity as a structured process rather than as the persistence of unchanging entities. This interpretation offers a more integrated account of the relationship between natural processes and intelligible order in early Greek thought.

### 5\. Epicurus on the Ontology of Empty Space

**Abstract**

Epicurus' account of the void is usually discussed in connection with the possibility of motion and the existence of indivisible bodies. This article examines the further ontological question of how empty space can be said to exist without possessing the characteristics of body. Drawing on the surviving fragments of Epicurus and the testimony of Lucretius and Diogenes Laertius, it argues that the void occupies a distinctive category within Epicurean ontology. It is neither a material substance nor merely a conceptual abstraction, but an independently existing condition of corporeal movement and separation. The article also considers whether the void should be understood as spatial extension or as the absence of resistance. It concludes that Epicurean atomism requires a more sophisticated account of spatial existence than is sometimes acknowledged.

### 6\. Plato's Timaeus and the Mathematical Structure of Nature

**Abstract**

The mathematical organization of the cosmos in Plato's _Timaeus_ has often been interpreted as evidence of an underlying Pythagorean influence. This article investigates the philosophical function of mathematical proportion in the dialogue, focusing particularly on the construction of the world soul and the geometrical constitution of the four elements. It argues that mathematics serves two related but distinguishable purposes: it explains the structural organization of physical reality and establishes the intelligibility of the cosmos as a whole. The article further examines the relationship between mathematical intermediaries and sensible particulars, questioning whether Plato's account requires the independent existence of mathematical objects. It concludes that the mathematical framework of the _Timaeus_ is best understood as an account of how intelligible order becomes manifest within the physical world.

### 7\. Aristotle's Account of Chance in Physics II

**Abstract**

Aristotle's discussion of chance and spontaneity in _Physics_ II raises important questions about the relationship between accidental causation and teleological explanation. This article argues that chance is not introduced as an alternative to natural causation but as a qualification of the ways in which natural processes can be explained. By examining Aristotle's examples of unexpected encounters and purposive activities that produce unintended outcomes, the article identifies a distinction between causal regularity and the intentions of particular agents. It further considers the implications of this distinction for Aristotle's rejection of chance as a fundamental explanatory principle. The resulting interpretation suggests that chance is intelligible only against a background of regular causal structures, even though its particular occurrences cannot themselves be predicted or explained teleologically.

### 8\. The Stoic Theory of Lekta and the Ontology of Meaning

**Abstract**

The Stoic distinction between bodies, incorporeals, and sayables (_lekta_) presents a distinctive challenge to conventional accounts of ancient ontology. This article examines the status of complete and incomplete sayables and their role in Stoic theories of language and logic. It argues that the incorporeal character of _lekta_ does not render them philosophically marginal. On the contrary, they are indispensable to the explanation of how propositions acquire truth values and how linguistic expressions relate to states of affairs. The article also considers the relationship between the meaning of an utterance and the physical production of speech. By distinguishing linguistic expression from propositional content, it offers an account of the explanatory advantages of the Stoic framework and its significance for subsequent philosophical discussions of meaning and reference.

### 9\. Sextus Empiricus and the Limits of Rational Justification

**Abstract**

Sextus Empiricus' presentation of Pyrrhonian skepticism is commonly understood as an argument against the possibility of establishing knowledge through rational demonstration. This article reconsiders the structure of that argument by focusing on the skeptical treatment of criteria and standards of justification. It argues that Sextus' objections are directed not simply against particular epistemological principles but against the attempt to establish any criterion without presupposing what it is intended to prove. The resulting regress, circularity, or unsupported assumption provides the framework for the suspension of judgment. The article further explores the relationship between this strategy and the skeptic's acceptance of appearances and ordinary practices. It concludes that Pyrrhonian skepticism offers a distinctive account of rational restraint rather than a straightforward denial of the possibility of knowledge.

### 10\. Aristotle on the Perception of Time

**Abstract**

Aristotle's account of time in _Physics_ IV depends upon a complex relationship between motion, succession, and the activity of the soul. This article examines the claim that time is the number of motion with respect to before and after, paying particular attention to the role of perception in the identification of temporal succession. It argues that Aristotle does not reduce time to a subjective construction, nor does he treat temporal measurement as wholly independent of cognitive activity. Instead, the soul's capacity to distinguish successive phases of motion explains how temporal intervals become numerically identifiable. The article considers the implications of this interpretation for Aristotle's account of the existence of time in the absence of a perceiver. It concludes that his position combines an objective account of temporal order with an account of its measurement.

### 11\. The Parmenidean Challenge and the Origins of Ancient Atomism

**Abstract**

The development of ancient atomism is often presented as a response to the Eleatic denial of plurality and motion. This article reexamines the precise nature of the philosophical problem that Parmenides bequeathed to Leucippus and Democritus. Rather than treating atomism as a simple reversal of Eleatic monism, it argues that atomists preserve an important aspect of Eleatic reasoning by assigning strict ontological permanence to indivisible bodies. Change and plurality are subsequently explained through the rearrangement and separation of these bodies within the void. The article explores the extent to which this strategy constitutes a genuine solution to the Eleatic challenge. It concludes that early atomism is best understood as an attempt to retain the demands of rational ontology while accommodating the evident plurality and motion of the sensible world.

### 12\. The Concept of Potentiality in Aristotle's Metaphysics

**Abstract**

Aristotle's distinction between potentiality and actuality is fundamental to his accounts of change, substance, and causation. Nevertheless, the relationship between different senses of potentiality remains difficult to establish. This article examines the treatment of _dynamis_ in _Metaphysics_ Θ, distinguishing capacities for change from the potentialities associated with matter and form. It argues that these senses are connected through a general explanatory structure but cannot be reduced to a single definition. Particular attention is given to Aristotle's distinction between possessing a capacity and exercising it, as well as to his account of the priority of actuality. The article suggests that potentiality is not merely an incomplete form of actuality but an indispensable principle for explaining the organization and development of natural substances.

### 13\. Mathematical Astronomy and Physical Reality in Ptolemy's Almagest

**Abstract**

Ptolemy's _Almagest_ provides a mathematical account of celestial motion whose relationship to physical cosmology remains a subject of considerable debate. This article investigates the epistemological status of Ptolemy's geometrical models and asks whether their predictive success commits him to a particular conception of astronomical reality. By examining the treatment of eccentric circles, epicycles, and the equant, it distinguishes mathematical representation from physical explanation. It argues that Ptolemy's methodology permits a productive separation between the geometrical determination of celestial phenomena and the investigation of their underlying causes. The article also considers the extent to which this separation is compatible with Aristotelian natural philosophy. It concludes that Ptolemy's astronomy should be understood as a distinct explanatory discipline whose mathematical achievements do not depend upon a complete physical theory of the heavens.

### 14\. Socratic Ignorance and the Possibility of Ethical Knowledge

**Abstract**

Socrates' repeated professions of ignorance have traditionally been interpreted either as expressions of intellectual humility or as components of a distinctive method of philosophical inquiry. This article argues that these interpretations are not mutually exclusive but depend upon a more fundamental distinction between possessing knowledge and recognizing the conditions under which knowledge is justified. Through an examination of the _Apology_, _Charmides_, and _Laches_, it investigates the relationship between Socratic questioning and the possibility of ethical expertise. Particular attention is given to cases in which interlocutors claim knowledge of virtues but cannot provide coherent accounts of their nature. The article concludes that Socratic ignorance functions as a critical epistemological position: it establishes the standards by which ethical claims must be assessed without assuming that ethical knowledge is impossible.

### 15\. The Ancient Paradoxes of Motion and the Structure of Continuity

**Abstract**

Zeno's paradoxes of motion continue to raise fundamental questions concerning the mathematical and metaphysical structure of continuity. This article reconsiders the paradoxes of dichotomy and Achilles in light of ancient debates about infinite divisibility. It argues that their philosophical significance lies not simply in the apparent impossibility of completing infinitely many tasks, but in the difficulty of reconciling discrete descriptions of movement with continuous spatial and temporal magnitudes. The article examines Aristotle's responses in _Physics_ VI and distinguishes his treatment of potential infinity from later mathematical interpretations. It also considers whether the paradoxes depend upon an ambiguity between the divisibility of a magnitude and the succession of actual events. The analysis suggests that ancient discussions of motion reveal a persistent distinction between mathematical representation and the metaphysical conditions of physical change.

### 16\. Plutarch on the Relationship between Mind and Nature

**Abstract**

Plutarch's philosophical writings frequently engage with the relationship between rational order and the disorderly processes of the natural world. This article examines his account of the world soul in _On the Generation of the Soul in the Timaeus_, focusing on the relationship between rational principles and the pre-cosmic condition of disorder. It argues that Plutarch's interpretation of Plato cannot be reduced to a straightforward reproduction of the _Timaeus_. Rather, it reflects an attempt to reconcile the priority of divine rationality with the apparent independence of irrational processes. The article considers the metaphysical implications of this position and its relationship to Middle Platonic debates concerning matter, evil, and providence. It concludes that Plutarch's cosmology offers a distinctive account of the conditions under which rational order can govern a world characterized by material instability.

### 17\. The Meaning of Physis in the Presocratic Tradition

**Abstract**

The concept of _physis_ occupies a central position in early Greek philosophical inquiry, yet its precise significance varies across the surviving Presocratic evidence. This article examines the uses of the term in the fragments and testimonia associated with Heraclitus, Empedocles, Anaxagoras, and Democritus. It argues that the familiar translation of _physis_ as nature can obscure its connections with emergence, constitution, and the characteristic development of things. Rather than presupposing a unified Presocratic conception of nature, the article identifies several related but distinct explanatory uses. Particular attention is given to the relationship between the origin of living beings and the underlying constituents of the cosmos. It concludes that the semantic development of _physis_ reflects a gradual transformation in the aims and methods of early Greek natural philosophy.

### 18\. Aristotle's Categories and the Problem of Individuality

**Abstract**

Aristotle's treatment of primary substance in the _Categories_ has often been regarded as difficult to reconcile with the more developed account of substance in the _Metaphysics_. This article investigates whether the two works employ fundamentally different conceptions of individuality. It argues that the _Categories_ emphasizes the independence and numerical unity of individual substances, whereas the _Metaphysics_ investigates the principles responsible for their unity and intelligibility. The apparent discrepancy can therefore be understood as a difference in explanatory perspective rather than a direct contradiction. By examining the treatment of predication, substantial change, and the relationship between particulars and universals, the article develops an account of the continuity between the two works. It concludes that Aristotle's conception of individual substance provides the common foundation for both his logical and metaphysical investigations.

### 19\. The Role of Analogy in Ancient Greek Natural Philosophy

**Abstract**

Analogy plays an important but frequently underestimated role in the explanation of natural phenomena in ancient Greek philosophy. This article examines the use of analogical reasoning in selected passages from Plato, Aristotle, and the early medical writers. It argues that analogy functions not merely as a rhetorical device but as a method for extending explanatory principles from familiar domains to less accessible objects of inquiry. Particular attention is given to comparisons between the human body and the cosmos, the operation of political communities and natural systems, and the relationship between artificial and natural processes. The article distinguishes analogies that provide heuristic guidance from those intended to establish genuine structural similarities. It concludes that analogical reasoning constitutes an important component of ancient explanatory practice, while also exposing the limits of inference across different domains of reality.

### 20\. Providence and Necessity in the Hellenistic Philosophical Traditions

**Abstract**

The relationship between providence and necessity constitutes one of the principal points of disagreement among Hellenistic philosophical schools. This article compares Stoic and Epicurean accounts of cosmic order, focusing on their respective treatments of causation, divine activity, and human agency. It argues that the familiar opposition between Stoic determinism and Epicurean contingency requires qualification. Although the Stoics understand the cosmos as an interconnected causal whole governed by rational providence, the Epicureans also recognize stable patterns of natural necessity. Their disagreement concerns principally the explanatory and theological status of these patterns. The article further examines the role of human responsibility in both traditions and the implications of their accounts for practical philosophy. It concludes that the debate reveals competing conceptions of the relationship between natural order, freedom, and the intelligibility of human action.

Editorial note: The proposed abstracts are deliberately varied in subject matter and methodology, combining textual interpretation, historical reconstruction, conceptual analysis, and the history of ancient science. Together, they would make a credible multidisciplinary issue of Apeiron.
