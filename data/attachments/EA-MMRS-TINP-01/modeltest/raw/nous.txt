Twenty Hypothetical Articles for Noûs
1. The Metaphysics of Mere Possibility
Abstract. What is it for something to be possible? Contemporary modal metaphysics typically understands possibility in terms of possible worlds, conceiving of these as either abstract representations or concrete alternatives to actuality. This paper develops a third approach, according to which possibilities are primitive features of the modal structure of reality. I argue that this account avoids familiar difficulties concerning the ontological status of possible worlds while preserving the explanatory advantages of possible-world semantics. In particular, it provides a unified treatment of metaphysical possibility, counterfactual dependence, and the distinction between necessary and contingent existence. I further argue that the resulting view has resources for distinguishing genuine possibilities from merely describable scenarios. The account thus offers a novel understanding of modality that neither reduces possibility to linguistic representation nor requires an extravagant ontology of nonactual worlds.

2. Knowing Without Being Able to Say
Abstract. Is it possible to know something without being able to articulate what one knows? Although this possibility is familiar in discussions of practical knowledge and perceptual expertise, its epistemological significance remains insufficiently understood. I argue that propositional knowledge need not always be accompanied by an ability to formulate or communicate the proposition known. The central obstacle is an overly demanding conception of epistemic access, according to which knowing that p requires the capacity to recognize one's knowledge of p under an appropriate description. I distinguish possession of knowledge from reflective access to that possession and show how the distinction applies to cases involving skilled action, perceptual discrimination, and linguistic limitations. The resulting account preserves the normativity of knowledge while rejecting intellectualism about epistemic self-awareness. It also suggests that familiar accounts of epistemic justification underestimate the variety of ways in which subjects can rationally respond to their environments.

3. The Problem of Temporary Identity
Abstract. Ordinary objects persist through changes in their properties, but the metaphysical conditions governing such persistence remain controversial. In this paper, I examine cases in which an object appears to undergo a change so substantial that its identity becomes difficult to determine. I argue that these cases reveal a limitation in standard accounts of persistence, particularly those that identify an object's temporal parts or essential properties as decisive. My alternative is a historically sensitive account of identity, according to which the persistence of an object depends partly on the continuity of its causal history. This proposal does not imply that every causal continuation constitutes survival, nor does it eliminate the possibility of indeterminate identity. Rather, it provides a framework for explaining why certain transformations preserve an object while others produce a numerically distinct entity. I conclude by considering the implications of this account for artifacts, organisms, and ordinary material objects.

4. Rationality and the Limits of Reflection
Abstract. Philosophical accounts of rationality frequently assume that rational agents should be able to reflect on, and appropriately revise, their own commitments. Yet reflection is itself a cognitive activity governed by limited information, imperfect memory, and potentially unreliable evaluative standards. This paper investigates whether rationality can require agents to assess the reliability of their own reflective procedures without generating an infinite regress. I argue that the apparent regress depends on conflating two distinct demands: the demand that an agent's beliefs be appropriately supported and the demand that the agent possess an independently justified belief about the reliability of every process of support. A more modest conception of epistemic responsibility avoids this conflation. On the resulting account, rationality requires sensitivity to accessible defeaters but does not require comprehensive reflective endorsement. This distinction has consequences for internalist epistemology, theories of epistemic responsibility, and the relationship between rational agency and intellectual self-knowledge.

5. What Makes a Reason a Reason?
Abstract. Reasons are commonly understood as considerations that count in favor of actions, beliefs, or attitudes. But this familiar formulation leaves open what distinguishes a genuine reason from a consideration that merely influences an agent's behavior. I argue that reasons possess an essentially normative structure that cannot be explained exclusively through their causal or motivational roles. Nevertheless, their normative force does not require that agents recognize them as reasons. To establish this claim, I distinguish between the existence of a reason, its accessibility to an agent, and its capacity to motivate. These distinctions illuminate cases in which agents act for bad reasons despite the existence of decisive considerations against their actions, as well as cases in which agents respond appropriately to reasons they cannot explicitly formulate. The account offers a unified treatment of normative reasons across practical and theoretical domains and challenges the assumption that reasons must be understood through a common psychological relation to rational agency.

6. The Semantics of Unspoken Commitments
Abstract. Speakers routinely communicate commitments that are neither explicitly asserted nor straightforwardly implicated by what they say. These commitments can persist across conversational contexts and may constrain the interpretations available to subsequent participants. Existing pragmatic theories have difficulty explaining their stability, particularly when speakers deny having made the relevant commitment. I develop an account according to which certain conversational contributions introduce defeasible normative commitments into a shared discursive structure. Unlike conventional presuppositions, these commitments need not be lexically triggered; unlike conversational implicatures, they need not depend on assumptions about communicative cooperation. I show how the proposal explains the persistence, cancellation, and attribution of unspoken commitments across a range of ordinary exchanges. I also consider its implications for disagreement and conversational responsibility. The resulting framework suggests that the distinction between semantics and pragmatics should be supplemented by an account of the normative structures generated through linguistic interaction.

7. The Asymmetry of Regret
Abstract. Regret appears to have an asymmetrical temporal structure. We regret decisions made in the past, but we do not ordinarily regret future decisions, even when we anticipate that those decisions will be mistaken. This paper argues that the asymmetry is philosophically significant because regret is not simply a negative evaluation of an outcome. Rather, it involves a distinctive relation to one's practical history. I distinguish retrospective regret from prospective concern and argue that only the former involves an appropriately situated sense of authorship. This distinction helps explain why regret can remain rational even when an agent would make the same decision again under identical circumstances. It also illuminates the relationship between regret and responsibility, particularly in cases where agents act under conditions of uncertainty. I conclude that regret is best understood as a temporally structured practical attitude rather than as a species of dissatisfaction or counterfactual preference.

8. Vagueness and the Metaphysics of Boundaries
Abstract. Many ordinary objects have boundaries that resist precise determination. Mountains, clouds, biological organisms, and geographical regions all provide familiar examples. While semantic theories of vagueness explain how language can tolerate borderline cases, they leave open whether the underlying objects themselves possess indeterminate boundaries. I defend a moderate metaphysical account according to which some objects have genuinely indeterminate spatial extent. This position does not entail that every vague predicate corresponds to an indeterminate feature of reality. Rather, I argue that certain patterns of vagueness are best explained by ontological indeterminacy, particularly where competing precisifications cannot be privileged by either linguistic convention or underlying physical structure. I develop a criterion for distinguishing semantic from metaphysical vagueness and consider its implications for theories of composition. The resulting account challenges the assumption that a complete description of reality must assign every material object a perfectly determinate boundary.

9. Perception and the Authority of Experience
Abstract. Perceptual experiences seem to provide subjects with immediate reasons for believing propositions about their surroundings. Yet the epistemic authority of experience is difficult to reconcile with the possibility of perceptual error. This paper develops an account of perceptual justification that distinguishes between the rational significance of an experience and its evidential reliability. I argue that an experience can provide prima facie justification even when the subject lacks independent grounds for believing that the experience is reliable. This does not imply that perceptual justification is immune to defeat. Rather, its authority depends on a defeasible entitlement to treat experience as a source of information about the environment. I defend this position against the objection that it makes perceptual justification epistemically arbitrary. The resulting view explains both the rationality of ordinary perceptual belief and the possibility of rational suspension in unusual circumstances. It also offers a distinctive response to traditional skeptical arguments.

10. The Normativity of Attention
Abstract. Attention is usually treated as a psychological mechanism that determines which features of an environment become cognitively prominent. But attention also appears to be subject to normative assessment: we can attend to the wrong things, overlook what matters, or distribute our attention irresponsibly. This paper investigates the source of this normativity. I argue that attentional norms cannot be reduced either to epistemic norms governing belief or to practical norms governing action. Instead, attention has a distinctive normative dimension grounded in its role in structuring an agent's engagement with the world. I distinguish attentional appropriateness from attentional efficiency and show why an agent can attend efficiently while attending inappropriately. The distinction illuminates familiar cases involving distraction, curiosity, moral attention, and selective perception. I conclude by suggesting that theories of rational agency should recognize attention as an independently assessable dimension of intellectual and practical life.

11. Could There Be a World Without Laws?
Abstract. Physical laws are commonly regarded as fundamental features of the natural world, but it remains unclear whether their existence is metaphysically necessary. This paper examines the possibility of a world containing regularities without laws, and a world containing laws without corresponding regularities. I argue that both possibilities are coherent, provided that we distinguish governing conceptions of laws from descriptive conceptions. The distinction reveals that several familiar arguments for the necessity of laws depend on an unnoticed transition between metaphysical and explanatory claims. I develop a dispositional account of lawhood that preserves the explanatory significance of laws while allowing for variation in the relationship between laws and actual events. The account also clarifies the difference between a genuine law and an accidental regularity. I conclude by considering whether the resulting position supports a realist understanding of laws without requiring that laws govern every possible world.

12. The Epistemology of Other Minds Revisited
Abstract. Our knowledge of other minds is often contrasted with our knowledge of our own mental states. Whereas first-person knowledge is supposedly immediate, knowledge of others appears to depend on behavioral evidence and analogical inference. I argue that this contrast exaggerates the epistemic difference between first-person and third-person access. Both forms of knowledge depend on interpretative capacities that are ordinarily exercised without explicit inference. The crucial distinction concerns the kind of evidence available to the subject, rather than the presence or absence of inferential mediation. I develop this account by examining cases of self-deception, interpersonal misunderstanding, and the attribution of unfamiliar mental states. I show that the resulting framework avoids both the skepticism associated with traditional arguments from analogy and the implausible suggestion that mental states are transparent to their possessors. The paper concludes with a reconsideration of the relationship between self-knowledge and social understanding.

13. Moral Luck and the Ownership of Outcomes
Abstract. Moral luck arises when agents are morally assessed for outcomes that depend partly on factors beyond their control. Existing accounts often attempt to resolve the problem by distinguishing responsibility for an action from responsibility for its consequences. I argue that this distinction is insufficient because consequences can become part of the moral significance of an action without being fully attributable to the agent. I propose instead that responsibility admits of several dimensions, including responsibility for undertaking a risk, responsibility for bringing about an outcome, and responsibility for responding to its consequences. These dimensions can come apart in cases of resultant luck. The proposal explains why two agents who act equally recklessly may nevertheless have different moral obligations after their actions produce different results. It also preserves an important intuition underlying the control principle without denying the moral significance of actual consequences. The account offers a revised framework for understanding responsibility under conditions of uncertainty.

14. The Metaphysics of Absence
Abstract. Absences appear to play an important role in ordinary explanation. We explain a failed harvest by the absence of rain, a missed opportunity by the absence of timely action, and a physical accident by the absence of an appropriate safeguard. Yet it is unclear what, if anything, absences are. This paper examines three approaches: treating absences as negative facts, as omissions in an otherwise positive causal structure, and as merely convenient explanatory descriptions. I defend a qualified version of the second approach. According to this account, an absence is causally relevant when a particular positive condition would have interrupted an actual causal process, even though the absent condition itself does not constitute an additional entity. This approach explains the explanatory importance of absences without multiplying the ontology of negative facts. I also consider objections concerning counterfactual dependence and causal overdetermination. The resulting theory provides a unified treatment of absences in metaphysics, causation, and ordinary explanatory practice.

15. Is Understanding a Kind of Knowledge?
Abstract. Philosophers have increasingly questioned whether understanding can be adequately characterized in terms of propositional knowledge. Although understanding appears to involve knowing how facts relate to one another, it also seems to require a form of cognitive integration absent from many ordinary instances of knowing. I argue that understanding is a distinctive epistemic achievement constituted by the ability to grasp explanatory connections among propositions. This account avoids treating understanding as either a superior degree of knowledge or an entirely separate epistemic category. I distinguish explanatory integration from the mere possession of numerous relevant beliefs and show how this distinction applies to scientific, historical, and everyday understanding. I further argue that understanding can survive certain forms of ignorance and may occasionally be present despite false auxiliary beliefs. These results suggest that understanding has a distinctive normative profile. The paper concludes by considering whether its proposed account can illuminate disagreements about scientific explanation.

16. The Limits of Conceptual Analysis
Abstract. Conceptual analysis has traditionally been regarded as a central method of philosophical inquiry. However, the prospects of discovering necessary and sufficient conditions for philosophically significant concepts have become increasingly controversial. This paper defends a more limited conception of conceptual analysis, according to which its principal function is not to identify exceptionless definitions but to reveal the inferential and explanatory commitments associated with our concepts. I argue that this conception accommodates both the apparent instability of ordinary intuitions and the possibility of genuine philosophical progress. In particular, it explains why counterexamples can undermine an analysis without showing that conceptual inquiry is methodologically defective. I distinguish between the analysis of a concept's application conditions and the investigation of the theoretical role that concept plays. The distinction provides a framework for assessing contemporary debates about knowledge, causation, freedom, and moral responsibility. It also suggests that the limitations of traditional analysis should motivate methodological revision rather than wholesale abandonment.

17. The Rationality of Changing One's Mind
Abstract. Changing one's mind is often regarded as a paradigmatic expression of rationality. Yet there are circumstances in which revising a belief seems less rational than maintaining it, even after acquiring apparently relevant new evidence. This paper investigates the relationship between rational belief revision and diachronic consistency. I argue that rationality requires neither unconditional responsiveness to new evidence nor the preservation of previously held beliefs. Instead, it requires agents to assess new information in light of the epistemic commitments that originally supported their beliefs. This approach explains why some revisions are rational despite producing inconsistencies with earlier judgments, while others constitute an epistemic failure even when they happen to yield true beliefs. I develop the account through cases involving testimony, scientific disagreement, and personal conviction. The resulting framework offers a more nuanced understanding of intellectual humility and challenges the assumption that rationality can be captured by a simple principle of belief updating.

18. Fictional Truth and the Problem of Incomplete Worlds
Abstract. Fictional narratives routinely leave questions unanswered. A novel may establish that a character has a brother without specifying whether that brother is older or younger, or describe a city without determining its precise geographical location. Such cases raise a problem for theories that treat fictional truth as truth at a possible world. If fictional worlds are complete, they appear to determine facts that the narrative leaves unsettled. If they are incomplete, it becomes unclear how their truth conditions should be specified. I defend an account according to which fictional truth is fundamentally constrained by narrative content rather than by the completeness of an associated world. On this view, a proposition can be neither true nor false according to a fiction without being indeterminate in any metaphysical sense. I show how the account handles inconsistent narratives, unreliable narrators, and interpretative disagreement. It also provides a new perspective on the relationship between imagination and modal reasoning.

19. The Ethics of Epistemic Dependence
Abstract. Much of what individuals reasonably believe depends on the testimony and intellectual labor of others. This dependence is unavoidable in complex societies, yet philosophical accounts of epistemic responsibility have tended to focus on the obligations of individual believers. I argue that epistemic dependence generates responsibilities that are irreducibly relational. In particular, those who possess disproportionate opportunities to produce, circulate, or evaluate information have obligations that cannot be discharged simply by maintaining individually reasonable beliefs. I distinguish these responsibilities from ordinary duties of honesty and argue that they arise from asymmetries in epistemic position. The resulting account illuminates institutional expertise, public disagreement, and the distribution of epistemic authority. It also explains why an agent may be epistemically responsible for failing to investigate a claim even when the claim turns out to be true. The paper concludes by developing a conception of epistemic responsibility that accommodates both individual agency and collective knowledge production.

20. What Is It to Be the Same Person?
Abstract. Personal identity is commonly understood as a relation of numerical sameness holding between a person at different times. Theories of identity then attempt to identify the psychological, biological, or metaphysical conditions under which this relation obtains. This paper questions whether the standard framing adequately captures the phenomenon that motivates our concern with personal identity. I argue that several puzzles about persistence arise because numerical identity is asked to explain practical relations that do not themselves require strict identity. In particular, responsibility, anticipation, and prudential concern can extend across changes in memory, character, and psychological continuity. I develop an account that distinguishes the metaphysics of personal persistence from the practical significance of continuity. This distinction does not render numerical identity unimportant; rather, it explains why facts about who survives and facts about whose future matters need not coincide. The proposal offers a new way of approaching familiar thought experiments involving memory loss, psychological transformation, and personal survival.
