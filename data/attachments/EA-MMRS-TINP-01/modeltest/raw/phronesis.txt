### Twenty Hypothetical Abstracts for a Forthcoming Issue of Phronesis

Original, fictional articles composed in the style of Phronesis: A Journal for Ancient Philosophy. The abstracts are intended to resemble ordinary submissions accepted for publication: narrowly formulated interpretive problems, sustained engagement with primary texts, identifiable scholarly interventions, and arguments of manageable scope. None represents an actual forthcoming article.

### 1\. The Priority of Definition in Aristotle's Posterior Analytics II.8–10

Abstract

Aristotle's account of the relation between definition and demonstration in Posterior Analytics II.8–10 has often been taken to require a distinction between nominal and causal definitions. Yet this distinction leaves unresolved the question of how an investigator can identify the relevant causal structure before possessing the definition that articulates it. I argue that Aristotle's discussion distinguishes two forms of epistemic priority that need not coincide: the priority of a cause in explanation and the priority of a provisional account in inquiry. On this reading, the investigator's initial grasp of what a term signifies is neither a defective scientific definition nor a merely linguistic possession. It is an indispensable condition for identifying the explanatory question to which demonstration supplies an answer. This interpretation clarifies the transition from knowing that something is the case to knowing why it is so, without attributing to Aristotle a circular procedure of scientific discovery.

### 2\. Plato's Sophist and the Unity of Negative Predication

Abstract

The Stranger's treatment of not-being in the Sophist is commonly understood as a solution to the problem of false statement through the identification of negation with difference. This paper argues that the account at 257b–259d does not, by itself, establish the conditions under which a negative predication is true. The distinction between being different from something and not being something requires an additional account of the relation between the subject of predication and the kind denied of it. I show that the subsequent discussion of logos at 261d–263d supplies this account by distinguishing the combination of names and verbs from the combination of kinds. The resulting theory neither reduces negation to simple difference nor anticipates a modern propositional semantics. Rather, it explains how statements can deny determinations of subjects while preserving the ontological commitments that make meaningful predication possible.

### 3\. Theophrastus on Hypothetical Syllogisms and the Limits of Aristotelian Logic

Abstract

The evidence for Theophrastus' treatment of hypothetical syllogisms is fragmentary and predominantly transmitted through later commentators. Nevertheless, it permits a more precise reconstruction of his contribution than the conventional contrast between Aristotelian categorical logic and Stoic propositional logic allows. I examine the testimonia concerning wholly hypothetical syllogisms and argue that Theophrastus' innovations concern the systematic treatment of inferential transitions rather than the replacement of terms by propositions as the fundamental objects of logic. Particular attention is given to the relation between the transitivity of hypothetical connections and the explanatory requirements of syllogistic demonstration. I suggest that the surviving evidence supports an extension of Aristotelian inferential practice whose theoretical significance has been obscured by retrospective classifications. Theophrastus emerges not simply as a transitional figure but as a thinker investigating forms of necessity that Aristotle's categorical syllogistic did not independently systematise.

### 4\. Perception and the Common Sense in Aristotle's De Anima III.2

Abstract

Aristotle's claim that we perceive that we see has generated competing interpretations of the relation between individual senses and the common perceptual capacity. According to one influential approach, reflexive awareness belongs to a distinct faculty that monitors the operations of the particular senses. I argue instead that De Anima III.2 treats awareness of perceiving as an intrinsic feature of perceptual activity, while assigning to the common sense the coordination of perceptual discriminations that cannot be accomplished by any single modality. The distinction is supported by a close examination of 425b12–426b8 and its relation to Aristotle's account of actuality in II.5. On this interpretation, Aristotle neither postulates an infinite hierarchy of perceptual observers nor identifies all perceptual awareness with a single undifferentiated activity. The common sense explains the unity of perception without becoming the exclusive subject of perceptual consciousness.

### 5\. Socratic Ignorance and the Conditions of Refutation in the Gorgias

Abstract

Socrates' professions of ignorance appear to stand in tension with the substantive moral commitments he defends in the Gorgias. This paper argues that the tension arises from treating Socratic ignorance as a general prohibition on affirmative philosophical claims. I distinguish the epistemic commitments required to conduct an elenchus from those required to establish a demonstrative science of virtue. Through an examination of Socrates' exchanges with Polus and Callicles, I show that the refutations depend on asymmetries between the interlocutors' acknowledged beliefs and the commitments governing their practical choices. Socrates' confidence in the superiority of justice is therefore neither straightforward knowledge nor merely a strategic posture. It expresses a form of rational commitment whose justification remains answerable to examination. The Gorgias consequently offers an account of philosophical authority grounded in the continuing possibility of refutation rather than in possession of an unassailable doctrine.

### 6\. Aristotle on Chance, Incidental Causes, and the Explanation of Rare Events

Abstract

In Physics II.4–6 Aristotle characterises chance as an incidental cause of events that occur for the sake of something. This account is frequently interpreted as making chance explanatorily derivative and therefore philosophically unimportant. I argue that Aristotle's distinction between incidental and per se causation identifies a genuine explanatory structure that cannot be eliminated by providing a complete description of the relevant efficient causes. The example of the man who unexpectedly encounters his debtor in the marketplace shows that an event can possess an intelligible causal history without possessing the teleological unity under which it is described as fortunate. I further argue that the rarity associated with chance is not simply a statistical property but depends upon the explanatory classification of the event. This interpretation preserves Aristotle's commitment to natural regularity while accounting for the irreducibility of incidental causal descriptions.

### 7\. Recollection without Innate Propositions: The Argument of Plato's Meno

Abstract

The geometrical interrogation of the slave in Plato's Meno is often understood as evidence for the pre-existence of explicitly formulated mathematical knowledge. Yet the dialogue carefully distinguishes the possession of true opinions from the possession of knowledge, and the demonstration concludes without establishing that the slave has achieved the latter. I argue that the recollection argument requires only the prior availability of structures of rational recognition, not the preservation of determinate propositions acquired before birth. The geometrical episode illustrates how questioning can transform an initially unexamined confidence into an awareness of explanatory dependence. This interpretation also clarifies the relation between recollection and the subsequent hypothesis concerning the teachability of virtue. Recollection does not furnish an independent epistemology that renders inquiry unnecessary; it explains why inquiry can proceed despite the absence of an antecedently adequate account of its object.

### 8\. The Stoic Criterion of Truth and the Possibility of Cognitive Error

Abstract

The Stoic conception of the cognitive impression has frequently been criticised on the grounds that an impression's causal origin cannot guarantee its truth. I argue that this objection risks overlooking the distinction between the conditions under which an impression is cognitive and the conditions under which an agent recognises it as such. Drawing upon Sextus Empiricus and Cicero, I reconstruct the Stoic response to the possibility of impressions indistinguishable from cognitive ones. The resulting account does not require the infallibility of ordinary acts of assent. Instead, it locates epistemic security in the disciplined discrimination of impressions whose distinctive character depends upon their relation to the objects that produce them. Although the Academic objection exposes a difficulty in specifying that distinctive character, it does not establish that the Stoic criterion is internally inconsistent. The dispute concerns the accessibility of epistemic warrant rather than the bare possibility of error.

### 9\. The Unmoved Mover and the Unity of the Heavens in Metaphysics Λ.8

Abstract

The plurality of unmoved movers introduced in Metaphysics Λ.8 has long complicated the interpretation of Aristotle's account of the first principle in Λ.6–7. This paper argues that the apparent tension between plurality and primacy depends upon an insufficient distinction between the explanatory functions of the first mover and those of the subordinate movers. Through a close reading of 1073a14–1074a31, I show that Aristotle's astronomical considerations establish a plurality of eternal explanatory objects without distributing the first mover's causal priority among them. The ordering of celestial motions supplies the relevant structure of dependence, although it does not amount to an efficient-causal derivation of the subordinate movers from the first. I conclude that Λ.8 neither abandons the unity of the first principle nor offers a fully developed metaphysical hierarchy. Its argument instead specifies the conditions under which a plurality of separate substances is compatible with a uniquely primary actuality.

### 10\. Pleasure as an Unimpeded Activity in Aristotle's Rhetoric I.11

Abstract

The definition of pleasure in Rhetoric I.11 appears to differ substantially from the accounts developed in Nicomachean Ethics VII and X. Whereas the ethical discussions connect pleasure with activity and its completion, the Rhetoric describes pleasure in terms of a movement and a perceptible restoration toward a natural condition. I argue that the apparent disagreement is partly explained by the distinct explanatory tasks of the three discussions. The rhetorical account identifies the conditions under which pleasure can be anticipated, represented, and employed as a motive in deliberation, rather than offering an exhaustive analysis of its metaphysical nature. Nevertheless, its language of restoration cannot be reduced to a merely dialectical concession. I show that the account preserves an important role for the awareness of transitions between conditions, a feature that remains compatible with the ethical identification of pleasure as belonging to unimpeded activity.

### 11\. Parmenides' Two Ways and the Modal Force of Necessity

Abstract

The distinction between the two ways of inquiry in Parmenides' poem is frequently interpreted as a contrast between existence and non-existence. I argue that the modal vocabulary of fragment B2 requires a more complex account of the relation between being, thinking, and necessity. The goddess does not merely prohibit reference to what does not exist; she distinguishes a path whose object is necessarily available to thought from one whose purported object cannot satisfy the conditions of inquiry. By examining the interaction of esti, chrē, and the expressions of impossibility in B2 and B6, I show that the argument proceeds through constraints on intelligibility rather than through an unrestricted existential quantification. This interpretation does not remove the ontological force of Parmenides' conclusions. It explains why the necessity of being emerges from the requirements of inquiry without reducing being to a property of language or thought.

### 12\. Protagoras' Measure Doctrine and the Authority of Appearances

Abstract

Plato's Theaetetus presents the Protagorean measure doctrine as the claim that whatever appears to an individual is true for that individual. The standard reconstruction takes this to entail a radical relativism that eliminates the possibility of disagreement. I argue that the dialogue's distinction between the truth of an appearance and the authority of a judgement permits a more discriminating interpretation. The defence of Protagoras at 166a–168c allows expertise to concern the transformation of appearances without requiring the expert to possess access to truths independent of every perceiver. This position remains vulnerable to Socrates' subsequent objections, but not for the simple reason that it renders teaching impossible. Rather, the decisive difficulty concerns the extension of the measure doctrine from present perceptual conditions to judgements about future advantage. The argument exposes a tension between the immediacy of appearance and the temporal structure of practical expertise.

### 13\. Deliberation and the Particular in Aristotle's Nicomachean Ethics VI

Abstract

Aristotle's account of practical wisdom assigns an indispensable role to knowledge of particulars, yet it remains disputed whether such knowledge constitutes a distinct form of perception or an application of universal practical principles. I argue that the relevant passages of Nicomachean Ethics VI distinguish perceptual recognition from the practical identification of an action under a description. The latter depends upon experience and cannot be derived from universal premises alone, although it is not independent of rational evaluation. I examine Aristotle's comparison between practical wisdom and mathematical understanding at 1142a23–30 and his discussion of the final particular in VI.11. These passages suggest that practical judgement terminates in a recognition whose content is already shaped by the agent's conception of the good. The result is an account of deliberation in which particulars are not merely inputs to reasoning but help determine what counts as a practically relevant conclusion.

### 14\. Epicurus on the Swerve and the Explanation of Voluntary Action

Abstract

The Epicurean atomic swerve is often understood as an attempt to secure freedom by introducing indeterminacy into an otherwise deterministic physical system. I argue that this reconstruction assigns the swerve a more direct explanatory role in individual decisions than the surviving evidence warrants. Lucretius' discussion in De rerum natura II.216–293 distinguishes the interruption of causal necessity from the positive source of voluntary movement. The swerve supplies a condition under which motions need not be determined by an antecedent infinite sequence, but it does not itself explain why an agent acts for a particular reason. I compare this account with the evidence concerning Epicurus' rejection of necessity in the Letter to Menoeceus and On Nature. The resulting interpretation preserves the importance of physical indeterminacy while locating the explanation of responsible agency at the level of the developed constitution of the agent.

### 15\. Plato's Divided Line and the Objects of Mathematical Thought

Abstract

The distinction between dianoia and noēsis in Republic VI has often been interpreted as establishing separate classes of mathematical and intelligible objects. This paper argues that the divided line primarily distinguishes modes of cognitive access rather than assigning mathematical reasoning an autonomous ontology. The mathematician's reliance upon hypotheses and visible diagrams determines the limitations of mathematical understanding, even when its objects are not themselves sensible. I show that the contrast between the two upper sections of the line turns on the treatment of hypotheses as starting points or as objects requiring justification. This interpretation explains why dialectic can investigate the same structures that mathematics employs without merely repeating mathematical demonstrations. It also avoids the assumption that Plato's epistemological hierarchy must correspond to a one-to-one hierarchy of distinct entities. The divided line articulates differences in explanatory completeness, not simply differences in subject matter.

### 16\. The Meaning of Oikeiōsis in Hierocles' Account of Social Attachment

Abstract

Hierocles' account of concentric circles of attachment is commonly presented as an extension of the Stoic doctrine of oikeiōsis from self-preservation to universal human concern. I argue that this presentation obscures the role of practical habituation in the surviving account. The injunction to draw the circles inward does not follow automatically from the recognition of shared rational nature. It describes an activity through which agents modify the salience of existing social relations. By examining the fragments preserved in Stobaeus alongside Hierocles' treatment of the animal's awareness of its own constitution, I distinguish the original appropriation of oneself from the deliberate appropriation of others. The latter is neither a spontaneous consequence of biological attachment nor an external supplement to Stoic ethics. It is a practical achievement grounded in the capacity to reorganise one's relations of concern according to rationally defensible descriptions.

### 17\. Aristotle's Categories and the Predication of Differentiae

Abstract

The status of differentiae in Aristotle's Categories presents a difficulty for interpretations that identify the distinction between substance and quality with a distinction between essential and accidental predication. At 3b10–23 Aristotle maintains that differentiae, like genera, are predicated synonymously of their subjects, although they do not appear to be substances in the primary sense. I argue that this treatment reflects a distinction between the classificatory role of a predicate and its categorial status. Differentiae contribute to the determination of what a substance is without themselves functioning as independent substantial kinds. This reading is supported by the contrast between predication in the Categories and the analysis of definition in Topics I.4–8. The resulting account explains why the predication of a differentia can be essential without requiring that every essential predicate belong to the category of substance.

### 18\. Plotinus on Memory and the Activity of the Intellect

Abstract

Plotinus' account of memory appears to generate a paradox: the soul requires memory because its activities unfold in time, while intellect, whose activity is eternal, has no need of recollection. Yet the soul's ascent toward intellect seems to require the preservation of what it has previously understood. I argue that Enneads IV.3–4 distinguishes memory as the retention of past experience from the continuing availability of intelligible content. The distinction permits Plotinus to deny memory to intellect without denying continuity to the soul's intellectual life. Through an examination of IV.3.25–32 and IV.4.1–5, I show that recollection becomes unnecessary not because intelligible objects are forgotten but because their apprehension no longer depends upon temporal succession. This interpretation clarifies the relation between discursive thinking and intellectual contemplation, while preserving the asymmetry between the soul's acquired understanding and intellect's unchanging actuality.

### 19\. The Unity of Virtue in Plato's Protagoras: Measurement and Motivation

Abstract

The argument concerning the unity of virtue in Plato's Protagoras culminates in a comparison between courage, knowledge, and the measurement of pleasures and pains. Scholars disagree about whether Socrates' hedonistic argument expresses his own ethical commitments or merely exploits assumptions accepted by Protagoras and the many. I argue that the argument's central function is to establish a structural relation between practical error and failures of discrimination, independently of its commitment to hedonism. The measuring art provides a model of rational agency in which apparent conflicts of motivation are explained by differences in cognitive presentation. This model supports the identification of courage with knowledge only under additional assumptions about the objects of fear and confidence. I conclude that the dialogue offers a conditional argument for the unity of virtue whose unresolved premises are integral to its philosophical significance rather than evidence of an incomplete demonstration.

### 20\. Explanation and Explanatory Priority in Aristotle's Metaphysics Ζ.17

Abstract

In Metaphysics Ζ.17 Aristotle proposes a new starting point for the investigation of substance by asking why matter constitutes a determinate thing. The argument is often interpreted as establishing form as the cause of the unity of a composite substance. I argue that its more immediate achievement is to specify the logical conditions under which a question of substantial explanation can be properly formulated. The contrast between asking why a thing is itself and asking why certain materials constitute a house or a human being reveals that explanatory inquiry presupposes a distinction between the subject to be explained and the determination in virtue of which it is what it is. On this reading, form emerges as an explanatory principle through the analysis of the why-question rather than through an independent appeal to ontological composition. This interpretation clarifies both the continuity and the difference between Ζ.17 and Aristotle's earlier accounts of demonstration and definition.

These twenty abstracts collectively reproduce several recognisable features of Phronesis: close engagement with disputed passages, restrained claims about interpretive novelty, arguments developed through distinctions rather than sweeping historical narratives, and a concentration on problems of inference, explanation, ontology, epistemology, and practical reasoning. They are deliberately conventional in their scholarly presentation, even where their proposed interpretations are substantive.
