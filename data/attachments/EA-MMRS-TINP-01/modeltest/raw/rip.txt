The following twenty abstracts are fictional but designed to be plausible submissions for a forthcoming issue of Research in Phenomenology. They reflect the journal's scholarly orientation toward phenomenological philosophy, including engagements with Husserl, Heidegger, Merleau-Ponty, Levinas, Sartre, and contemporary continental thought. The proposed articles vary in subject matter, methodological approach, and philosophical emphasis, while maintaining the conceptual density and academic register appropriate to the journal.

# Twenty Proposed Articles for _Research in Phenomenology_

### 1\. The Phenomenology of the Unavailable: Absence, Negativity, and the Limits of Experience

**Abstract**

Phenomenological accounts of experience have traditionally emphasized the structures through which objects become present to consciousness. Yet the experience of absence presents a distinctive difficulty: how can what is not given nevertheless become phenomenologically significant? This article examines the constitutive role of absence in intentional experience, drawing principally on Husserl's analyses of empty intentions and Heidegger's account of withdrawal. It argues that absence should not be understood merely as a deficient mode of presence but as a positive structural dimension of appearing. Through an analysis of waiting, recollection, and the experience of loss, the article develops an account of how the unavailable can nevertheless orient experience. It concludes by suggesting that phenomenology requires a more differentiated conception of givenness, one capable of accommodating forms of manifestation that depend upon non-presence.

### 2\. Flesh and Finitude: Rethinking Mortality in Merleau-Ponty's Later Phenomenology

**Abstract**

Merleau-Ponty's late ontology of flesh offers an account of embodiment that complicates the traditional opposition between subject and world. Less frequently examined, however, is the significance of mortality within this account. This article argues that finitude is not simply an existential condition imposed upon embodied existence but an internal dimension of the reversibility that characterizes flesh. Beginning with Merleau-Ponty's analyses of visibility, touching, and the chiasm, it examines the ways in which bodily existence is simultaneously exposed to and withdrawn from the world. The experience of aging and bodily vulnerability provides a particularly revealing point of departure. By placing these phenomena in conversation with Heidegger's analysis of being-toward-death, the article develops a phenomenology of mortality that emphasizes corporeal exposure rather than anticipatory understanding.

### 3\. The Other Side of Attention: Distraction and the Phenomenology of Everyday Consciousness

**Abstract**

Phenomenological investigations of attention have generally privileged focused awareness, treating distraction as a secondary or deficient modification of intentional life. This article challenges that hierarchy by examining distraction as an irreducible structure of everyday experience. Drawing on Husserl's analyses of attention and Merleau-Ponty's account of perceptual intentionality, it argues that consciousness is never exclusively directed toward a determinate object. Rather, attention emerges against a background of competing solicitations that remain operative even when they are not thematically apprehended. Distraction therefore reveals something fundamental about the temporal and embodied organization of experience. The article considers ordinary activities such as reading, walking, and listening to demonstrate how awareness is constituted through movements of approach, withdrawal, and interruption. It concludes that a phenomenology of consciousness must account for its constitutive susceptibility to dispersion.

### 4\. The Silence of the World: Heidegger, Language, and the Possibility of Meaning

**Abstract**

The relationship between language and silence occupies an important but insufficiently developed position in Heidegger's later thought. Although language is frequently understood as the site in which Being comes to expression, silence appears to resist the very articulation through which meaning becomes accessible. This article examines silence not as the mere absence of speech but as a distinctive mode of disclosure. Through a reading of Heidegger's _On the Way to Language_ and _The Origin of the Work of Art_, it argues that silence makes possible a dimension of intelligibility that cannot be reduced to propositional articulation. Particular attention is given to the relationship between poetic language, listening, and reticence. The article concludes that silence should be understood as internal to the event of language itself, rather than as its external limit.

### 5\. The Weight of the Past: Retention, Habit, and the Constitution of Temporal Experience

**Abstract**

The phenomenological analysis of temporality has often concentrated on the relationship between primal impression, retention, and protention. While these structures explain the temporal continuity of experience, they leave open the question of how the past becomes sedimented into habitual patterns of perception and action. This article revisits Husserl's account of internal time-consciousness in conjunction with his later investigations of passive synthesis and habituality. It argues that the past does not simply remain available as a modified present but acquires a practical weight that shapes the possibilities of subsequent experience. Habit is thus interpreted as a temporal phenomenon through which previous experience becomes operative without being explicitly remembered. By distinguishing recollection from sedimentation, the article proposes a broader account of temporal constitution that accommodates the persistence of the past within present experience.

### 6\. Vulnerability and the Face: Levinas on the Phenomenology of Exposure

**Abstract**

Levinas's account of the face-to-face encounter is frequently interpreted through the ethical priority of the Other and the asymmetrical responsibility that this encounter establishes. Less attention has been given to the phenomenological significance of vulnerability within this account. This article argues that vulnerability is not simply a moral condition attributed to the Other but a fundamental structure of interpersonal appearing. Through a close examination of _Totality and Infinity_ and _Otherwise than Being_, it considers how exposure unsettles the stability of the perceiving subject and challenges the assumption that experience begins with self-possession. The analysis distinguishes vulnerability from empirical fragility, emphasizing its constitutive role in the possibility of ethical relation. It concludes that Levinas's phenomenology offers an account of subjectivity in which responsibility emerges through an originary dispossession rather than through reflective decision.

### 7\. The Phenomenology of Boredom Reconsidered: Heidegger and the Temporality of Indifference

**Abstract**

Heidegger's analysis of profound boredom in _The Fundamental Concepts of Metaphysics_ identifies an experience in which beings as a whole become indifferent and time appears to stand still. This article reconsiders the phenomenological significance of boredom by distinguishing profound boredom from ordinary states of tedium and restlessness. It argues that boredom discloses not an absence of meaningful experience but a transformation in the conditions under which meaningful possibilities become accessible. Through an examination of Heidegger's distinction between being left empty and being held in suspense, the article explores the relationship between affectivity, temporality, and possibility. It further suggests that boredom exposes the limits of an understanding of existence organized around purposeful activity. In this respect, boredom becomes a privileged phenomenon through which the ontological significance of seemingly unproductive experience can be reconsidered.

### 8\. The Invisible Body: Pain, Self-Awareness, and the Limits of Embodied Subjectivity

**Abstract**

The body is ordinarily experienced not as an object among objects but as the tacit medium through which the world becomes accessible. Pain, however, disrupts this transparency, transforming the body from an inconspicuous condition of experience into its immediate and sometimes overwhelming focus. This article investigates the phenomenological significance of pain through a comparative reading of Husserl, Merleau-Ponty, and Sartre. It argues that pain cannot be adequately described either as an internal sensation or as a disturbance of practical bodily agency. Rather, pain reorganizes the relationship between bodily subjectivity and the surrounding world, altering the possibilities of movement, attention, and temporal anticipation. By examining the experience of chronic pain in particular, the article develops an account of embodiment in which bodily self-awareness is neither fully transparent nor entirely inaccessible to reflection.

### 9\. Dwelling after Displacement: Heidegger and the Phenomenology of Homelessness

**Abstract**

Heidegger's reflections on dwelling have frequently been interpreted as an ontological account of humanity's belonging to the world. Yet the experience of displacement raises questions about the relationship between dwelling, place, and existential familiarity that cannot be resolved through an appeal to rootedness alone. This article examines homelessness as a phenomenological disturbance of the structures through which places acquire familiarity and significance. Drawing on Heidegger's account of dwelling, alongside Merleau-Ponty's analysis of spatiality, it argues that dwelling should be understood as an ongoing achievement rather than a stable relation between human beings and geographical locations. The experience of displacement reveals how spatial orientation depends upon affective, temporal, and practical attachments. The article concludes by proposing a phenomenology of dwelling that acknowledges estrangement as an internal possibility of being-in-the-world.

### 10\. The Gaze without Possession: Sartre, Recognition, and the Ambiguity of Intersubjectivity

**Abstract**

Sartre's celebrated analysis of the Look describes the encounter with another person as an experience in which the subject discovers itself as an object within a world it does not exclusively organize. Although this analysis has been influential, its emphasis on conflict has often obscured the more ambiguous possibilities of intersubjective experience. This article revisits Sartre's account by examining the relationship between visibility, recognition, and the impossibility of possessing another consciousness. It argues that the Look reveals not simply the vulnerability of the self to objectification but the structural non-coincidence that characterizes interpersonal relations. Through a comparison with Merleau-Ponty's account of intercorporeality, the article considers whether the experience of being seen might also disclose a dimension of shared embodiment. It concludes by identifying an unresolved tension between recognition and alienation within Sartrean phenomenology.

### 11\. The Phenomenology of Forgiveness: Memory, Responsibility, and the Irreversibility of Time

**Abstract**

Forgiveness presents a distinctive problem for phenomenology because it involves a transformation of the relationship to a past that cannot itself be changed. How can an event remain irrevocably what it was while losing some of its determining force in the present? This article addresses this question through a dialogue between Husserl's phenomenology of time-consciousness and Ricoeur's analyses of memory and forgiveness. It argues that forgiveness cannot be understood as the erasure of recollection or the suspension of responsibility. Rather, it constitutes a modification of the temporal horizon within which an injury continues to signify. The article distinguishes forgiveness from forgetting, reconciliation, and excusing, showing how each involves a different relation to the past. It concludes that forgiveness discloses a possibility of temporal transformation without requiring the denial of historical irreversibility.

### 12\. Beyond the Human Horizon: Animality and the Limits of Phenomenological Experience

**Abstract**

The question of animal experience presents a fundamental challenge to phenomenology's account of intentionality and world-disclosure. If phenomenological description begins from structures of experience that are accessible through reflection, how can it approach forms of life whose modes of appearing may differ radically from our own? This article examines the problem through Husserl's account of the life-world and Heidegger's controversial distinction between human world-forming and animal world-poverty. It argues that these approaches risk reproducing an anthropocentric hierarchy by measuring animal experience against specifically human possibilities of understanding. Drawing on Merleau-Ponty's reflections on animal behavior, the article proposes an alternative conception of interspecies phenomenology grounded in embodied responsiveness and shared environmental involvement. The aim is not to assimilate animal experience to human consciousness but to articulate the limits and possibilities of phenomenological access to other forms of life.

### 13\. The Phenomenology of Waiting: Anticipation, Passivity, and the Experience of Time

**Abstract**

Waiting is an ordinary experience that nevertheless exposes fundamental structures of temporal consciousness. Unlike deliberate anticipation, waiting involves a suspension of immediate practical activity and an orientation toward an event whose arrival remains uncertain. This article investigates waiting through Husserl's analyses of protention and Heidegger's account of temporality, arguing that waiting cannot be reduced to an empty interval between successive moments. Rather, it constitutes a distinctive mode of temporal existence in which the present is experienced through its relation to an unresolved future. The analysis distinguishes active waiting, anxious waiting, and involuntary waiting, showing how each modifies the relationship between agency and passivity. It further suggests that waiting reveals the dependence of subjective time upon events and possibilities that exceed individual control. In this respect, waiting provides an important corrective to interpretations of temporality centered exclusively on projection and purposeful action.

### 14\. The World before Reflection: Passive Synthesis and the Phenomenology of Familiarity

**Abstract**

Phenomenology has often been associated with the reflective clarification of structures that ordinarily remain unnoticed. Yet the familiar world is not primarily constituted through acts of explicit reflection. It is encountered through habitual expectations, associative connections, and affective orientations that operate beneath the threshold of thematic awareness. This article examines Husserl's analyses of passive synthesis as a basis for understanding the pre-reflective constitution of familiarity. It argues that familiarity is neither a merely psychological disposition nor a secondary product of repeated experience, but a fundamental dimension of world-experience itself. Particular attention is given to the role of associative awakening and affective prominence in organizing the perceptual field. By emphasizing the passive dimensions of constitution, the article seeks to develop an account of phenomenological experience that does not privilege reflective consciousness at the expense of its tacit conditions.

### 15\. Anxiety and the Failure of Possibility: Reconsidering Heidegger's Existential Analytic

**Abstract**

In _Being and Time_, anxiety discloses the nullity of everyday significance and brings Dasein before its ownmost possibility of being. This article reexamines the relationship between anxiety and possibility, questioning whether Heidegger's analysis adequately distinguishes existential disclosure from the affective experience of disorientation. It argues that anxiety should not be interpreted simply as a privileged access to authentic existence, since its phenomenological structure involves both the collapse of familiar possibilities and the difficulty of projecting new ones. Through a close reading of the analysis of anxiety in relation to falling, uncanniness, and being-toward-death, the article identifies a tension between disclosure and paralysis. This tension, it suggests, is not incidental but essential to the phenomenon itself. The article concludes by proposing that anxiety reveals possibility precisely through its capacity to suspend the practical frameworks that ordinarily sustain it.

### 16\. The Phenomenology of Touch: Intimacy, Reversibility, and the Limits of Reciprocity

**Abstract**

Touch occupies a distinctive position in phenomenological accounts of embodiment because it involves an immediate relation between the sensing body and what it senses. Merleau-Ponty's analysis of the touching-touched body develops this relation into an account of reversibility, yet the precise status of reciprocity remains philosophically unsettled. This article examines the limits of reversibility by considering the experience of touching another person. It argues that interpersonal touch cannot be adequately described as an exchange between equivalent sensing bodies, since intimacy involves an irreducible asymmetry between touching and being touched. Drawing on Merleau-Ponty's working notes and Levinas's account of proximity, the article develops a conception of tactile experience in which bodily contact both establishes and disrupts reciprocity. It concludes that touch offers a particularly revealing site for reconsidering the relationship between embodiment, alterity, and ethical exposure.

### 17\. Technology and the Transformation of Perception: A Phenomenological Inquiry into Digital Presence

**Abstract**

Digital technologies increasingly mediate the ways in which objects, places, and other persons become present to experience. Although phenomenological philosophy has long examined the relationship between technical mediation and world-disclosure, contemporary digital environments require renewed attention to the transformation of perceptual and interpersonal relations. This article develops a phenomenological account of digital presence through a dialogue between Heidegger's analysis of enframing and Merleau-Ponty's account of embodied perception. It argues that digital mediation does not simply introduce representations between an already constituted subject and world. Rather, it reorganizes the structures of proximity, attention, and temporal availability through which experience is constituted. Particular consideration is given to video communication and virtual environments, where presence is simultaneously intensified and displaced. The article concludes by suggesting that digital experience reveals the need for a more differentiated phenomenology of mediation.

### 18\. The Stranger within Experience: Otherness and the Limits of the Life-World

**Abstract**

The concept of the life-world has traditionally served phenomenology as a means of articulating the pre-theoretical horizon within which objects and others become intelligible. Yet the encounter with the stranger complicates any assumption that the life-world is necessarily a shared or homogeneous domain. This article examines the phenomenological significance of strangeness through Husserl's analyses of intersubjectivity and Levinas's account of alterity. It argues that the stranger is not simply someone whose cultural or experiential background remains unfamiliar, but a figure who exposes the limits of the horizon through which familiarity is constituted. The analysis considers the tension between the universal structures of world-experience and the irreducible particularity of another's perspective. It concludes that phenomenology must understand the life-world not merely as a condition of commonality but also as a field in which the possibility of non-coincidence remains constitutive.

### 19\. The Experience of Ruins: Historical Time, Materiality, and the Persistence of the Past

**Abstract**

Ruins occupy an ambiguous temporal position: they are materially present while referring to a world that has disappeared. Their phenomenological significance therefore cannot be exhausted by an account of historical representation or aesthetic contemplation. This article investigates ruins as phenomena through which the relationship between material persistence and historical absence becomes perceptible. Drawing on Husserl's account of sedimentation, Heidegger's reflections on historicality, and Merleau-Ponty's conception of expressive materiality, it argues that ruins disclose the past not as a completed sequence of events but as a dimension that remains operative within present experience. Particular attention is given to the ways in which architectural remains solicit bodily movement and imaginative reconstruction. The article concludes that ruins provide a distinctive example of temporal appearing, in which historical absence acquires a material and perceptual presence without becoming fully recoverable.

### 20\. The Possibility of Beginning Again: Natality, Temporality, and Phenomenological Renewal

**Abstract**

The possibility of beginning again raises a question that lies at the intersection of phenomenology, existential philosophy, and political thought: how can a subject initiate something genuinely new within a world already structured by prior experience and inherited meanings? This article approaches the problem through an examination of Husserl's account of habituality, Heidegger's conception of projection, and Arendt's notion of natality. It argues that beginning should not be understood as an absolute rupture with the past, since every new initiative emerges within a horizon of sedimented possibilities. Nevertheless, novelty cannot be reduced to the rearrangement of what is already given. Through an analysis of decision, interruption, and the experience of unforeseen possibility, the article develops a phenomenological conception of renewal as a transformation of inherited horizons. It concludes that the capacity to begin again reveals an essential openness within the temporal constitution of human existence.

Editorial note: The twenty proposals deliberately distribute their attention across classical phenomenological problems and newer areas of inquiry. Numbers 1–8 are primarily concerned with consciousness, embodiment, affectivity, and intersubjectivity; numbers 9–16 develop existential, ethical, and temporal questions; and numbers 17–20 extend phenomenological analysis toward technology, historical experience, and the possibility of renewal.

These are proposed article concepts, not actual forthcoming contributions or representations of the journal's editorial decisions.
