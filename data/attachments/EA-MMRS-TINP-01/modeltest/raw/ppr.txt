1. The Limits of Epistemic Transparency
Abstract

Philosophers frequently assume that subjects have privileged access to the grounds of their own beliefs, even when they lack comparable access to the grounds of other people's beliefs. This paper challenges the assumption that such privilege amounts to epistemic transparency. I argue that first-person access is better understood as a distinctive form of epistemic responsiveness: subjects can sometimes recognize and revise their commitments without being able to identify the considerations that originally supported them. This account explains why introspective confidence can coexist with substantial ignorance about one's own cognitive processes. It also provides a framework for distinguishing genuine self-knowledge from mere psychological certainty. After examining several influential accounts of introspective justification, I develop a model on which self-knowledge is neither inferential nor incorrigible but depends upon a subject's capacity to participate in the rational regulation of her own mental life. The resulting view preserves an important asymmetry between first-person and third-person knowledge without treating the former as epistemically foundational.

2. Agency Without Authorship: Rethinking the Source of Action
Abstract

An influential assumption in theories of agency is that an agent must be the author of her actions in order to be responsible for them. Yet ordinary cases of intentional action suggest that authorship is neither necessary nor sufficient for agency. This paper develops an alternative account according to which agency consists primarily in a distinctive relation between an agent's practical perspective and the movements through which she acts. On this account, an action can be attributable to a person even when its production depends upon habits, social expectations, or processes that she neither initiated nor consciously controls. I distinguish this notion of attribution from both causal responsibility and moral responsibility, arguing that the three need not coincide. The distinction helps explain familiar cases involving compulsive behavior, unreflective habits, and socially conditioned action. I conclude by considering whether this account can accommodate the intuition that genuine agency requires some degree of critical self-governance, without thereby reintroducing an implausibly demanding conception of authorship.

3. The Phenomenology of Uncertainty
Abstract

Uncertainty is commonly analyzed as an epistemic attitude toward propositions whose truth remains unsettled. This paper argues that such an analysis overlooks the distinctive experiential character of uncertainty. In particular, uncertainty often involves not simply a diminished degree of confidence but a transformation in how possibilities appear as available for consideration and action. Drawing upon phenomenological descriptions of hesitation, anticipation, and indecision, I develop an account of uncertainty as a mode of practical openness. On this view, uncertainty concerns the organization of a subject's deliberative field rather than merely the strength of her propositional attitudes. This proposal has implications for debates about the relationship between belief and action, the rationality of suspension of judgment, and the nature of intellectual humility. It also explains why uncertainty can sometimes be experienced as liberating rather than debilitating. I conclude that an adequate epistemology of uncertainty must account for its first-person structure as well as its normative and probabilistic dimensions.

4. What Makes a Reason Normative?
Abstract

The question of what makes reasons normative has generated competing accounts in terms of desire, value, rationality, and the constitutive standards of agency. This paper argues that these approaches share a common difficulty: they often presuppose the normativity they aim to explain. I propose instead that reasons acquire their normative significance through their role in organizing the space of intelligible criticism. To recognize something as a reason is, on this account, to recognize a consideration as capable of making a difference to what an agent may reasonably be expected to do, believe, or feel. This conception does not reduce normativity to social approval or interpersonal agreement. Rather, it identifies a structural connection between reason-giving and the possibility of holding oneself and others answerable. I defend the proposal against the objection that it makes normativity dependent upon actual practices of criticism. The resulting account offers a unified explanation of the normative force of practical and theoretical reasons.

5. The Persistence of Personal Identity
Abstract

What explains the persistence of a person through substantial psychological and bodily change? Contemporary theories typically appeal to psychological continuity, bodily continuity, or some combination of the two. This paper argues that these accounts overlook the significance of an individual's continuing practical orientation toward her own past and future. I develop a relational account of personal persistence according to which a person persists partly in virtue of the continuing organization of commitments, projects, and patterns of practical concern. Such continuity need not involve the preservation of particular memories or dispositions. Nor does it require that an individual remain psychologically similar to her earlier self. The account therefore accommodates cases involving memory loss, radical character transformation, and changes in fundamental values. I argue that practical continuity is not an additional psychological criterion of identity but a distinct dimension of what makes a life belong to one person. The proposal also illuminates the relationship between personal identity and responsibility.

6. Perception and the Problem of Cognitive Penetration
Abstract

The possibility that beliefs and expectations influence perceptual experience has become a central issue in contemporary philosophy of perception. While some philosophers regard cognitive penetration as a threat to the epistemic authority of perception, others argue that top-down influences are indispensable to perceptual understanding. This paper challenges the assumption that these positions exhaust the available options. I distinguish between influences that alter the content of perceptual experience and influences that determine the significance assigned to perceptually presented objects. The distinction allows us to explain how background beliefs can shape perceptual attention and interpretation without necessarily changing what is experienced. I develop the proposal through cases involving ambiguous figures, expertise, and perceptual learning. I then argue that the epistemic consequences of cognitive penetration depend upon the particular stage of perceptual processing at which an influence operates. The resulting account preserves the possibility of epistemically significant perception while recognizing the constitutive role of cognition in ordinary perceptual activity.

7. The Value of Being Wrong
Abstract

Philosophical discussions of epistemic value generally focus on the acquisition of true beliefs and the avoidance of error. This paper examines a neglected possibility: that certain forms of being wrong can themselves contribute to intellectual achievement. I distinguish between errors that merely reflect defective inquiry and errors that arise through the responsible pursuit of difficult questions. The latter can disclose limitations in one's conceptual resources, reveal previously unnoticed assumptions, and make possible forms of understanding unavailable through straightforward success. I argue that the value of such errors is not merely instrumental. In appropriate circumstances, acknowledging and investigating a mistake manifests intellectual virtues that are independently valuable. This account challenges familiar attempts to identify epistemic success exclusively with true belief or accurate representation. It also suggests that the evaluation of inquiry should attend to the intellectual significance of its trajectory rather than exclusively to its final results. I conclude by considering how this conception bears on disagreement and scientific progress.

8. The Metaphysics of Ordinary Objects
Abstract

Ordinary objects appear to persist through changes in their material composition, spatial arrangement, and functional organization. Yet philosophical theories of persistence disagree about which features of an object are essential to its identity over time. This paper develops an account of ordinary objects as historically constituted entities whose persistence conditions are partly determined by the practices through which they are identified and maintained. The account rejects both unrestricted conventionalism and the view that object identity is entirely independent of human interests. I argue that ordinary objects possess objective historical structures, although the relevance of particular changes to their continued existence depends upon the kinds of objects they are. This approach offers a unified treatment of familiar cases involving repaired artifacts, gradually replaced components, and the transformation of functional objects. It also explains why apparently similar material changes can have different consequences for identity. The resulting metaphysics seeks to preserve the objectivity of ordinary persistence judgments without imposing a single universal criterion of identity.

9. Moral Responsibility and the Opacity of Character
Abstract

Responsibility for wrongdoing is frequently understood to depend upon an agent's capacity to recognize the moral significance of her conduct. But people are often ignorant of their own characters, including dispositions that substantially influence their behavior. This paper examines the implications of such ignorance for moral responsibility. I argue that responsibility does not require comprehensive self-knowledge, but neither can it be grounded solely in the capacity to recognize particular reasons at the moment of action. Instead, it depends partly upon an agent's diachronic responsiveness to opportunities for self-correction. This account distinguishes unavoidable ignorance of character from ignorance sustained through culpable inattention. It also explains why individuals may be responsible for patterns of conduct that they do not fully understand. After considering objections concerning manipulation, moral luck, and the limits of self-improvement, I develop a conception of responsibility that is sensitive to the temporal structure of agency. The resulting view connects moral responsibility with the ongoing project of becoming answerable for one's own character.

10. Can There Be Knowledge Without Understanding?
Abstract

Recent epistemology has increasingly distinguished knowledge from understanding, raising the question of whether the former can obtain in the absence of the latter. This paper argues that the distinction is both important and more difficult to sustain than commonly supposed. I begin by identifying cases in which individuals possess extensive true information while remaining unable to explain the phenomena those truths concern. I then argue that these cases do not establish that knowledge and understanding are wholly independent epistemic achievements. Rather, they reveal different dimensions of epistemic competence: knowledge concerns successful cognitive contact with truths, whereas understanding involves an ability to grasp explanatory and counterfactual relations among them. This distinction permits genuine knowledge without substantial understanding while preserving a constitutive connection between the two. I defend the account against the objection that it reduces understanding to an especially demanding form of knowledge. The paper concludes by considering the implications of this position for testimony, education, and the epistemic division of intellectual labor.

11. The Normative Significance of Attention
Abstract

Attention is often treated as a psychologically descriptive phenomenon, distinct from the normative dimensions of belief, action, and emotion. This paper challenges that separation. I argue that attention has a distinctive normative structure because what individuals attend to partly determines which considerations become available for their practical and epistemic deliberation. The argument begins with cases in which failures of attention seem blameworthy despite the absence of any corresponding failure of belief or intention. I show that such cases cannot be adequately explained by appealing exclusively to negligence or deficient cognitive performance. Instead, they reveal a form of answerability concerning the allocation of one's limited attentional resources. I develop an account of attentional responsibility that avoids the implausible implication that agents are responsible for everything they fail to notice. The account distinguishes deliberate inattention, habitual selectivity, and involuntary distraction. It also illuminates the ethical significance of attention in interpersonal relationships, political life, and the pursuit of knowledge.

12. Fiction, Imagination, and the Limits of Possibility
Abstract

Engagement with fiction invites us to imagine situations that may be physically impossible, conceptually incoherent, or inconsistent with what we know about the actual world. What, then, constrains the content of imaginative engagement? This paper argues that the familiar distinction between actual and merely fictional possibilities is insufficient to explain the structure of imagination. I propose that imaginative activity is governed by context-sensitive standards of coherence that need not correspond to metaphysical possibility. These standards explain how readers can imaginatively entertain impossible situations without thereby imagining contradictions in the ordinary logical sense. I distinguish between the acceptance of fictional premises, the construction of imaginative scenarios, and the evaluation of what follows within those scenarios. The distinction clarifies several puzzles concerning impossible fiction, narrative inconsistency, and imaginative resistance. It also challenges the assumption that the imagination is essentially a faculty for representing possible worlds. I conclude that fictional imagination is best understood as a flexible form of cognitive engagement governed by internally structured constraints.

13. The Meaning of Emotional Experience
Abstract

Emotions are commonly understood as responses to evaluative features of the world, but it remains controversial whether their representational character is analogous to that of perception or belief. This paper develops an account of emotional experience that emphasizes its distinctive form of significance. I argue that emotions do not simply represent objects as possessing particular evaluative properties; they present those objects as mattering to the subject in a particular way. Fear, resentment, admiration, and grief therefore involve different structures of practical and interpersonal significance, rather than merely different evaluations of an independently specified object. This approach explains why emotions can disclose aspects of situations that remain inaccessible to detached judgment, while also allowing that emotional experience can be systematically misleading. I consider the implications for debates about emotional rationality and the possibility of emotional knowledge. I conclude that an adequate theory of emotion must explain not only what emotions represent but also how they transform the subject's relation to what is represented.

14. Disagreement and the Rationality of Independence
Abstract

When two equally informed and apparently competent individuals disagree, each faces a familiar epistemic question: whether to revise her judgment in light of the other's disagreement or to maintain her original position. This paper examines the assumptions underlying the independence of these two responses. I argue that the standard framing of the disagreement problem obscures an important distinction between confidence in one's conclusion and confidence in one's deliberative procedure. An agent may reasonably maintain her judgment while becoming less confident that her reasoning was sufficient, or she may revise her judgment without regarding her original reasoning as defective. I develop an account of epistemic independence that accommodates these possibilities. On this view, rational disagreement requires neither automatic deference nor an unrestricted right to disregard opposing testimony. Instead, the appropriate response depends upon the agent's assessment of the shared and unshared features of the parties' epistemic situations. The account offers a new perspective on peer disagreement and its implications for intellectual humility.

15. The Problem of Unchosen Commitments
Abstract

Individuals frequently find themselves committed to values, relationships, and projects that they did not explicitly choose. Such commitments raise a difficult question for theories of autonomy: can an agent genuinely govern her life through commitments whose origins lie outside her control? This paper argues that autonomy should not be identified with the capacity to authorize every significant commitment through reflective choice. Such a requirement misunderstands the temporal and social conditions under which practical identities develop. I propose instead that autonomous agency consists partly in the capacity to negotiate, reinterpret, and sometimes repudiate inherited commitments. This account distinguishes the origin of a commitment from the agent's present relationship to it, without assuming that reflective endorsement can always transform an alienating commitment into an autonomous one. I consider cases involving familial obligation, cultural inheritance, and professional identity. The resulting account provides a conception of autonomy that acknowledges the constitutive role of unchosen attachments while preserving the possibility of meaningful self-determination.

16. Time, Experience, and the Present Moment
Abstract

The experience of the present is often described as temporally extended, encompassing a succession of impressions rather than an instantaneous point. Yet philosophical accounts disagree about whether this extension belongs to consciousness itself or reflects the way in which consciousness represents temporal relations. This paper develops an account of temporal experience according to which the present is constituted through the integration of retention, immediate awareness, and anticipation. I argue that these dimensions are not independent components subsequently assembled by the mind but mutually dependent aspects of a single temporal structure. The argument draws upon phenomenological descriptions of listening to music, perceiving movement, and anticipating familiar events. I then examine whether this account commits us to the existence of a metaphysically privileged present. I argue that it does not: the phenomenological structure of temporal experience can be distinguished from the metaphysical structure of time. The distinction helps clarify the relationship between subjective temporality and competing theories of temporal reality.

17. The Epistemology of Moral Perception
Abstract

Moral perception is frequently invoked to explain how individuals recognize ethically significant features of ordinary situations without engaging in explicit moral reasoning. Critics, however, question whether such recognition can provide genuine epistemic justification. This paper argues that the debate has been distorted by an overly restrictive conception of perception as the passive reception of independently identifiable properties. I develop an account of moral perception as an acquired capacity to recognize patterns of significance through experience and habituation. Such perception is neither reducible to spontaneous intuition nor dependent upon the application of explicit moral principles. I argue that it can provide defeasible justification, while remaining vulnerable to distortion by prejudice, emotional investment, and deficient experience. The account explains both the epistemic value of moral expertise and the possibility of systematic moral blindness. I conclude by considering the implications for moral education, arguing that the cultivation of ethical judgment requires more than the transmission of general principles: it requires the development of perceptual sensitivity to morally relevant features of the world.

18. What Is It to Understand Another Person?
Abstract

Understanding another person is often analyzed in terms of attributing beliefs, desires, and intentions on the basis of observable behavior. This paper argues that such an approach captures only one dimension of interpersonal understanding. In many ordinary interactions, understanding involves recognizing how another person's experiences and concerns make sense from within a practical perspective that is not one's own. I develop an account of interpersonal understanding that distinguishes predictive accuracy from interpretive responsiveness. An individual may predict another's behavior correctly while misunderstanding the significance of that behavior for the person who performs it. Conversely, one may understand another person's reasons without being able to predict what she will do. This distinction has implications for debates concerning empathy, interpretation, and the epistemology of other minds. I argue that genuine interpersonal understanding requires a capacity to remain responsive to the possibility that one's interpretation is incomplete. Such responsiveness is not merely an epistemic virtue but a constitutive feature of recognizing another person as an independent subject.

19. The Metaphysics of Dependence
Abstract

Metaphysical dependence relations are commonly invoked to explain why some entities exist or possess their properties in virtue of other entities. Despite the prominence of these relations, their explanatory and ontological roles remain contested. This paper argues that several apparently competing accounts of dependence arise from a failure to distinguish dependence as a relation between entities from dependence as a structure of explanation. I develop a framework that recognizes both dimensions while denying that either can be reduced to the other. In particular, I argue that explanatory asymmetry does not always establish ontological priority, and that ontological dependence need not correspond to a single direction of explanation. The distinction is applied to familiar cases involving properties and particulars, wholes and parts, and facts and their constituents. I show that it permits a more flexible understanding of metaphysical grounding without abandoning the idea that dependence relations are objective. The paper concludes by considering whether this framework can accommodate reciprocal dependence without collapsing into metaphysical circularity.

20. The Possibility of Practical Wisdom
Abstract

Practical wisdom is traditionally understood as the capacity to deliberate and act well across the varied circumstances of human life. Contemporary accounts disagree about whether it is principally a matter of moral knowledge, reliable judgment, or the integration of intellectual and emotional capacities. This paper argues that these accounts overlook the importance of practical orientation: the ability to perceive which features of a situation deserve deliberative priority. I develop a conception of practical wisdom that combines sensitivity to particulars with an understanding of the limits of general principles. Such wisdom does not guarantee correct action, nor does it require comprehensive moral knowledge. Rather, it consists in a cultivated capacity to recognize what is at stake, to identify the relevant considerations, and to respond appropriately to uncertainty. I distinguish practical wisdom from mere experience and from the possession of stable moral dispositions. The account explains why excellent judgment can involve hesitation, revision, and even regret. It also suggests that practical wisdom is fundamentally developmental rather than simply a fixed intellectual achievement.
