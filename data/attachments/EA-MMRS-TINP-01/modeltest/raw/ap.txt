1. The Unity of Opposites and the Limits of Explanation in Heraclitus
This paper reexamines Heraclitus’ account of the unity of opposites, focusing particularly on fragments B10, B50, and B51. Against interpretations that understand the unity of opposites primarily as a metaphysical thesis about the structure of reality, I argue that Heraclitus employs opposition as a methodological principle governing intelligibility. Opposites are not simply identical, nor are they reconciled through the elimination of their differences. Rather, their relationship reveals the inadequacy of ordinary forms of classification when detached from an understanding of the logos. I further suggest that the image of the bow and the lyre provides a model for understanding how differentiated elements can constitute a coherent whole without losing their distinctive properties. This interpretation offers a way of connecting Heraclitus’ epistemology with his account of cosmic order.

2. Plato on the Relationship between Knowledge and Definition in the Meno
The relationship between knowledge and definition is central to the philosophical investigation undertaken in Plato’s Meno. Although the dialogue famously concludes without providing an adequate definition of virtue, its discussion of inquiry, recollection, and true belief raises important questions about the conditions under which a person may properly claim to know something. This paper argues that the dialogue distinguishes between possessing a correct judgment and understanding why that judgment is correct. The discussion of recollection provides a framework for explaining how inquiry can transform initially ungrounded beliefs into knowledge, while the comparison between true opinions and securely fastened statues clarifies the epistemic significance of explanation. I suggest that the failure to define virtue should not be understood as a merely negative conclusion but as an indication of the methodological requirements that any successful account of knowledge must satisfy.

3. Socratic Ignorance and the Authority of the Delphic Oracle in Plato’s Apology
This paper investigates the philosophical significance of the Delphic oracle in Plato’s Apology, with particular attention to Socrates’ interpretation of the god’s declaration that no one is wiser than he is. I argue that Socrates’ subsequent examination of politicians, poets, and craftsmen is not simply an attempt to discover the meaning of an ambiguous pronouncement. It constitutes a distinctive form of philosophical inquiry grounded in the recognition of epistemic limitations. Socrates’ wisdom consists neither in the possession of privileged knowledge nor in the simple acknowledgment of ignorance, but in his refusal to mistake an appearance of understanding for genuine understanding. This interpretation also illuminates the relationship between divine authority and human rationality in the dialogue. The oracle authorizes philosophical examination without exempting its recipient from the obligation to provide rational accounts of his convictions.

4. The Forms and the Problem of Predication in Plato’s Phaedo
The argument concerning the Forms in Plato’s Phaedo is often treated as a straightforward defense of the existence of separate intelligible entities. Yet the dialogue’s discussion of participation raises a more difficult question: how can a particular thing possess a property in virtue of its relation to a Form? This paper examines the explanatory role assigned to the Forms in the final argument for the immortality of the soul. I argue that the Forms function not merely as paradigms resembling sensible particulars but as necessary conditions of predication. The distinction between possessing a property essentially and possessing it derivatively helps explain Socrates’ account of the soul’s relationship to life. On this reading, the final argument develops a general theory of explanation whose implications extend beyond the immediate question of immortality.

5. The Dramatic Function of the Stranger in Plato’s Sophist
The Eleatic Stranger occupies an unusual position within Plato’s dialogues. Although he assumes the role of principal philosophical interlocutor in the Sophist, his authority and methodological commitments remain deliberately open to interpretation. This paper argues that the Stranger’s philosophical identity is integral to the dialogue’s investigation of being and non-being. His Eleatic inheritance initially appears to constrain the possibility of speaking about what is not, yet the subsequent division between different senses of negation allows him to reformulate the problem without abandoning the underlying commitments of Eleatic thought. The Stranger therefore represents neither a simple continuation nor a repudiation of Parmenides. Instead, he demonstrates how inherited philosophical doctrines can be critically transformed through dialectical investigation. This interpretation also sheds light on the dialogue’s broader account of philosophical expertise and the distinction between the sophist and the philosopher.

6. Aristotle on Perception and the Possibility of Perceptual Error
Aristotle’s account of perception in De Anima II.5–6 appears to establish a close connection between perceptual activity and the reception of sensible forms. Yet his subsequent discussion of perceptual error complicates the suggestion that perception is invariably reliable. This paper examines the distinction between perceiving proper sensibles, common sensibles, and incidental sensibles, arguing that it provides the basis for a differentiated account of perceptual accuracy. I suggest that Aristotle does not regard error as an accidental defect superimposed upon an otherwise uniform perceptual faculty. Rather, the possibility of error depends upon the particular objects and conditions involved in perceptual activity. By distinguishing the different ways in which something may be perceived, Aristotle can preserve the authority of perception in its primary operation while acknowledging the limitations of perceptual judgment. The resulting account has significant implications for his epistemology.

7. Potentiality, Actuality, and the Identity of Substance in Aristotle’s Metaphysics
Aristotle’s distinction between potentiality and actuality plays a central role in his account of substance, but the precise relationship between these notions remains controversial. This paper examines the distinction as it appears in Metaphysics Θ and its implications for the account of substantial unity developed in Books Z and H. I argue that actuality should not be understood merely as the temporal completion of a previously existing potentiality. Rather, actuality provides the explanatory principle through which a substance is understood as the particular thing it is. This interpretation helps clarify Aristotle’s claim that actuality is prior to potentiality in several distinct senses. It also offers a response to the objection that hylomorphic substances are insufficiently unified. The paper concludes by considering whether Aristotle’s account of actuality can explain substantial identity without reducing form and matter to independent constituents.

8. Aristotle’s Conception of Practical Wisdom and the Unity of the Virtues
The relationship between practical wisdom and moral virtue is among the most significant features of Aristotle’s ethical theory. In Nicomachean Ethics VI, Aristotle appears to suggest that moral virtue and practical wisdom are mutually dependent, while earlier discussions seem to allow for distinctions among particular virtues. This paper examines whether Aristotle is committed to a strong thesis concerning the unity of the virtues. I argue that practical wisdom provides a form of integration among the virtues without rendering their individual characteristics philosophically insignificant. The practically wise person does not merely possess a collection of independent dispositions but understands how different aspects of human flourishing relate to one another in particular circumstances. This account also clarifies the distinction between natural virtue and virtue in the strict sense. The result is a conception of ethical excellence in which rational integration is indispensable without requiring psychological uniformity.

9. Pleasure and the Good Life in Aristotle’s Nicomachean Ethics
Aristotle’s treatment of pleasure in Nicomachean Ethics VII and X has generated considerable debate concerning the relationship between pleasure and happiness. This paper argues that the two discussions are best understood as addressing complementary rather than competing philosophical questions. Book VII examines pleasure in connection with the restoration of damaged or deficient conditions, whereas Book X develops an account of pleasure as completing an activity. The distinction allows Aristotle to reject certain hedonistic assumptions without denying that pleasure is an essential feature of the good life. I further suggest that the account of pleasure as an activity’s completion provides a more precise explanation of why different pleasures correspond to different forms of human activity. This interpretation has implications for Aristotle’s hierarchy of pleasures and for the privileged status he assigns to contemplation in his account of happiness.

10. The Meaning of Natural Justice in Aristotle’s Politics
Aristotle’s distinction between natural and conventional justice in Nicomachean Ethics V.7 raises important questions about the relationship between moral norms and political institutions. This paper investigates the implications of that distinction for the account of political justice developed in the Politics. I argue that Aristotle’s conception of natural justice does not require the existence of an entirely fixed and universally applicable legal code. Instead, natural justice establishes normative principles whose realization may depend upon particular political circumstances. The apparent variability of political arrangements therefore does not eliminate the possibility of objective standards for evaluating them. This reading also helps explain Aristotle’s discussion of citizenship, constitutions, and the common advantage. By examining the relationship between natural justice and political convention, the paper offers a more nuanced account of the normative foundations of Aristotelian political theory.

11. Epicurus on the Limits of Fear and the Nature of Death
Epicurus’ argument that death is nothing to us is frequently interpreted as a therapeutic strategy designed to eliminate anxiety about personal nonexistence. This paper argues that the argument also depends upon a substantive account of the relationship between sensation and value. Since good and evil are grounded in sensation, and death involves the permanent absence of sensation, death cannot constitute an experienced harm to the individual who dies. I examine the relationship between this argument and Epicurus’ broader account of the limits of pleasure and the removal of disturbance. Particular attention is given to the possibility that the fear of death concerns not death itself but imagined conditions of posthumous deprivation. I suggest that Epicurus’ argument is more successful when understood as a criticism of mistaken evaluative assumptions than as an attempt to demonstrate that mortality is intrinsically insignificant.

12. Stoic Fate and the Possibility of Human Responsibility
The Stoic commitment to universal causal determination appears to threaten the possibility of meaningful human responsibility. If every event is determined by antecedent causes, it may seem that human actions cannot properly be attributed to the agents who perform them. This paper examines the Stoic response to this problem, focusing on Chrysippus’ distinction between external causes and the internal dispositions of rational agents. I argue that the Stoic account does not attempt to preserve responsibility by introducing exceptions to causal necessity. Rather, it identifies responsibility with the distinctive manner in which rational agents respond to the circumstances that confront them. The analogy of the cylinder and the cone illustrates this point by showing how external influences operate through the particular constitution of the affected object. This interpretation clarifies the compatibility of Stoic determinism with moral assessment and ethical self-cultivation.

13. Skepticism and the Problem of Appearing in Sextus Empiricus
Sextus Empiricus’ account of skeptical suspension of judgment raises an important question concerning the epistemological status of appearances. Although the skeptic refrains from making claims about the nature of things, he continues to follow appearances in ordinary life. This paper argues that appearances play a more substantial philosophical role in Sextus’ account than is sometimes recognized. They provide the practical basis for action without requiring the skeptic to endorse any corresponding theory of reality. I examine the relationship between appearances, beliefs, and the fourfold account of skeptical conduct presented in the Outlines of Pyrrhonism. I suggest that the skeptic’s reliance on appearances is neither a covert commitment to empiricism nor an inconsistency in his suspension of judgment. Rather, it constitutes a carefully delimited form of practical orientation that distinguishes skeptical living from dogmatic philosophical commitment.

14. The Stoic Theory of Oikeiosis and the Origins of Justice
The Stoic theory of oikeiosis is commonly presented as an account of the development of social concern from an individual's initial attachment to self-preservation. Yet the transition from self-concern to concern for others remains philosophically difficult. This paper examines the role of rational development in explaining that transition, with particular attention to the relationship between oikeiosis and the Stoic conception of human nature. I argue that the expansion of concern is not adequately described as an accumulation of increasingly extensive preferences. Rather, it involves a transformation in the agent's understanding of the good, through which other rational beings come to be recognized as objects of appropriate concern. The account therefore provides a foundation for Stoic cosmopolitanism that is both psychological and normative. It also helps explain why justice is understood as an expression of rational nature rather than merely as an instrument of social cooperation.

15. Plato’s Philebus and the Measure of the Good
The Philebus offers a complex account of the good life through its investigation of pleasure, knowledge, and the mixture of different elements. This paper focuses on the role of measure and proportion in the dialogue’s final classification of goods. I argue that the mathematical language of limit and unlimitedness is not merely a classificatory device but expresses a substantive claim about the conditions under which a life can be genuinely good. Pleasure, when considered independently, lacks the internal determination necessary to constitute a complete human good. Knowledge and measure, by contrast, contribute to the organization of the mixture through which a flourishing life becomes possible. The paper also examines the relationship between the dialogue’s metaphysical divisions and its ethical conclusions. I suggest that the Philebus presents an integrated account of value in which intelligible order provides the standard for evaluating human experience.

16. Aristotle on Friendship, Self-Love, and the Good Person
Aristotle’s discussion of friendship in Nicomachean Ethics VIII and IX contains an apparent tension between his criticism of self-love and his claim that the good person is appropriately a friend to himself. This paper argues that the tension can be resolved by distinguishing between competitive self-preference and the rational self-love characteristic of virtue. The vicious person seeks private advantage even when doing so damages his character and relationships, whereas the virtuous person desires what is genuinely good for himself. Such a person can therefore wish the same goods for a friend that he wishes for himself. I examine the implications of this distinction for Aristotle’s account of friendship between good people, particularly his claim that a friend is another self. The paper concludes that self-love is not simply an obstacle to interpersonal concern but may constitute one of its necessary ethical foundations.

17. The Soul as Principle of Life in Plato’s Republic
The argument for the immortality of the soul in Republic X has often been interpreted as a direct extension of the psychological theory developed in Republic IV. This paper examines the relationship between the soul’s functional definition and the argument from its characteristic evil. I argue that the argument depends upon a conception of the soul as an organizing principle whose identity cannot be reduced to the particular functions it performs under changing conditions. The soul's characteristic evils, including injustice and ignorance, may damage its proper operation without destroying its underlying nature. This distinction explains why Plato can maintain that the soul is corrupted in an ethical sense while remaining indestructible in a metaphysical sense. The interpretation also clarifies the relationship between the Republic’s account of justice and its concluding discussion of posthumous reward and punishment.

18. Aristotle’s Criticism of Plato’s Theory of Forms Reconsidered
Aristotle’s criticism of the Platonic Forms in Metaphysics A and M is frequently understood as a rejection of the explanatory value of separate universals. However, Aristotle’s objections are more complex than a straightforward denial of the existence of intelligible structures. This paper argues that the central difficulty concerns the relationship between universality and causal explanation. By separating Forms from sensible substances, Plato appears to introduce entities that duplicate rather than explain the properties of the things they are intended to illuminate. Aristotle’s criticisms therefore arise from a broader commitment to understanding explanatory principles as internal to the substances whose nature they explain. I examine the objections concerning participation, duplication, and the argument from the sciences, suggesting that they are connected by a common concern about explanatory relevance. This reading places Aristotle’s critique within his positive account of substance, form, and scientific understanding.

19. The Concept of Time in Aristotle’s Physics
Aristotle’s definition of time as the number of motion with respect to before and after has generated persistent difficulties concerning the relationship between time, motion, and the soul. This paper revisits Physics IV.10–14, arguing that Aristotle’s account should be understood as an analysis of the conditions under which temporal succession becomes measurable. Time is neither identical with motion nor wholly independent of it; rather, it is the aspect of motion through which succession can be numerically articulated. The role of the soul in counting consequently requires careful qualification. I suggest that Aristotle does not make the existence of time depend straightforwardly upon the presence of an actual human observer. Instead, his account distinguishes between the objective possibility of temporal succession and its explicit numerical recognition. This interpretation offers a way to reconcile Aristotle’s realism about motion with the apparently subject-dependent character of temporal measurement.

20. Philosophical Life and the Limits of Human Flourishing in Late Antiquity
The ideal of philosophical life undergoes significant transformations in the later ancient philosophical tradition. This paper examines how late antique Platonists reconcile the pursuit of intellectual perfection with the practical and embodied conditions of human existence. Particular attention is given to the relationship between philosophical contemplation, ethical purification, and the soul’s orientation toward intelligible reality. I argue that the late antique conception of philosophical life cannot be reduced to a simple rejection of embodied existence. Rather, philosophical activity involves the progressive reordering of the soul's capacities, enabling the philosopher to participate more fully in a reality that transcends ordinary experience. The paper also considers the extent to which this ideal preserves earlier Platonic commitments while responding to new metaphysical and religious concerns. It concludes by suggesting that late antique philosophy offers a distinctive account of human flourishing grounded in intellectual transformation.
