Reader’s Report
“ΦΑΙΝΕΤΑΙ ΜΟΙ: Sappho 31 and the Inscription of the Future Reader”
Classical Philology
2026075

This submission reads Sappho's celebrated fr. 31 Voigt metapoetically: κῆνος is the future reader; the second person addressee 'is the poem itself'; the 'I' meanwhile undergoes a transformation from embodied presence to inscribed papyrus. A proposal is then made for the lost last stanza: ἀλλὰ πᾶν τολματόν· ἐπεί σε, Κύπρι, καὶ πένητα καὶ βασιλῆα σώζεις· καὶ πόλιν ὀλίγαν ὑπερέχεις ἄτας· μολπὰ γράμμασι ζώοι. 'But all must be dared. Since you, Kypris, save beggar and king alike, and lift even the small city beyond ruin — let this song survive in script.'

The following considerations form the backbone of the argument:

(1) κῆνος is a demonstrative pronoun expressing distance (hence the 'future' reader).

(2) 'The addressee has a voice (φωνείσας) and a laugh (γελαίσας ἰμέροεν)' but otherwise has 'no face, no body, no name, no history. She is pure audibility — which is to say, she is what a lyric poem is.'

(3) The list of symptoms is a 'systematic shutdown of the performing body', executed by the poem upon its own speaker, so that the voice can pass from one medium to another. The main exhibit here is the memorable χλωροτέρα δὲ ποίας ἔμμι: the poem is paler than grass, because the papyrus is dried up and therefore no longer as green as a living plant.

Catullus and D'Angour are then used to formulate a proposal for the missing last stanza, but neither it, nor the reading it aims to capture, seem to me convincing. The main problem is not, in any case, whether the argument convinces me, but rather that the most obvious objections are nowhere addressed. I summarize at least a few of them below:

Concerning (1), the author of the submission complains that standard readings of the poem 'treat the distal deixis as dramatically proximate — the rival is in the room, seated across from the beloved, close enough to hear her voice and laughter. But the poem's own grammar holds him at deictic arm's length. He is that one, not this one. The distance between distal pointing and intimate proximity (ἐνάντιος, πλάσιον) is the first datum a reading must explain.'
This is not at all hard to explain. 'That' man is close to the girl and distant from the poet's 'I'. So if we are imagining a love triangle the man and the sweetly-laughing girl are close to each other and are being observed from a distance by the first person.

Concerning (2), the girl may be identified just by her smile and voice, but this is arguably how Sappho's poetry works more generally and not something sufficient to turn her into 'a poem', any more than any other girl. Anaktoria is identified just by the sparkle of her face and her lovely step. Dika has delicate hands… and that's it, we know nothing more about her. In short: the memorability of the women who populate Sappho's songs often depends on singling out just one or two elements.

Concerning (3), reference to papyri and the idea of 'saving a song through script' is otherwise unattested in ca 600 BCE… and long after. Some would doubt that Sappho was literate, though I think a cautious argument can be made for her literacy. There is, however, a big difference between that and the kind of reading proposed here, playing as it does on body of the poet/body of work and other tricks reminiscent of, say, the Mnesiepes inscription and other Hellenistic modes of playing with poetic media. When is γράμματα first attested in the use made of it here? When do we get the first reference to papyrus?

There are some other strange inaccuracies, e.g. ''the transmitted πένητα, whatever the following crux proves to be…' Actually, πένητα is already part of the crux, because unmetrical after καί.
In short, although the argument presented here is thought-provoking, it ultimately fails to carry conviction. I do not recommend publication.
