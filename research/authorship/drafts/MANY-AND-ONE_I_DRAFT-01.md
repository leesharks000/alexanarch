# I. What Counts as a Part

## 1

Near the end of the *Sophistical Refutations* Aristotle describes a way of teaching that is not teaching. The men who taught argument for pay, he says, handed their pupils finished speeches to memorise, some rhetorical, some in question form, and supposed that they were educating them — as if someone who promised to teach a science of not hurting one's feet should then not teach shoemaking, nor where shoes might be got, but simply hand out many kinds of shoes. οὐ γὰρ τέχνην ἀλλὰ τὰ ἀπὸ τῆς τέχνης διδόντες παιδεύειν ὑπελάμβανον.[^1] The image is fair and exact. It tells us what a predecessor may have in abundance and still not have contributed: products. A man may own every shoe in the city and know nothing of the art. The teaching was quick, Aristotle says, and without art — ταχεῖα μὲν ἄτεχνος δ᾿ ἦν ἡ διδασκαλία.

He is equally careful, in the same paragraph, about the other case, the one in which a predecessor has contributed. Of everything that is discovered, he says, some things are taken over from others who had worked on them beforehand and are advanced part by part by those who inherit them — παρ᾿ ἑτέρων ληφθέντα πρότερον πεπονημένα κατὰ μέρος; other things are found from the beginning, and these grow at first by a small increase, though a more useful one by far than all the later growth that comes from them. The beginning is greatest, as the saying goes, and therefore hardest: strongest in power, smallest in size, and so most difficult to see. Once it is found, the rest is easier to add.[^2]

Then he gives the case. This is what has happened with rhetoric, and with nearly all the arts. Those who found the principles carried them only a little way; the men now in repute took them over from many — οἷον ἐκ διαδοχῆς, as if by succession — who had advanced them part by part, κατὰ μέρος, and so enlarged them: Tisias after the first beginners, Thrasymachus after Tisias, Theodorus after him, and many have contributed many parts, πολλοὶ πολλὰ συνενηνόχασι μέρη. No wonder the art has bulk.[^3]

So we have, from Aristotle, in one paragraph, a complete account of the two ways a body of knowledge can stand to those who came before it. Either it was handed on in parts and grown by succession, and he can name the links; or nothing of it existed and someone had to find the beginning, which is small, powerful and hard to see. The vocabulary of the first case is the vocabulary of parts: κατὰ μέρος, κατὰ μέρος, μέρη. He has just defined, with a named example, what it is for a predecessor's work to count.

## 2

Against both he sets his own inquiry.

> Ταύτης δὲ τῆς πραγματείας οὐ τὸ μὲν ἦν τὸ δ᾿ οὐκ ἦν προεξειργασμένον, ἀλλ᾿ οὐδὲν παντελῶς ὑπῆρχεν.[^4]

Of this study it was not the case that part had been worked out beforehand and part had not; nothing at all existed. The form of the sentence should be noticed before its content. He does not only say that nothing existed. He first names the intermediate case — some of it, τὸ μέν, worked out beforehand; some of it, τὸ δέ, not — and denies that case explicitly, and then states the total absence, παντελῶς. The disjunct he goes out of his way to exclude is the disjunct his own theory of the arts had just made the normal one.

And he says, immediately, what he means by it. The comparison is with the paid teachers of eristic, whose instruction was like Gorgias's practice: they gave speeches to be learned by heart. Καὶ περὶ μὲν τῶν ῥητορικῶν ὑπῆρχε πολλὰ καὶ παλαιὰ τὰ λεγόμενα, περὶ δὲ τοῦ συλλογίζεσθαι παντελῶς οὐδὲν εἴχομεν πρότερον ἄλλο λέγειν, ἀλλ᾿ ἢ τριβῇ ζητοῦντες πολὺν χρόνον ἐπονοῦμεν.[^5] About rhetoric there existed many old things said; about syllogizing we had nothing else at all to say before, but laboured a long time searching by trial. Read where it stands, between the shoemaker and the rhetoricians, this is not a boast. It is a claim about a category, made by the man who has just defined the category and supplied its criterion. What counts as prior existence is λεγόμενα — things said, old and many. Rhetoric had them. Syllogizing had none.

A reader has every reason to accept this. It is precise, it is marked, and it is generous: Aristotle is not saying that no one reasoned before him. The shoemaker's customers walked. He is saying that no one had said the art.

## 3

There is one more thing on the page, and it should be read before anything from outside is brought to it. Two sentences before the denial, recalling why the inquiry was extended from the questioner's side to the answerer's, Aristotle names his predecessor, and it is Socrates.

> Σωκράτης ἠρώτα, ἀλλ᾿ οὐκ ἀπεκρίνετο· ὡμολόγει γὰρ οὐκ εἰδέναι.[^6]

Socrates asked questions but did not answer; for he confessed he did not know. The practice is credited, by name, as the reason for a whole side of the enterprise — and then nothing is said to have existed. This is the conventional reading at its strongest, and it is Aristotle's own. Socrates questioned; he had no art of it; the products are not the art. The shoemaker's image was made for exactly this case, and it fits.

Everything to this point is one paragraph of Aristotle, read in its own order. The reader is now entitled to be satisfied.

## 4

Now open the *Phaedrus*.

At 265d Socrates, looking back over the two speeches on love, asks whether the power of the two procedures they used might be grasped by art — εἰ αὐτοῖν τὴν δύναμιν τέχνῃ λαβεῖν δύναιτό τις. He then states the two procedures as two εἴδη of one thing. The first: to see together things scattered in many places and bring them under one form, so that by defining each one makes clear what one intends to teach. The second: to be able to cut again by forms, at the joints, as they naturally are, καὶ μὴ ἐπιχειρεῖν καταγνύναι μέρος μηδέν — and not to try to break any part, in the manner of a bad carver.[^7] It is a method, it has two named operations, it has a rule, and it has a failure mode. Then:

> τούτων δὴ ἔγωγε αὐτός τε ἐραστής, ὦ Φαῖδρε, τῶν διαιρέσεων καὶ συναγωγῶν, ἵνα οἷός τε ὦ λέγειν τε καὶ φρονεῖν … καὶ μέντοι καὶ τοὺς δυναμένους αὐτὸ δρᾶν … καλῶ δὲ οὖν μέχρι τοῦδε διαλεκτικούς.[^8]

Of these I am a lover, Phaedrus, the divisions and collections, in order to be able to speak and to think; and those who can do it I have so far called dialecticians. The procedure is named, twice; it is held for a purpose; it is general enough to be what makes speech and thought possible; and its practitioners have a name. It is spoken by Socrates.

This is not a pile of shoes. By Aristotle's own criterion in the *Sophistical Refutations* — the criterion is λεγόμενα, things said about the subject — there are things said here. And the *Phaedrus* is not alone in saying them. The *Sophist* asks whether dividing by kinds, and not taking the same form for a different one nor a different one for the same, belongs to dialectical knowledge — τὸ κατὰ γένη διαιρεῖσθαι … τῆς διαλεκτικῆς ἐπιστήμης — having just said that the task needs knowledge, and perhaps the greatest.[^9] The *Philebus* calls it the way than which none is fairer, of which Socrates has always been a lover, and says that everything belonging to art that was ever discovered has come to light through it: πάντα γὰρ ὅσα τέχνης ἐχόμενα ἀνηυρέθη πώποτε διὰ ταύτης φανερὰ γέγονε.[^10] Whatever else these passages are, they are λεγόμενα, and by the internal chronology every reader accepts, they are old.

## 5

Stay on the *Phaedrus* page. Having named the dialecticians, Socrates turns to the other art of speeches, ἡ λόγων τέχνη, the one Thrasymachus and the rest use, and asks what is left over for rhetoric once division and collection have been taken out of it. Phaedrus answers that there is a great deal — τά γ᾿ ἐν τοῖς βιβλίοις τοῖς περὶ λόγων τέχνης γεγραμμένοις, the things in the books written about the art of speaking.[^11] And Socrates catalogues them: the proem, narrative, testimony, proofs, probabilities; confirmation and further confirmation, from the best word-craftsman of Byzantium — Phaedrus supplies the name, Theodorus; refutation and counter-refutation; Evenus of Paros, who first discovered covert allusion; Tisias and Gorgias, whom we shall let sleep, who saw that probabilities are to be honoured above truths; Prodicus, who laughed and said that he alone had found what the art of speeches requires; Hippias; Polus; Protagoras; and the strength of the Chalcedonian, Thrasymachus again, master of the pathetic speech on old age and poverty.[^12]

Set the two pages side by side. Aristotle's paragraph contrasts an art with a written tradition of λεγόμενα about λόγοι, and names the tradition's links: Tisias, Thrasymachus, Theodorus, with Gorgias as the type of the teacher who hands out products. The *Phaedrus* page contrasts an art with the books written about λόγοι, and names the same four men, on the same page, in the same role — writers of handbooks who possess the products and not the art, and who make priority claims about it. The four names are the canonical names of the rhetorical tradition; Aristotle had them from the *Synagoge Technon* and needed no dialogue to supply them.[^13] That is the null, and it is stated. But the reader who has both pages open now knows something the paragraph alone did not tell him. The text that had already made Aristotle's exact contrast — real art against the rhetoricians' λεγόμενα — had already said what the real art was. It was division and collection. And the man who made the contrast there was Socrates.

## 6

Now the *Prior Analytics*.

At I.31 Aristotle has just finished laying out the method for finding the premises of a syllogism, and refers the reader elsewhere for the detail: Καθόλου μὲν οὖν, ὃν δεῖ τρόπον τὰς προτάσεις ἐκλέγειν, εἴρηται σχεδόν· δι᾿ ἀκριβείας δὲ διεληλύθαμεν ἐν τῇ πραγματείᾳ τῇ περὶ τὴν διαλεκτικήν. In general the way to select premises has more or less been said; we have gone through it in detail in the inquiry concerning dialectic. The next sentence:

> Ὅτι δ᾿ ἡ διὰ τῶν γενῶν διαίρεσις μικρόν τι μόριόν ἐστι τῆς εἰρημένης μεθόδου, ῥᾴδιον ἰδεῖν· ἔστι γὰρ ἡ διαίρεσις οἷον ἀσθενὴς συλλογισμός.[^14]

That division through the genera is some small part of the method just described is easy to see; for division is a kind of weak syllogism. It begs what it should prove, and always syllogizes something higher than it needs. And this, he says, had escaped all who used it — Πρῶτον δ᾿ αὐτὸ τοῦτο ἐλελήθει τοὺς χρωμένους αὐτῇ πάντας — who tried to persuade people that demonstration of substance and of what a thing is was possible; so that they neither understood what could be syllogized by dividing, οὔτε ὅ τι ἐνδέχεται συλλογίσασθαι διαιρούμενοι ξυνίεσαν, nor that it could be done in the way we have described.[^15]

Three things are said here and each should be held separately. Division is classified: it is a part, μόριον, of the method — Aristotle's own word for the unit in which predecessors contribute. The method is identified: it is the one worked out in detail in the dialectical inquiry, the inquiry whose epilogue we have just read. And the practitioners are characterised: all of them missed what the operation was. The last of these is the conventional reading's best friend, and Aristotle supplies it. Plato may have performed division without possessing it as syllogistic. The classification is retrospective; the dividers did not know what they had.

But notice what this does and does not do. It explains why Aristotle files division as a part of his method. It does not touch the sentence that says no part of the method was worked out before. The subsumption is his; the denial is also his; and the subsumption is the reason the denial now has an object.

## 7

The reader has four texts and has, I think, already asked the question. It should be stated once, in the narrowest form the texts allow.

Division is a part of the method (*APr.* I.31). No part of the method had been worked out beforehand (*SE* 34). Division had been worked out beforehand, as a named art with a rule and practitioners, in the Platonic corpus (*Phdr.* 265d–266c, *Soph.* 253c–e, *Phlb.* 16c). Each sentence is Aristotle's or Plato's. They cannot all be held together with the received chronology unless one of four words moves: *part*, *worked out*, *the method*, or *before*.

That is the whole of the arithmetic, and it is worth seeing that every reading the tradition has offered is a decision about which word to move.[^16] The first reading moves *worked out*: people reasoned, Plato divided, but no one had the art — the shoemaker. The second moves *the method*: ταύτης τῆς πραγματείας is the dialectical programme of the *Topics*, not syllogizing as such. The third moves *part*: division only became a part of the method when Aristotle discovered what it was; before that it was a Platonic thing with no syllogistic character — ξυνίεσαν. The fourth moves *before*, but for Aristotle only: the *Topics* and its epilogue precede the *Analytics*, and the man who denied any prior part had not yet seen that division was one. A fifth reading, which I set aside as beneath the others though not defeated by them, moves nothing and charges Aristotle with an inventor's overstatement.

Readings one through four are strong, and they are strongest together: in the early dialectical treatise Aristotle claims novelty for a systematic art; predecessors had reasoned and Plato had division, but none possessed a theory of syllogizing; later, having the *Analytics*, he classifies division retrospectively while saying its users never understood it. No contradiction; nothing unusual about the provenance. This is the account any reader of these passages carries, and the section's task is not to deny it but to make it draw a line it has never been made to draw.

## 8

The line runs through πραγματεία, and the second reading depends on where it runs. The word has three senses in this neighbourhood and the epilogue uses more than one. It can mean the written treatment — this work, these books; on that sense the priority claim is true and worthless, and no one defends it. It can mean the inquiry as a project, the thing recalled at the head of the epilogue — δύναμίν τινα συλλογιστικὴν περὶ τοῦ προβληθέντος ἐκ τῶν ὑπαρχόντων ὡς ἐνδοξοτάτων, a syllogistic capacity for any problem from the most reputable premises, the work of dialectic in itself and of testing.[^17] And it can mean the discipline as such, the sense the close requires when it sets ἡ μέθοδος against τὰς ἄλλας πραγματείας τὰς ἐκ παραδόσεως ηὐξημένας, the other disciplines grown by tradition, of which rhetoric is the instance.[^18]

The second reading takes the middle sense and holds it tight: the denial concerns the dialectical programme, not syllogizing. Three observations on the page make it hard to hold.

The epilogue does not distinguish μέθοδος from πραγματεία. It calls the inquiry ταύτην τὴν πραγματείαν and, in the closing sentence, ἡ μέθοδος, and speaks of what has been left out τῆς μεθόδου; earlier, τῆς αὐτῆς μεθόδου τῶν λόγων.[^19] The two words name one thing. Whatever *Prior Analytics* I.31 means by τῆς εἰρημένης μεθόδου, the shift of noun is not itself a shift of referent.

The programme names itself as syllogistic. What was proposed was δύναμίν τινα συλλογιστικήν. The clause that explains the denial (γάρ) does not contrast rhetoric with dialectic; it contrasts rhetoric with τὸ συλλογίζεσθαι. If the restriction to dialectic were meant, the natural word was to hand and was not used.

And *Prior Analytics* I.31 sends the reader to the dialectical pragmateia in the sentence before it files division. The method of which division is a small part is the method whose detailed treatment is ἐν τῇ πραγματείᾳ τῇ περὶ τὴν διαλεκτικήν. Narrowing the *SE* denial to the dialectical inquiry therefore does not insulate it. Division is a part of that inquiry's method, on Aristotle's own cross-reference, and that inquiry's epilogue says no part of it existed. The second reading, held tightly, collapses into the third.

## 9

The third reading is Aristotle's own, and it is the one that survives. The dividers did not know what they had. Plato possessed division as a τέχνη — the *Phaedrus* uses the word, the *Sophist* says ἐπιστήμη — but not as a part of syllogistic, because there was no syllogistic for it to be a part of. What was worked out beforehand was worked out under another description. That is a real boundary and it is coherent: no part of the method was worked out *as* the method.

Two costs must be charged to it, and both are on Aristotle's page.

The first is the sentence itself. He does not say that nothing had been worked out under his description. He names the case in which part had been worked out and part had not, and denies it. A predecessor who possessed, as a stated art, one operation which the later inquiry would classify as its part is precisely τὸ μὲν ἦν προεξειργασμένον. To read the sentence intensionally is to read into it a restriction its own form excludes; and the restrictions that are marked — the shoemaker, Gorgias, the paid teachers — all mark the difference between having the art and having its products. They do not mark the difference between having a part of the art and having it under the right name.

The second is the model case. Tisias contributed a part of rhetoric. He did not contribute it under Aristotle's description of rhetoric; there was none. The criterion Aristotle applies to the succession he holds up as his example of inherited development counts a contribution as a part of the art regardless of the description under which its contributor worked. Applied to division, that criterion counts. The third reading must therefore say why the standard that makes Tisias a link in a διαδοχή does not make the *Phaedrus* one — and it must say it in the presence of a text that puts Tisias and division on the same page, in opposite columns, one as the holder of products and the other as the art.

## 10

This is the place to say why the literal reading is the charitable one, because the instinct runs the other way. The four readings each supply something on Aristotle's behalf: a restriction of scope, a shift in the sense of *part*, a change of mind between treatises. The fifth charges him with carelessness. The literal reading alone supplies nothing and charges nothing. It is the only reading on which the man who has just defined the category of prior partial contribution, given it a named example, and stated its criterion, and who elsewhere classifies division as a part of his method, says exactly what he means and is right. It is uniquely charitable to the author and uniquely expensive to the chronology. Every treatment of these lines has paid the cost with Aristotle's precision rather than with the chronology, and the choice has never once been made in the open.[^20] It is not an unreasonable choice. The chronology carries a very large prior. But a prior is not a reading, and the reading has to be made on the page.

## 11

Two more Platonic texts belong here, not as evidence of anything but as λεγόμενα the predecessor corpus contains about the very terms in which Aristotle files and denies.

The *Statesman*, warning the young Socrates against a bad cut, says: μὴ σμικρὸν μόριον ἓν πρὸς μεγάλα καὶ πολλὰ ἀφαιρῶμεν, μηδὲ εἴδους χωρίς· ἀλλὰ τὸ μέρος ἅμα εἶδος ἐχέτω — let us not cut off one small part against many large ones, nor a part apart from a form; let the part carry a form with it.[^21] And a page later, formally: εἶδος μὲν ὅταν ᾖ του, καὶ μέρος αὐτὸ ἀναγκαῖον εἶναι τοῦ πράγματος ὅτουπερ ἂν εἶδος λέγηται· μέρος δὲ εἶδος οὐδεμία ἀνάγκη — a form of something is necessarily a part of it; a part is not necessarily a form.[^22] The corpus that is said to have contributed no part contains a theory of what it is for something to be a part, stated for the case of division, in the phrase — σμικρὸν μόριον — that Aristotle uses to file division as a part.

The *Philebus* says of the method that the ancients, better than we and living nearer the gods, handed it down, ταύτην φήμην παρέδοσαν, and that it is the route by which every discovery belonging to art has ever been made.[^23] Aristotle's law of discoveries, τῶν εὑρισκομένων ἁπάντων, distinguishes what grows ἐκ παραδόσεως from what is found ἐξ ὑπαρχῆς, and makes his own inquiry the exception. The exception is stated over a text that calls division the handed-down thing by which arts are found.

None of this is a confession and I do not read it as one. It is the shape of the problem. The predecessor's texts speak about parts, about handing down, about the discovery of arts, about the art of speaking against the handbooks of Tisias and Theodorus, and about division as the art that does what the handbooks cannot; and the successor's text, using the same words and the same names, says that of this art no part was handed down.

## 12

Aristotle's account of how arts grow ends with a request. If the method seems to you, on inspection, to stand well beside the other disciplines that grew by tradition, given that it began from nothing, then it is for all of you who have heard it to pardon what was left out and to be grateful for what was found.[^24] The request is fair and the gratitude is owed. The question this section leaves is not whether he deserved it.

It is this. Can a predecessor possess a general, named, repeatable art, which the successor calls a part of his method, while the successor truthfully says that no part of his method had been worked out beforehand? The received account has an answer available. It has never been made to state it, because it has never had to: the chronology stated it in advance. The answer must show the boundary — must say what distinguishes *having a part of the method* from *having part of it worked out* — and must show it in a way that lets Tisias through and stops the *Phaedrus*. Until it does, the question stands open on Aristotle's own taxonomy, and it stands open before any hypothesis about who wrote what has been introduced.

Is division a shoe, or is it shoemaking? Aristotle drew the distinction. He gave the criterion. He named the case that would satisfy the middle term and denied it. The one thing he did not do was apply the distinction to the text that had already drawn it.

The two passages were read together from the beginning. Philoponus, glossing I.31, writes that Aristotle wishes to celebrate the method he handed down, for no one before us knew this, but all used the method of division.[^25] The sixth century joined the classification to the priority claim without hesitation. What the commentators made of the join is the next section's business.
