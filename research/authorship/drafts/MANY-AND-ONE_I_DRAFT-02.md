# I. Socrates Asked, But Did Not Answer

## 1

Near the end of the *Sophistical Refutations*, a few lines before Aristotle says what did and did not exist before his inquiry, he names Socrates.

He is recalling what the inquiry set out to do: to find a certain syllogistic capacity, δύναμίν τινα συλλογιστικήν, for any problem put forward, from the most reputable premises available. That, he says, is the work of dialectic in itself, and of testing. But because sophistic lives next door, the inquiry had to be furnished beyond its stated task. It was not enough to be able to take an account; one had also to be able to give one — to sustain a thesis while answering, ὅπως λόγον ὑπέχοντες φυλάξομεν τὴν θέσιν. And he gives the reason.

> Τὴν δ᾿ αἰτίαν εἰρήκαμεν τούτου, ἐπεὶ καὶ διὰ τοῦτο Σωκράτης ἠρώτα, ἀλλ᾿ οὐκ ἀπεκρίνετο· ὡμολόγει γὰρ οὐκ εἰδέναι.[^1]

We have said the cause of this; for this is also why Socrates asked but did not answer: he acknowledged that he did not know.

The remark explains a technical decision. Aristotle does not accuse Socrates of anything. He assigns him a side. There are two sides to the activity the inquiry describes, the asking and the answering, and one of them is already occupied. Socrates asked. The present inquiry, Aristotle says, has had to take up the other.

It is worth pausing on the name, because until this sentence it has meant nothing in particular. Twice earlier in the same treatise Socrates is the man in an example — if he is other than Socrates, and Socrates is a man, then he is other than a man — the way Coriscus is the man in an example, the way, in the *Categories*, Socrates is well and Socrates is ill and there is no Socrates at all.[^2] The name has been Aristotle's word for anyone. Here, for one sentence, it is someone. He asked. He did not answer. He said he did not know.

## 2

In the *Phaedrus*, the man who did not know assigns himself a side of his own.

He is looking back over the two speeches on love, and asks whether the power the two of them displayed could be taken hold of by art — εἰ αὐτοῖν τὴν δύναμιν τέχνῃ λαβεῖν δύναιτό τις.[^3] The answer he gives is the procedure. There are two forms of it. One brings what is scattered in many places under a single look, so that by defining, one makes clear what one means to teach. The other cuts again by forms, at the joints, as the thing is naturally jointed, and does not try to break any part — καὶ μὴ ἐπιχειρεῖν καταγνύναι μέρος μηδέν — in the manner of a bad carver. Then:

> τούτων δὴ ἔγωγε αὐτός τε ἐραστής, ὦ Φαῖδρε, τῶν διαιρέσεων καὶ συναγωγῶν, ἵνα οἷός τε ὦ λέγειν τε καὶ φρονεῖν … καλῶ δὲ οὖν μέχρι τοῦδε διαλεκτικούς.[^4]

Of these he is a lover, the divisions and the collections, in order to be able to speak and to think; and whoever he judges able to see toward one and toward many as things are naturally disposed, he follows behind, in his footsteps, as if he were a god. Those who can do it he has so far called dialecticians.

So the man who asked, and did not answer, and said he did not know, possesses the thing that makes speaking and thinking possible. He does not say he invented it. He says he loves it, and follows anyone who has it, and has a name for them. He does not say it is knowledge. He asks whether it can be held by art, and then holds it.

Nothing here needs resolving. The two remarks are made in different books by different hands. But the reader should notice that the same name has now been given two functions, and that they do not obviously fit: he does not know; he has what one thinks with.

## 3

Aristotle will later say what that thing is.

In the thirty-first chapter of the first book of the *Prior Analytics*, having laid out how the premises of a syllogism are to be found, and having sent the reader for the detailed treatment to the inquiry concerning dialectic — ἐν τῇ πραγματείᾳ τῇ περὶ τὴν διαλεκτικήν — he turns to division.

> Ὅτι δ᾿ ἡ διὰ τῶν γενῶν διαίρεσις μικρόν τι μόριόν ἐστι τῆς εἰρημένης μεθόδου, ῥᾴδιον ἰδεῖν· ἔστι γὰρ ἡ διαίρεσις οἷον ἀσθενὴς συλλογισμός· ὃ μὲν γὰρ δεῖ δεῖξαι αἰτεῖται, συλλογίζεται δ᾿ ἀεί τι τῶν ἄνωθεν.[^5]

That division through the genera is some small part of the method just described is easy to see; for division is a kind of weak syllogism. It begs what it should show, and always syllogizes something higher up than it needs. And then, of those who used it:

> Πρῶτον δ᾿ αὐτὸ τοῦτο ἐλελήθει τοὺς χρωμένους αὐτῇ πάντας … ὥστ᾿ οὔτε ὅ τι ἐνδέχεται συλλογίσασθαι διαιρούμενοι ξυνίεσαν, οὔτε ὅτι οὕτως ἐνεδέχετο ὥσπερ εἰρήκαμεν.[^6]

This very thing had escaped all who used it. They tried to persuade people that demonstration of substance and of what a thing is was possible; so that they neither understood what could be syllogized by dividing, nor that it could be done in the way we have described.

Read the three texts in the order they have now been given. In the earlier one, a man who says he does not know performs an operation and calls it the art of speaking and thinking. In the later one, another man says what the operation is — a part, a small part, of his method; a weak syllogism — and says that everyone who performed it did not know what they were doing when they did it.

The earlier text says: he did not know. The later text says: they did not understand. Between the two, the operation has been performed, loved, named, followed, classified, and filed as a part. The one thing no one in either text claims to have done is discover it.

## 4

Then Aristotle says that nothing existed.

The sentence comes at the end of the paragraph that began with Socrates, and it is the paragraph's conclusion. He has described how discoveries grow: things taken over from others who worked on them beforehand advance part by part, κατὰ μέρος, in the hands of those who inherit them; things found from the beginning grow by a small first increase, which is worth more than all the later growth put together, because the beginning is the greatest thing, and the hardest to see, being smallest in size and strongest in power.[^7] He has given the case: rhetoric, and nearly all the arts, grew this way, by succession — Tisias after the first ones, Thrasymachus after Tisias, Theodorus after him, and many contributed many parts. And then:

> Ταύτης δὲ τῆς πραγματείας οὐ τὸ μὲν ἦν τὸ δ᾿ οὐκ ἦν προεξειργασμένον, ἀλλ᾿ οὐδὲν παντελῶς ὑπῆρχεν.[^8]

Of this inquiry it was not that part had been worked out beforehand and part had not. Nothing whatsoever existed.

He does not only deny that there was anything. He names the middle case — some of it worked out, τὸ μέν, some of it not, τὸ δέ — the case his own account of the arts had just made the ordinary one, and denies it first, and then denies everything. And in the next breath he says what he means by *existed*. About rhetoric, πολλὰ καὶ παλαιὰ τὰ λεγόμενα, many old things had been said; about syllogizing, nothing at all — we had nothing else to say, and laboured a long time searching by trial.[^9]

The reader who has come here in the order set out above is not in a position to say "contradiction." That word belongs to someone who has an alternative in hand, and no alternative has been offered. What the reader is in a position to say is stranger and simpler. I have just watched it exist. I was shown where. I was told, by the man who now says nothing existed, what it was and which part of his method it is. And I was told, by the same man, who performed it and that they did not understand what they had.

The sentence does not read as false. It reads as if it were being said by someone who knew exactly where everything was.

## 5

Aristotle seems to anticipate this. The lines that follow the denial supply what looks like the answer.

The men who taught argument for pay — their instruction resembled Gorgias's practice — handed their pupils speeches to learn by heart, some rhetorical and some in the form of questions, the arguments into which they expected the other side's arguments most often to fall. So the teaching was quick, and without art. οὐ γὰρ τέχνην ἀλλὰ τὰ ἀπὸ τῆς τέχνης διδόντες παιδεύειν ὑπελάμβανον — for they supposed they were educating by handing over not the art but the things from the art, as if a man who professed to teach a science of not hurting one's feet should teach no shoemaking, nor where shoes might be got, but should hand out many kinds of shoes of every sort. He has helped with the need; he has not handed over the art.[^10]

There it is, and it is the reader's own thought, made by Aristotle before the reader can make it. A predecessor may have products and not the art. Socrates asked questions. Question-form arguments, ἐρωτητικοὶ λόγοι, were among the things handed out to be memorised. The dialogues are full of them. The distinction holds and the floor is level again.

For a moment.

Then go back to the *Phaedrus*. The passage did not present division as a trick that came off. It asked, before anything else, whether the δύναμις of the procedure could be taken hold of τέχνῃ; it gave the procedure two named operations, a rule, a natural structure to follow, and a way of going wrong. The *Sophist* says that dividing by kinds — τὸ κατὰ γένη διαιρεῖσθαι — and not taking the same form for another, nor another for the same, belongs to dialectical ἐπιστήμη, and has just said the task needs knowledge, perhaps the greatest.[^11] The *Philebus* calls the route than which none is fairer the one Socrates has always loved, and says that everything belonging to art that was ever discovered, πάντα ὅσα τέχνης ἐχόμενα ἀνηυρέθη πώποτε, came to light by it.[^12]

The distinction between shoes and shoemaking was offered to close the gap. Held against the texts, it reopens it. The predecessor corpus does not say *shoes*. It says τέχνη. It says ἐπιστήμη. It says: this is how arts are found.

And the man who says so, in all three dialogues, is the one Aristotle says did not answer, because he did not know.

## 6

One more thing is on Aristotle's page and it should be seen before the section ends. He has told us what genuine inheritance looks like. It is Tisias, Thrasymachus, Theodorus: named men, in order, each contributing parts, until the art has bulk. That is his own model of an inquiry with predecessors, set out so that the reader will know his inquiry is not one.

Now stay on the page of the *Phaedrus* where the dialecticians were named. Having named them, Socrates turns to the other art of speeches, ἡ λόγων τέχνη, the one Thrasymachus and the rest use, and asks what is left over to rhetoric once division and collection are taken out of it. A great deal, Phaedrus says — τά γ᾿ ἐν τοῖς βιβλίοις τοῖς περὶ λόγων τέχνης γεγραμμένοις, the things in the books written on the art of speaking. And Socrates goes through them: the proem, the narrative, the proofs; confirmation and further confirmation, from the best word-craftsman of Byzantium, whom Phaedrus names as Theodorus; Evenus of Paros, who first discovered covert allusion; Tisias and Gorgias, whom we shall let sleep, who saw that probabilities are to be honoured above truths; Prodicus, who laughed, and said that he alone had found what the art of speeches requires; and the strength of the Chalcedonian, Thrasymachus, master of the speech that draws tears for old age and poverty.[^13]

The names Aristotle gives for succession by parts, and the name he gives for the teacher who hands out products, are the names on this page. They stand here in the same role: writers of handbooks, holders of what is written, makers of claims to have found the art first. They are the canonical names of that tradition, and Aristotle had them from his own collection of the handbooks; he did not need a dialogue to supply them.[^14] But the reader now has both pages, and on this one the contrast Aristotle draws — an art, against many old written things about speeches — has already been drawn, with the same men on the same side of it. And on this page the other side of the contrast has a name. It is division and collection. The speaker is Socrates.

The corpus that is said to have contributed no part also contains an account of what a part is. The *Statesman*, correcting a bad cut, warns against taking off one small part, σμικρὸν μόριον, against many large ones, apart from a form — let the part carry a form with it — and then states, as a rule the young Socrates is to keep, that a form of something is of necessity a part of it, but a part is not of necessity a form.[^15] Aristotle files division as μικρόν τι μόριον. And the *Philebus* says of the route by which arts are discovered that the ancients, living nearer the gods, handed it down, παρέδοσαν — the word Aristotle uses, ἐκ παραδόσεως, for the way every inquiry but his own has grown.[^16]

## 7

Aristotle has now told us who asked and who did not answer; who said he did not know; what the method was and which part of it division is; that those who used it did not understand it; what inheritance looks like and who its links were; and what, in his own case, was found from nothing. He has told us these things in his own words, on his own pages, and the pages of the corpus he did not name as a predecessor tell the rest: who loved the operation, who followed it, who called it art and knowledge and the way arts are found, who set it against the handbooks of Tisias and Theodorus, who said what a part is, who said the route was handed down.

The operation is distributed across the names in front of us. One asks. One holds the art and says he does not know. One says what the art is and that its holders did not know it. One says nothing existed. Nothing on these pages tells us to identify those names with one another. Nothing yet permits it.

But neither do the pages permit the names to answer the question for us. The question is not who wrote what. It is why the account of a single operation — performed, named, classified, denied — keeps being given by someone who, each time, knows where the other parts are.
